Rationale and what changed

MATH-1107 is a single semester, and it stops where much of the mathematics of computing begins: derivatives of functions of many variables, optimisation with and without constraints, integrals over regions, the frequency view of signals, and models of systems that change over time. This second calculus course was added after review of the first draft, on the condition that its content be chosen for computing and not taken from the second half of a traditional engineering calculus sequence. It has five strands, each kept because a named later course uses it: multivariable differentiation up to reverse-mode automatic differentiation; optimisation; multiple integrals and continuous densities; complex numbers and Fourier methods; and difference and differential equations as models of computing systems. Left out on purpose, because no later course in the programme uses them: line and surface integrals and the theorems of Green, Stokes and Gauss; long lists of integration techniques (trigonometric substitution, partial fractions, reduction formulas); Laplace transforms; the catalogue of solution methods for second-order and higher equations; and complex analysis beyond Euler's formula. The Mathematics department teaches it as a service course, so examples, assignments and examination questions come from machine learning, networks, embedded systems and algorithms. Each method is worked by hand on a small case, then written as a short NumPy function, then replaced by the library call (numpy.fft, scipy.optimize) or, for derivatives, by the automatic-differentiation library that later AI courses use.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Compute the gradient and Jacobian of a small function, and carry out the forward and backward passes on a computational graph of up to six nodes, writing every local derivative.
  • Write the Hessian of a two-variable function and classify its critical points; take two gradient-descent steps and one Newton step by hand, and say whether a given step size makes gradient descent on a quadratic diverge.
  • Solve a two-variable problem with one equality constraint by a Lagrange multiplier, and a two-variable linear program by drawing the feasible region.
  • Evaluate a double integral over a rectangle, a triangle or (in polar coordinates) a disc, and use it to find the probability of a region for a given joint density.
  • Convert a complex number between Cartesian and polar form, compute a four-point DFT by hand, and say whether a given sampling rate aliases a given frequency; solve a first-order linear recurrence and take three Euler steps of a differential equation.

Stretch goals

  • Write a small reverse-mode automatic-differentiation engine in Python (a value class with overloaded operators), train a two-layer network with it, and check every gradient by finite differences.
  • Show that gradient descent on a convex quadratic converges at a rate set by the condition number, and derive the dual of a small linear program and read the dual variables as prices.
  • Multiply large integers with the FFT, find the size at which it beats schoolbook multiplication, and explain the round-off that limits floating-point FFT multiplication.

Weekly plan — lab

None. This is a theory-only course with no paired lab. The four NumPy assignments described under Assessment take its place and are done outside class.

Assessment

Theory: mid-term and final (80). The mid-term covers weeks 1–7. A formula sheet with standard integrals and Fourier pairs should be provided, so that marks go to set-up and interpretation. At least half the marks are for application and analysis; recall is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • A computational graph is given, such as logistic regression on one example or a network of two neurons: complete the forward and backward passes, or find the wrong local derivative in a worked backward pass.
  • A loss of two parameters is given: find its gradient and Hessian, decide whether it is convex, take one gradient step and one Newton step, and say what a step twice as large would do.
  • An allocation problem in words (bandwidth among users, jobs across servers, a production plan): formulate it as a linear program or a Lagrange problem, solve the two-variable case, and say what the multiplier or the binding constraint means.
  • A joint density of two response times is given: find the normalising constant, a probability such as P(X < Y), and an expectation.
  • A signal is described or plotted: sketch its spectrum, give the lowest sampling rate that avoids aliasing, give the frequency that appears when it is sampled too slowly, or compute a short convolution by hand.
  • A recurrence or a differential-equation model of a queue, a cache or an epidemic is given: solve it or take Euler steps, and decide whether the model, or its simulation, is stable.
  • Printed NumPy or SciPy output is given (an FFT magnitude plot, error against iteration for three step sizes, a linprog result): say what it shows.

Continuous assessment (20). Four 15-minute quizzes, best three counted (10), Level 0. Four computational assignments in Python with NumPy and Matplotlib (2.5 each), Level 1, handed in as Jupyter notebooks, as in MATH-1209:

  1. Weeks 1–3: reverse-mode automatic differentiation for a small expression graph (addition, multiplication, exponential, logarithm), checked against finite differences and used to compute the gradient of a logistic-regression loss.
  2. Weeks 4–6: gradient descent, momentum and Newton's method on an elongated quadratic and on a logistic-regression loss, with the paths drawn on a contour plot; a three-variable linear program solved with scipy.optimize.linprog, and a two-variable version checked graphically.
  3. Weeks 7–11: a probability for a joint density computed by hand and checked by Monte Carlo; aliasing shown by sampling one signal at two rates; a supplied audio or sensor recording analysed with numpy.fft; a recursive FFT written and timed against the direct DFT as n grows.
  4. Weeks 12–14: an image blurred and edge-detected by 2-D convolution written as loops and then with scipy.signal.convolve2d; an SIR epidemic or a server-backlog model simulated with Euler's method at three step sizes, with the step at which the simulation becomes unstable found and explained.

Marks go to the comparison and the written explanation in the notebook. Each student's parameters (the loss, the signal frequencies, the model rates) are generated from the student ID as the seed, so no two notebooks agree, and the quiz after each assignment asks one question about the student's own results.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • This is not the second half of a traditional engineering calculus sequence, and it should not drift into one. Vector-calculus theorems, Laplace transforms and integration techniques are out because no later course uses them; the time goes to automatic differentiation, optimisation and the FFT, which several do. Agree the computing examples with a CSE colleague before the semester, and, as in MATH-1209, have a CSE lab instructor prepare the starter notebooks and help mark them.
  • Semester 4 is the heaviest in the programme, and CSE-2421 Algorithms and the Core Competency Assessment run alongside this course. Use the overlap: the FFT recurrence (week 11) uses the master theorem of CSE-2421 week 3, and linear programming (week 6) prepares for its flow and NP-hardness weeks. Set no quiz or assignment deadline in week 10, when sitting 1 of CSE-2440 falls.
  • The usual failures are backpropagation learned as a recipe for one network, which breaks on a new graph, and complex numbers, which the weaker quarter last met in the EEE-1225 primer. Every quiz gives a graph students have not seen, and week 9 starts from rotation in the plane, not from algebra. For weaker students, issue each assignment as a starter notebook with the plotting code and test cases written, and give a weekly problem sheet in three tiers.
  • AI assistants, SciPy and automatic-differentiation libraries produce any gradient, integral, linear program or spectrum on request. At Level 1 a student may ask an assistant to explain a concept or an error, not to write the solution, and must say in the notebook what help was used; the marks sit on hand-worked steps, set-up and interpretation, and the follow-up quiz checks the notebook. Give students an AI-written backward pass or Lagrange solution with one planted error and ask them to find it.

About this group of courses: Mathematics, Physics and Electrical Fundamentals

This file holds the outlines for the seven mathematics, physics and electrical courses of the programme. The seventh, MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, was added after review of the first draft. Together they replace about 40 credits of mathematics, physics, chemistry and electrical courses in the 2022 syllabus with 23 credits. A topic is kept only if a later course in the programme uses it, and each outline has a table naming the courses that do. The courses are taught as service courses by the Mathematics, Physics and EEE departments, so the outlines are written for a teacher from outside CSE, with examples, assignments and examination questions drawn from computing.
