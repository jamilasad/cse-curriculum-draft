Rationale and what changed

The 2022 syllabus had one networks course and nothing after it. CSE-3543 explains how networks work and configures a VLAN, OSPF and an access list once each in a simulator. This course teaches what a network engineer at an ISP, a bank or a data centre does: design an addressing plan, build and secure campus and branch networks, run routing, plan wireless, connect sites, and then automate the routine parts. Every feature is first configured by hand on the command line and verified with show commands and captures; only in the last weeks are configurations generated and pushed with Python and Ansible. Students work in Packet Tracer for Cisco-style syntax, in GNS3 or containerlab with FRRouting for open-source routing, and on the track rack in rotation for physical skills. Left out: BGP configuration beyond a two-AS lab, MPLS from the provider's side, vendor SD-WAN and wireless-controller products, and data-centre fabrics beyond a mention.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • divide an IPv4 block into variable-length subnets for given host counts, and an IPv6 /48 into /64 subnets, on paper and without errors;
  • configure two VLANs, a trunk and inter-VLAN routing so that stated hosts reach each other, and prove it with show commands and ping;
  • configure single-area OSPF on three routers, verify neighbours and routes, and explain why a given route is missing;
  • write and place an extended access list that meets a written policy, and configure PAT for an inside network;
  • find and fix three planted faults in a small network within a time limit, recording each step.

Stretch goals

  • Build eBGP between two autonomous systems in FRRouting with prefix filtering, and show what a route leak does.
  • Keep the project network in NetBox and generate device configurations from it with Jinja2 templates.
  • Sit CCNA.

Assessment

Theory. The written mid-term and final (80) give most marks to application and analysis: subnet and summarise a block for a described organisation; predict spanning-tree roles from a diagram; read OSPF neighbour and route output and name the fault; order and place access-list entries for a policy; trace a packet's addresses through NAT; choose a BGP best path from listed attributes; plan channels for a floor of access points; read a short Netmiko or Ansible snippet and say what it changes. Recall takes at most a fifth of the marks. Continuous assessment (20): Level 0 quizzes in class (10), including a short subnetting quiz most weeks; two assignments at Level 2 with disclosure (10): an addressing plan for a supplied organisation, and a wireless plan for one campus building.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and record 30; two supervised practical tests 30; project 25; individual viva 15. The project, in pairs: design and build the network of a supplied multi-site organisation (for example a bank with a head office, two branches and a disaster-recovery site) with dual-stack addressing, VLANs, OSPF, an internet edge with NAT and access lists, a wireless plan, a VPN between sites and automated configuration backup. It is delivered as an emulated topology with one part rebuilt on the rack in a booked slot, a design document and a troubleshooting log. In the viva each student changes their own part of the network on request and explains the result.

Resources

  • Main texts: Wendell Odom, CCNA 200-301 Official Cert Guide, Volumes 1 and 2 (configuration); Priscilla Oppenheimer, Top-Down Network Design (design and addressing).
  • Supporting: Russ White and Ethan Banks, Computer Networking Problems and Solutions (protocols across vendors); Jason Edelman, Scott S. Lowe and Matt Oswalt, Network Programmability and Automation; James F. Kurose and Keith W. Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (revision from CSE-3543).
  • Software, all free: Cisco Packet Tracer (free Networking Academy account); GNS3; containerlab; FRRouting; Wireshark; Python 3 with Netmiko; Ansible; FreeRADIUS; WireGuard; NetBox for the stretch goal. Hardware: the track rack (switches, routers, two access points, console cables) described in the track overview.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Subnetting is still the commonest failure in semester 7. Keep a five-minute unaided drill in most classes until every student is accurate; the minimum competency and both tests depend on it.
  • Weaker students configure without predicting. Require a written prediction (which routes, which root, which hosts reach which) before each configuration, and give marks for comparing prediction with result.
  • Emulators teach syntax and logic; the rack teaches the physical work. Plan the rotation so that every student does console access, cabling, password recovery and a configuration restore on real devices at least once.
  • Assistants produce plausible configurations that mix vendors and software versions, or that lock the engineer out of the device. Every assistant-drafted change is tested in emulation first and applied with a safety in place (reload in, commit-confirmed, or an open console session); real configurations with passwords or public addresses are never pasted into a public assistant.
