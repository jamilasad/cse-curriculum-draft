Rationale and what changed

Infrastructure graduates are usually hired first into operations: network operations centres, data-centre shifts, service desks and on-call rotas at banks, telecom operators, MFS providers, data-centre and colocation companies, and managed-service firms serving foreign clients. Those jobs need the physical layer an operator touches and the processes that keep a service running: monitoring, capacity, availability, change, incident and problem management, service levels and documentation. The 2022 syllabus had nothing comparable. The course builds on CSE-3677 and CSE-4787 and goes beyond the first dashboard of CSE-3654 to alert design, capacity forecasting, on-call and service management. By hand first: students read hardware sensors, compute power budgets, and write alert rules and runbooks themselves before they use templates or AI drafts. Left out: electrical and mechanical design of facilities (the course stops at what an operations engineer must check and report) and certificate-style study of ITIL; frameworks are used as vocabulary, not memorised.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  1. From a rack's equipment list with power figures, compute the load, check it against the PDU and UPS ratings with one feed lost, and say whether one more server fits.
  2. Install node and blackbox exporters, write a PromQL query and an alert rule with a sensible threshold and duration, and route it with Alertmanager.
  3. Replace a failed disk in a ZFS or mdadm mirror, confirm the rebuild, and read the SMART data of the removed disk.
  4. Record an incident with timeline, impact and severity, and raise a normal change with risk, test plan and rollback.
  5. Run a supplied runbook in the staging environment, find the step that is wrong or missing, and correct it.

Stretch goals

  • Monitor network and facility devices, including UPS status, over SNMP with snmp_exporter or Zabbix templates.
  • Automate one runbook end to end with checks before and after each step, and keep it under change control.
  • Compare N+1 and 2N power for a small server room in availability and cost, and recommend one.

Weekly plan — lab

CSE-4888 Data Center Operations & IT Service Management Lab · Lab · 1 credits · 2 contact hours a week

How the lab works with limited campus time:

  • The lab keeps its two scheduled hours on one of the two campus days. The department runs a small simulated service (a web shop with a load balancer, two application hosts and a database) on the CSE-4787 Proxmox cluster in two projects: staging, where students may change anything, and production, which changes only through an approved change record. Both are reached through a WireGuard VPN and a browser.
  • Weeks 1–6 (Remote) cover monitoring, alerting, capacity, service desk and documentation. They run as a recorded live online clinic in the scheduled slot, with the deliverable due within the week for students whose placement hours clash. Students with poor connectivity run the same monitoring stack locally with Docker Compose from a supplied repository.
  • Weeks 7–14 (Campus) cover the teaching rack, storage failure, high availability, the visit, the game day, both supervised practical tests and the viva. The lab room stays open on both campus days for catch-up.
  • On-call: between weeks 8 and 12 each student takes two on-call shifts on campus days (10:00–16:00), receiving alerts from the simulated service; acknowledgement times and handover notes go into the lab record. Shifts never fall on placement days.
  • Material from a student's Industry Practice host is used only with the host's written consent and with names removed.

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term and final (80). At least half the marks are application and analysis; recall is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • A rack's equipment list: compute load and heat, check the PDU and UPS with one feed lost, and propose a layout.
  • A set of graphs: find the saturated resource by the USE method and say which alert should have fired.
  • A noisy alert rule set: rewrite two rules, remove one, and add the missing one.
  • Component availabilities: compute service availability and choose redundancy to meet a stated SLA, with its cost.
  • An incident narrative from a card-payment service: build the timeline, set severity, find where the process failed, and write two actions.
  • A change request: find what is missing in risk, test and rollback, and decide whether to approve.
  • An AI-drafted runbook: find the dangerous step and the missing verification.

Continuous assessment (20): Level 0 quizzes on campus from week 7, best three of four (10); two assignments (10): a post-incident review of a published outage report, and a one-page power, cooling and redundancy review of the visited or toured facility.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record, including on-call shifts, 30; two supervised practical tests at Level 0, 30; lab project 25; individual viva 15. Project (teams of three): an operations handbook for the simulated service or, with consent and names removed, for a service at a member's Industry Practice host. It contains the NetBox inventory, monitoring and alert rules with a runbook per alert, a capacity plan, a high-availability design with its failover test, an SLA and service-desk procedure, five runbooks tested by another team, the game-day incident and change records and review, and an on-call rota with a handover template. A student who cannot explain their own runbooks and alert rules in the viva does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Textbooks: Thomas A. Limoncelli, Christina J. Hogan and Strata R. Chalup, The Practice of System and Network Administration; Betsy Beyer, Chris Jones, Jennifer Petoff and Niall Richard Murphy (eds), Site Reliability Engineering, with the companion The Site Reliability Workbook (both free to read online); Brian Brazil, Prometheus: Up & Running; Luiz André Barroso, Urs Hölzle and Parthasarathy Ranganathan, The Datacenter as a Computer; AXELOS, ITIL Foundation: ITIL 4 Edition (one library copy). Optional reading: Gene Kim, Kevin Behr and George Spafford, The Phoenix Project.
  • Free references: Uptime Institute tier classification overview, ASHRAE thermal guidelines summary, The Green Grid's definition of PUE, PagerDuty's public incident-response documentation.
  • Software (all free): Prometheus, Alertmanager, Grafana, node_exporter, blackbox_exporter, snmp_exporter, Zabbix (alternative), Grafana Loki, NetBox, GLPI or Zammad, Uptime Kuma, keepalived, HAProxy, PostgreSQL with Patroni and etcd, Pacemaker and Corosync (demonstration), ipmitool, smartmontools, ZFS, mdadm, restic, MkDocs, WireGuard, Proxmox VE, Docker Compose.
  • Hardware: a teaching rack with two or three decommissioned rack servers that have management controllers, a PDU (metered if available), a patch panel, cables and spare disks; the CSE-4787 Proxmox cluster.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The course fails if it becomes memorising ITIL terms and tier tables. Tie every week to an artefact: a power sum, an alert rule, a forecast, a change record, a runbook.
  • Semester-8 logistics: publish all remote material in week 1; book the visit a semester ahead (sites need ID lists, confidentiality forms and small groups, so plan two groups); agree the campus days and on-call shifts with the Industry Practice coordinator; keep staging and production separate so one student's mistake never breaks another's work.
  • Mid-level students often do well here, because operations rewards care and procedure. Give templates (incident record, change request, runbook, capacity memo) with one worked example each and a plain-English glossary; internal notes may be drafted in Bangla, customer-facing text is in English.
  • AI assistants draft alert rules, PromQL, scripts and runbooks in seconds, and also produce plausible commands with the wrong device name, outdated syntax or a destructive step. The rule is the one taught in week 1: AI-drafted material runs first in staging with its output recorded in the lab record; running unverified material against production loses the week's marks; production logs, customer data and placement-host material never go into an external AI service. The Level 0 tests and viva check that the student can read a dashboard, write a rule and spot an unsafe step unaided.
