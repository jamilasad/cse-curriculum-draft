Rationale and what changed

PO6 and PO7 need a course where they are taught and examined, not a paragraph at the end of a project report. The 2022 syllabus had a 2-credit course under the same code, Social, Professional, and Ethical Issues in Computing; this course keeps its place and credits and adds law, AI ethics, sustainability and accessibility; the general-education course GEEM-1101 covers moral foundations and is not repeated here. This course is about decisions a working engineer meets: what to do with personal data, what a licence allows, what to do on finding a vulnerability, when an AI system is unfair, and what a system costs society and the environment. It is taught by cases. The law is described by the principles that most jurisdictions share and by the regimes of the client markets (the European data protection regulation, the sector-by-sector approach of the United States), because client contracts pass those duties to Bangladeshi firms and freelancers. Domestic law is presented in one guest session by a practising lawyer and is not written into the syllabus, because it changes. Left out: the history of moral philosophy and memorised lists of statutes.

Blended delivery. Each week has one hour of input and one hour of case seminar. The input is live-streamed from the classroom and recorded, with a short reading. The seminar is held on a campus day in groups of five or six; students on a six-week Industry Practice block join an online seminar in weeks 1–6. Each group prepares one case brief during the semester and leads that seminar. In line with the semester-8 rule, weeks 1–6 use only work that can be done remotely, and the quizzes and the written mid-term (on weeks 1–7) are sat on campus in weeks 7–14, after the block has ended.

Minimum competency

  • Given a short unseen case, name the stakeholders and harms, cite at least two clauses of a supplied code of ethics that apply, and defend one action.
  • Given a list of dependencies with their licences (MIT, Apache-2.0, GPL, AGPL), say whether closed-source distribution is allowed and what must be done.
  • Draw the personal-data flow of a simple application and identify at least three privacy problems, each with a fix.
  • Check one web page against five basic accessibility tests (text alternatives, contrast, keyboard access, form labels, heading order) and report the failures.
  • State what to do, and what not to do, on discovering a vulnerability in someone else's system.

Stretch goals

  • Measure the fairness of a model on a real data set with Fairlearn and report which measures conflict.
  • Measure the energy use of a training or inference job with CodeCarbon and reduce it.
  • Report a real vulnerability through a coordinated disclosure or bug-bounty process, or publish a plain-language article on one of the course cases.

Weekly plan — theory

WeekTopics and casesCLO
1Why ethics is engineering work: software that harmed people (Therac-25, the Boeing 737 MAX flight-control software, the Volkswagen emissions software). Ethics, law and company policy are three different things. A five-step method for any case: facts, stakeholders, options, principles, decision1
2Tools for reasoning: consequences, duties, rights and character, in one session without the history. Professional codes: the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct, the IEEE Code of Ethics, the ACM/IEEE-CS Software Engineering Code. Using a clause as an argument1
3Responsibility at work: honest estimates and status reports, shipping known defects, conflicts of interest, client confidentiality, misrepresented skills, shared or rented freelance accounts, interviews taken by a proxy. Raising a concern; whistle-blowing. Seminar on outsourcing scenarios written by the teacher1
4Privacy: what personal data is and how harm occurs. Principles found in most data protection regimes: lawful purpose, consent, minimisation, security, limited retention, the rights of the person. Privacy by design. Drawing a personal-data flow. Case: Cambridge Analytica and Facebook data2
5Data protection for a developer serving foreign clients: the European regulation (GDPR) as the reference model; the sectoral approach of the United States; how contracts pass duties to a supplier; breach notification; cross-border transfer. Guest session: the domestic legal position, described generally2
6Intellectual property: copyright in code; who owns work done for an employer or client; trade secrets; patents and trademarks in outline; pirated software and unlicensed assets. Case: Oracle v. Google on copying an API3
7Open-source licences: permissive (MIT, BSD, Apache-2.0) and copyleft (GPL, LGPL, AGPL); compatibility; duties when distributing and when offering a service. Licences of data sets, images, fonts and models (Creative Commons). AI and IP: generated code, training data, model terms3
8Security ethics: unauthorised access is an offence in most jurisdictions whatever the intent. Authorised testing and its scope. Coordinated vulnerability disclosure, bug bounties, the duty to patch and to notify. Cases: the Equifax breach, the Log4Shell disclosure, the 2016 Bangladesh Bank transfer fraud4
9AI ethics I, bias and fairness: where bias enters (data, labels, objective, deployment); computing selection rate and error rates by group by hand; why fairness measures conflict. Cases: COMPAS risk scores, Amazon's abandoned CV-screening tool, the Gender Shades study of face analysis5
10AI ethics II, accountability and safety: who answers when a model is wrong; transparency, explanation and human oversight; generative AI risks (fabricated output, deepfakes, confidential data pasted into prompts); risk-based regulation such as the EU AI Act, in outline; honest disclosure of AI use in one's own work5
11AI, automation and work: effects on software service work and outsourcing; data-labelling and moderation labour; platform work; the digital divide, language and inclusion, including Bangla-language users. Seminar: what this means for a graduate entering this market5
12Sustainability: energy and carbon cost of data centres, model training and inference; device life cycle and e-waste; green software practice (measure, right-size, cache, schedule). The Sustainable Development Goals: linking a project to a target with a measure, and what an empty claim looks like6
13Accessibility and inclusive design: disability and assistive technology; the four WCAG principles; testing with keyboard only and with a screen reader; users on low-cost phones and slow networks; accessibility requirements in client markets, described generally6
14Synthesis: selected audit presentations of capstone projects; one unseen case worked by the whole class under examination conditions; review1–6

Assessment

Written mid-term and final (80). Both papers are built on unseen cases written in simple English, with the relevant extract of a code of ethics printed in the paper. Answers may be given as tables and numbered points; reasoning is marked, not prose style. Kinds of question:

  • A 300-word case (an engineer told to ship with a known defect; a freelancer asked to reuse one client's code for another): stakeholders, issues, code clauses, options, a recommended action and its cost.
  • A data-flow diagram of an application: find the privacy problems and redesign the flow.
  • A dependency list with licences and a business plan for the product: what is allowed, what must change, what notices are required.
  • A vulnerability scenario: order the steps of a responsible disclosure and name the two actions that would break the law.
  • A table of model results by group: compute selection rates and false-positive rates, say whether the model is fair by each measure and what to do.
  • A described screen: list the accessibility failures. A described deployment: name the two largest sources of energy use and a way to cut each.

Recall is at most a fifth of the marks.

Continuous assessment (20). Quizzes at Level 0 (10): three in-class quizzes in weeks 8, 11 and 13, when block students are back on campus, best two counted. Assignments (10):

  • Group case brief and leading one seminar (4): two pages, using the five-step method.
  • Individual audit of the student's own capstone project (6): about 1,200 words on a template: stakeholders; personal-data inventory and flow; licence inventory of dependencies, data and models; accessibility check of one screen; energy and cost estimate; AI risks; one SDG target with a measure; one change the team will make. The team's capstone report later summarises these audits; the individual analysis is marked here.

Resources

  • Michael J. Quinn, Ethics for the Information Age
  • Sara Baase and Timothy M. Henry, A Gift of Fire: Social, Legal, and Ethical Issues for Computing Technology
  • Herman T. Tavani, Ethics and Technology; Cathy O'Neil, Weapons of Math Destruction (further reading)
  • The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct with its published case studies; the IEEE Code of Ethics; the W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines; the licence texts published by the Open Source Initiative
  • Tools: WAVE and Lighthouse for accessibility checks, the NVDA screen reader, a licence scanner (license-checker for npm, pip-licenses for Python), Fairlearn, CodeCarbon.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Two ways to fail: a lecture course on moral philosophy, or a list of laws to memorise. Every session should end with students having decided something about a case and defended it.
  • The classic cases are foreign and can feel distant. Pair each with a short scenario set in a Bangladeshi service firm or freelance job, written by the teacher and kept generic.
  • English is weak on entry. Supply a glossary, keep cases short, accept structured answers, and let seminar groups prepare in Bangla as long as the brief and the report-back are in English.
  • Students on Industry Practice meet live cases. Invite them to bring one, anonymised, to the seminars from week 8.
  • AI tools write a plausible case analysis in seconds. The marks therefore sit in the unseen examination cases and in oral seminar work; the audit is checked against the capstone repository, which an invented answer will not match.

About this group of courses: Professional Practice, Capstone and Industry Practice

These six courses, all in semesters 7 and 8, carry most of the programme's evidence for outcomes PO6 to PO12; the technical courses carry PO1 to PO5. Each PO claimed here is tied to a named piece of marked work that can be kept on file for an accreditation visit. The courses feed one another: CSE-4753 and CSE-4760 run beside Capstone Project I and supply its plan, cost model, proposal and presentations, and CSE-4805 runs beside Capstone Project II and Industry Practice and takes its cases from them. Everything is specified for an intake of five to eleven sections twice a year (taken here as 200 to 300 students), so the supervision, panel and placement arithmetic is shown.
