Rationale and what changed

Almost every system a graduate builds or operates is a networked one, so the course aims at two abilities: explaining how data gets from one program to another across the Internet, and finding out why it did not. It absorbs the 2022 Data Communication course. Signals, encoding, modulation and multiplexing are cut to the essentials in weeks 2–4: what limits the data rate of a link, how bits are framed and checked, and how media differ. Added: TLS, network security basics, an introduction to software-defined and cloud networking, and troubleshooting taught as a method. Left out: line-coding and modulation schemes worked by hand, signal mathematics, and legacy technologies such as X.25, Frame Relay, ATM and token ring. The lab rests on four things: a packet analyser, a network simulator, socket programming and fault-finding.

Minimum competency

  • Given an IPv4 address and prefix length, give the network address, broadcast address, host range and number of hosts; divide a /24 into subnets for stated host counts.
  • In a packet capture, find a TCP three-way handshake, a DNS query with its response, and an HTTP request, and read off the addresses, ports and sequence numbers.
  • In the simulator, join two LANs through a router with correct addresses, masks and gateways so that hosts on each can ping the other.
  • Write a TCP client and server that exchange lines of text, and explain each socket call.
  • Given a host that cannot reach a website, work through a fixed sequence (link, address and mask, gateway, name resolution, port) with ping, traceroute and a DNS lookup tool, and name the failing step.

Stretch goals

  • Build reliable transfer over UDP (stop-and-wait, then a sliding window) and test it under simulated loss.
  • Program forwarding rules for an OpenFlow switch in Mininet.
  • Configure VLANs with inter-VLAN routing and multi-area OSPF in the simulator, as a start on CCNA preparation.

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term (weeks 1–7) and final (whole course, weighted to weeks 8–14) together carry 80 marks. At least half the marks are for application and analysis:

  • design an addressing plan for a described organisation and fill in the forwarding tables;
  • follow a packet hop by hop through a drawn network, giving source and destination MAC and IP addresses on each link, with and without NAT;
  • from a printed capture extract, give the next sequence and acknowledgement numbers, identify a retransmission, and say which side closed the connection;
  • find and correct the errors in a misconfigured network diagram (wrong mask, wrong gateway, missing route);
  • diagnose from symptoms, for example "ping to the address works but the name does not", and name the layer at fault;
  • run Dijkstra's algorithm on a small topology; compute a CRC; compute a delay or a throughput;
  • choose and justify: TCP or UDP for a described application, where to place a firewall, what an attacker on the same Wi-Fi can see with HTTP and with HTTPS.

Recall (layer names, port numbers, header fields) is at most a fifth. Continuous assessment (20): four quizzes of about 15 minutes at Level 0, two of them on subnetting, best three counted (10); two problem sets and one capture-analysis report, at Level 1 (10).

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30. Two supervised practical tests on lab machines, Level 0, with addresses that differ for each student, 30. Lab project 25, in pairs, one of: (a) the network of a small organisation designed and built in the simulator, with three or four departments, a server subnet, a wireless segment, DHCP, NAT at the edge and one access-control rule, with an addressing plan and test evidence; or (b) a networked application with its own application-layer protocol over TCP, such as a chat service with rooms or a file transfer that can resume, with a written protocol specification and a capture showing it at work. Either comes with a design note in English and the AI-use log required at Level 2. Individual viva 15. A student who cannot explain their own configuration or code in the viva does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • James F. Kurose and Keith W. Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (main text; after chapter 1 its chapters are assigned in the bottom-up order of the weekly plan).
  • Andrew S. Tanenbaum, Nick Feamster and David J. Wetherall, Computer Networks.
  • Larry L. Peterson and Bruce S. Davie, Computer Networks: A Systems Approach (free to read online).
  • Behrouz A. Forouzan, Data Communications and Networking (for the physical and link-layer essentials).
  • Wendell Odom, CCNA 200-301 Official Cert Guide (optional, for students who want the certificate).
  • Software, all free: Wireshark; Cisco Packet Tracer (needs a free Networking Academy account) or GNS3; Python 3 with its standard socket library; ping, traceroute, dig, curl, iperf3 and the OpenSSL command-line tool; Mininet for the stretch goal.
  • Hardware: the ordinary lab PCs and network. Optional and cheap: UTP cable, crimping tools and a cable tester, two unmanaged switches, one Wi-Fi access point. Physical routers are not needed in this course.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Subnetting decides whether a mid-level student passes this course and later the CCNA. Give a ten-minute drill at the start of a lecture most weeks from week 5, two quizzes on it, and a worked method that starts from the binary form before any shortcut.
  • Students learn the layer names by heart and still cannot say which addresses change at a router. Use "follow the packet" questions every week. Balance the simulator, where nothing is ever loose or noisy, with captures from the real lab network.
  • For weaker students: the fault-finding sequence printed on a card, lab sheets with screenshots for the first six weeks, and the simulator installed at home. Explanations in class may mix languages, but fault tickets and reports are written in English.
  • Assistants explain captures and generate device configurations well, but they do not know the state of the student's network. At Level 2 every generated configuration must be tested and logged. Set one rule in week 1: capture only your own traffic on the lab network.

About this group of courses: Systems and Hardware Core

These six packages take the student from a single gate to a networked computer running many programs. Digital Logic Design (S3) builds gates, registers and state machines; Computer Organization & Architecture (S4) assembles those parts into a RISC-V processor with a memory hierarchy and shows how C becomes instructions. Semester 5 then looks at the machine from three sides: Microcontrollers & Embedded Systems programs a bare processor with no operating system, Operating Systems explains the software that shares one machine among many programs, and Computer Networks connects machines to each other. Automata, Languages & Compilers (S7) closes the sequence with what machines can and cannot compute, and how the languages used throughout the programme are translated and run.
