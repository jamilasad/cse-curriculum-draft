Rationale and what changed

The 2022 syllabus had one credit of Technical Writing and Presentation in semester 7. This course triples the time and aims it at what gets a graduate hired and paid by clients abroad: a clear proposal and report, a talk and a demonstration, an honest CV and public portfolio, interviews, day-to-day English with clients and remote teams, and the basics of contracts and payment. It runs beside Capstone Project I, so the proposal, the defence and the technical report are real documents, not exercises. It builds on CSE-1130 and on the README and design notes written in every studio. Left out: general English grammar teaching (GEEL-1106 covers it), literary essay writing and business correspondence unrelated to software work.

Minimum competency

  • Write unaided, in 30 minutes, a 150–200-word status update or bug report from supplied facts, with no error that blocks understanding.
  • Give a five-minute talk on their own project without reading from slides or a script, and answer two questions.
  • Produce a one-page CV with no false claim and a public repository whose README lets a stranger run the project.
  • Reply in writing to a client request that cannot be met as asked: what is possible, by when, on what assumptions.
  • Answer "tell me about a project you built" and one behavioural question in English in a mock interview so that the interviewer follows the answer.

Stretch goals

  • Publish a technical article, or record a ten-minute conference-style talk, in English.
  • Run a real discovery call in English with the capstone client and circulate the minutes.
  • Apply for real remote roles or internships during the semester and bring the feedback back to the class.

Resources

  • Mike Markel and Stuart A. Selber, Technical Communication
  • Justin Zobel, Writing for Computer Science
  • William Strunk Jr. and E. B. White, The Elements of Style
  • Gayle Laakmann McDowell, Cracking the Coding Interview

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Speaking time is the scarce resource. In speaking weeks split the section into groups of three or four working at once, with observer rubrics, so that every student speaks for at least ten minutes each session.
  • With weak English, aim for fluency before accuracy: correct only the errors that block understanding, and give sentence frames for the hard moments (reporting a delay, asking for clarification, refusing).
  • "By hand once, then with tools": the first draft is written unaided in the lab; the student then revises with a grammar checker or an AI assistant and submits both versions with a note on what changed and why. This teaches AI as editor, not author.
  • Every student will ask an AI tool for a CV, and the results are alike and often contain invented claims. Check each CV line against the portfolio; the supervised tests and the mock interview hold most of the marks because no tool can help there.
  • Teach three or four sessions jointly with an English-language teacher where the university can release one, and recruit alumni online as mock interviewers and role-play clients.

About this group of courses: Professional Practice, Capstone and Industry Practice

These six courses, all in semesters 7 and 8, carry most of the programme's evidence for outcomes PO6 to PO12; the technical courses carry PO1 to PO5. Each PO claimed here is tied to a named piece of marked work that can be kept on file for an accreditation visit. The courses feed one another: CSE-4753 and CSE-4760 run beside Capstone Project I and supply its plan, cost model, proposal and presentations, and CSE-4805 runs beside Capstone Project II and Industry Practice and takes its cases from them. Everything is specified for an intake of five to eleven sections twice a year (taken here as 200 to 300 students), so the supervision, panel and placement arithmetic is shown.
