Rationale and what changed

This is the one core course in which every student writes software that touches hardware directly, with no operating system underneath and with limited time, memory and power. It replaces Microprocessors, Microcontrollers and Embedded Systems from the 2022 syllabus. The 8086 microprocessor, its assembly language and its support chips are reduced to a short historical note in week 1. The course is built on a current 32-bit microcontroller board. Whether that is an ARM Cortex-M board or an ESP32-class board is the department's choice, and the plan below is board-neutral: every topic is a peripheral or technique that both families provide. Left out: assembly programming beyond reading a start-up listing, printed-circuit-board design, control theory, and the internals of a real-time operating system, which belong to the EMB track (CSE-4779).

Minimum competency

  • From a pin-out and a datasheet extract, wire an LED, a button and one sensor to the board correctly: series resistor, pull-up or pull-down, matching voltage levels.
  • Write, build and flash a C program that reads a debounced button and blinks an LED from a timer, with no busy-wait delay.
  • Write an interrupt service routine that sets a flag or counts events, and share that data safely with the main loop.
  • Read an analogue sensor through the ADC, convert the raw value to physical units, and send it over UART to a terminal on a PC.
  • Capture a UART or I2C transaction with a logic analyser, identify the address and data bytes, and find the fault in a given broken set-up.

Stretch goals

  • Write a register-level I2C or SPI driver from the reference manual, without the vendor library.
  • Run the project under FreeRTOS with two or three tasks and a queue, and measure worst-case response time with the logic analyser.
  • Bring the device's average current below a set target using sleep modes, and show the measurement.

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term (weeks 1–7) and final (whole course, weighted to weeks 8–14) together carry 80 marks. Because the board is the department's choice, questions supply the register table or datasheet extract they need. At least half the marks are for application and analysis:

  • read a firmware fragment and say what the pins do over time;
  • find and fix the defect: a missing volatile, a blocking call inside a service routine, an unprotected shared variable, a wrong bit mask;
  • from a register extract, write the C statements that set a timer period or a PWM duty cycle; convert an ADC reading to volts and then to units; estimate battery life from a current profile;
  • decode a printed logic-analyser trace of a UART or I2C frame and identify the fault;
  • choose and justify: polling or interrupt, UART or I2C or SPI, super-loop or real-time operating system, one of three microcontrollers for a stated product;
  • draw the state machine for a described device and outline its firmware.

Recall (frame formats, definitions) is at most a fifth. Continuous assessment (20): four quizzes of about 15 minutes at Level 0, best three counted (10); two calculation-and-design problem sets and one datasheet-reading assignment, at Level 1 (10).

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30. Two supervised practical tests at the bench, Level 0, 30. Lab project 25: in pairs, a small connected device that meets a written specification, with at least one sensor, one actuator or display, one I2C or SPI device, a timer- or interrupt-driven structure with no blocking delays, and readings delivered to a PC or broker. Examples: a room climate monitor with an alarm, a water-tank level monitor that switches a pump relay, a soil-moisture irrigation controller, a door-entry logger. The repository holds the code, a design note in English with wiring diagram and state diagram, a logic-analyser capture as test evidence, and the AI-use log required at Level 2. Individual viva 15. A student who cannot explain their own firmware in the viva does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Elecia White, Making Embedded Systems (firmware structure and practice; main reading).
  • Jonathan W. Valvano, Embedded Systems: Introduction to ARM Cortex-M Microcontrollers (if a Cortex-M board is chosen).
  • Joseph Yiu, The Definitive Guide to ARM Cortex-M3 and Cortex-M4 Processors (teacher's reference).
  • The datasheet and reference manual of the chosen board, and the manufacturer's programming guide.
  • Software, all free: the manufacturer's toolchain (STM32CubeIDE for STM32 boards, ESP-IDF for ESP32 boards) or PlatformIO; a serial terminal; sigrok PulseView for the logic analyser; FreeRTOS; the Mosquitto MQTT broker; the Wokwi simulator for practice without a board.
  • Hardware per bench: one microcontroller development board with a debug connection; breadboard and jumper wires; LEDs, buttons, potentiometer; temperature and humidity sensor, light sensor, one I2C sensor, one SPI device; small OLED or character display; servo or small DC motor with driver; low-cost 8-channel USB logic analyser; multimeter.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Choosing the board: a Cortex-M board with an on-board debugger gives cleaner register-level teaching and step debugging but needs an add-on for wireless; an ESP32-class board is cheap, has Wi-Fi and Bluetooth built in and already runs FreeRTOS, but step debugging takes more set-up on some models. Buy one model for all sections, with spares, since boards are damaged by 5 V on 3.3 V pins.
  • The usual failure is hobby-style code copied from the internet and built on blocking delays. Forbid blocking delays after week 4. The second failure is treating a wiring fault as a software fault: teach a fixed order of checks (power, ground, pin, voltage level, then code).
  • For weaker students: a five-question pre-lab check each week, wiring diagrams supplied until week 6 and drawn by the students afterwards, alternating roles within the pair, and the simulator for practice at home.
  • Assistants write plausible firmware for the wrong board, pin or library version, and cannot see the wiring. Teach students to give the assistant the datasheet facts and to confirm every claim on the logic analyser. The practical tests and viva are at the bench at Level 0.

About this group of courses: Systems and Hardware Core

These six packages take the student from a single gate to a networked computer running many programs. Digital Logic Design (S3) builds gates, registers and state machines; Computer Organization & Architecture (S4) assembles those parts into a RISC-V processor with a memory hierarchy and shows how C becomes instructions. Semester 5 then looks at the machine from three sides: Microcontrollers & Embedded Systems programs a bare processor with no operating system, Operating Systems explains the software that shares one machine among many programs, and Computer Networks connects machines to each other. Automata, Languages & Compilers (S7) closes the sequence with what machines can and cannot compute, and how the languages used throughout the programme are translated and run.
