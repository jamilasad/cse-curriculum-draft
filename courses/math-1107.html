Rationale and what changed

This is Calculus I, the first of two calculus courses; MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4 continues it. With MATH-1209 it replaces Mathematics I–IV and Mathematical Analysis for CS from the 2022 syllabus. Topics were chosen by asking what three users need: machine learning (derivatives, the chain rule, gradients, optimisation), algorithm analysis (limits, logarithms, growth rates, sums and series), and physics and circuits (integration, exponential behaviour, first-order differential equations). Left out: integration techniques beyond substitution and parts, volumes and arc length, multiple integrals, vector calculus, second-order differential equations, Laplace and Fourier transforms, and complex variables; of these, multiple integrals, complex numbers and Fourier methods are taken up in MATH-2409. Most students have met the rules of differentiation and integration at HSC, so less time goes on rules and more on meaning, graphs and modelling. Each technique is practised by hand first; graphing software and a short Python loop come afterwards, to check answers and to reach cases too long for hand work.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Differentiate a function built from powers, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometric functions using the product, quotient and chain rules, and say what the value of the derivative means in the units of the problem.
  • Find the largest or smallest value of a one-variable function on an interval and justify the answer.
  • Evaluate a definite integral by substitution or by parts and explain it as an area or a running total.
  • Put log n, n, n log n, n² and 2ⁿ in order of growth with a reason, and sum a finite geometric series.
  • Compute the partial derivatives and the gradient of a two-variable function at a point, and solve dy/dt = ky with an initial value.

Stretch goals

  • State the ε–δ definition of a limit and use it to prove a simple limit.
  • Solve a constrained optimisation problem with a Lagrange multiplier, and evaluate a double integral over a rectangle.
  • Solve second-order linear differential equations with constant coefficients and describe the oscillation of an RLC circuit (useful before the EMB track).

Weekly plan — lab

None. This is a theory-only course with no paired lab. The graphing and Python work in the two assignments is done outside class.

Assessment

Theory: mid-term and final (80). The mid-term covers weeks 1–7. At least half the marks are for application and analysis; recall of formulas and theorem statements is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • A graph is given: sketch the derivative, mark where the function increases, estimate a definite integral from the grid.
  • Differentiate or integrate, then state the result in words with units ("the queue is growing by … requests per second").
  • A cost depends on one design parameter, such as a buffer size: find the value that minimises it and justify the answer.
  • Rank four running-time functions using limits.
  • Approximate a function with a Taylor polynomial and bound the error.
  • A loss function of two parameters is given: compute the gradient at a point and take one gradient-descent step.
  • A capacitor voltage or a cooling object is modelled by a differential equation: solve it and find the time to reach a given level.

Continuous assessment (20). Four 15-minute quizzes, best three counted (10), Level 0. Two assignments (5 each), Level 1. Assignment 1 (weeks 1–6): functions, tangents and one optimisation problem explored in GeoGebra, with the working written by hand. Assignment 2 (weeks 7–13): Riemann sums and Taylor approximations computed with a short Python loop and compared with the exact values. Marks are given for the set-up, the check and the written interpretation, because the bare answers can be had from any tool.

Resources

  • James Stewart, Calculus: Early Transcendentals (main text).
  • George B. Thomas and others, Thomas' Calculus (alternative).
  • Gilbert Strang and Edwin Herman, Calculus, Volumes 1–3, OpenStax (free).
  • GeoGebra (free) for graphs; Python 3 with Matplotlib for the second assignment.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Start each topic from a graph or a table of numbers, then bring in the symbols. Students arrive able to apply rules and unable to say what the answer means, and the examination marks are placed on the meaning.
  • Run a 20-minute diagnostic on algebra, logarithms and trigonometry in week 1. The weaker quarter will have gaps; give them a revision sheet with worked solutions and test again in week 3. Logarithms matter more to a CSE student than any integration technique.
  • Do not put back the topics that were cut. Multiple integrals, complex numbers, Fourier methods and systems of differential equations belong to MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, which builds directly on weeks 11–14 here (Taylor polynomials, partial derivatives, the gradient and chain rule, first-order equations). Agree notation for the gradient and the chain rule with the MATH-2409 teacher.
  • Symbolic calculators and AI assistants solve every routine exercise with the steps shown. Teach students to check such answers (differentiate the antiderivative, plot it, substitute a number), and keep the marks on set-up and interpretation.

About this group of courses: Mathematics, Physics and Electrical Fundamentals

This file holds the outlines for the seven mathematics, physics and electrical courses of the programme. The seventh, MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, was added after review of the first draft. Together they replace about 40 credits of mathematics, physics, chemistry and electrical courses in the 2022 syllabus with 23 credits. A topic is kept only if a later course in the programme uses it, and each outline has a table naming the courses that do. The courses are taught as service courses by the Mathematics, Physics and EEE departments, so the outlines are written for a teacher from outside CSE, with examples, assignments and examination questions drawn from computing.
