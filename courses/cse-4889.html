Rationale and what changed

Showing that a chip design is correct takes more of a project's effort than writing the design, because a fault found after manufacture costs a new mask set and months of delay. Chip projects therefore employ at least as many people on verification as on design, and verification is the realistic entry point to the semiconductor industry for a graduate here. The work is software-heavy (object-oriented testbenches, randomisation, coverage, scripting, debugging), which suits CSE students. The course is new with the track. It sits in semester 8 because it needs no hardware: everything runs in a simulator on a PC, which fits a semester with two campus days. SystemVerilog is taught because employers test it at interview; Python-based verification with cocotb is taught beside it because it runs on free simulators, so every student can practise at home. Left out: the full Universal Verification Methodology (register layer, layered sequences), formal verification beyond a demonstration, gate-level and low-power simulation, mixed-signal verification and emulation.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Write a self-checking test for a small design (FIFO, counter, ALU), in cocotb or SystemVerilog, that drives stimulus, compares against a reference model and reports pass or fail with the seed.
  • Write a SystemVerilog class with random fields and two or three constraints, and say which values can and cannot be generated.
  • Write three concurrent assertions for a handshake or FIFO (a request is granted within a stated number of cycles; valid holds until ready; no write when full) and read a failing assertion message.
  • Write a covergroup with bins and one cross for a given feature list, and read a coverage report to name what has not been tested.
  • From a failing log and waveform, find the first point of failure and write a bug report: expected, observed, how to reproduce (test, seed, time).

Stretch goals

  • A complete UVM or pyuvm agent for the APB bus with a sequence library, reused on two designs.
  • Formal property checking of the FIFO or an arbiter with SymbiYosys, compared with what simulation covered.
  • Verification of a small open-source RISC-V core against the Spike instruction-set simulator, or an accepted test, coverage model or bug report in an open hardware project.

Weekly plan — lab

CSE-4890 Digital Design Verification with SystemVerilog Lab · Lab · 1 credits · 2 contact hours a week

How the lab runs with limited campus time. The lab needs no hardware: every exercise is a simulation that runs on an ordinary PC. The lab repository holds the designs, a Makefile and the tool set-up. The Python flow (cocotb with Icarus Verilog or Verilator) installs on any laptop running Linux, macOS or Windows with WSL. The SystemVerilog flow runs on the lab PCs, which should also be reachable by remote login to a department server for students with weak laptops. The two scheduled hours on a campus day are used for the demonstration, marking of the previous week's checkpoint and help with debugging; the rest of the exercise is finished off campus and pushed to Git, where continuous integration reruns it. Students on the six-week block follow weeks 1–6 from a recorded demonstration and a weekly online help hour, and are marked from their commits. Both practical tests, the project demonstration and the viva are on campus, in weeks 8, 13 and 14, after the block has ended. Weeks 2–7 are written unaided; AI assistance at Level 2 is allowed from week 9.

Assessment

Theory. Written mid-term (weeks 1–7) and final (whole course, weighted to weeks 8–14), 80 marks together, sat on campus. Kinds of question:

  • Code reading: given a class with constraints, list the legal values or say why the solver fails; given an assertion and a waveform, say whether it passes, fails or passes vacuously, and in which cycle.
  • Find the defect in a testbench: a race with the design, a scoreboard that never compares, coverage sampled at the wrong event, an assertion that fires during reset.
  • Design: from a one-page specification, write a verification plan with a coverage model and three assertions; draw the testbench architecture.
  • Analysis: given a coverage report and a regression summary, decide what to do next and whether the design can be signed off.
  • UVM: label the parts of an environment diagram, order the phases, say where a given check belongs.
  • Recall (at most one-fifth of the marks): terms such as vacuous pass, cross coverage, sequence item.

Continuous assessment, 20: four short quizzes at Level 0 on campus days (10); two assignments (10), one a verification plan for an assigned open specification and one a written triage of a supplied regression log.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30; the record is the commit history, the automatic-run results and a short weekly log, checked at the next campus session. Two supervised practical tests 30 (weeks 8 and 13, 15 each). Lab project 25, alone or in pairs: verify one small design to a stated coverage goal. Designs: UART, SPI master, FIFO with flags, round-robin arbiter, timer or PWM peripheral with an APB-style register interface. The teacher seeds faults into each design and does not say how many. Delivered: verification plan, environment in SystemVerilog or cocotb/pyuvm, regression script, coverage report, bug reports, a closure summary and an AI-use log; the plan and bug reports are marked with the shared writing rubric. The mark follows the faults found and explained, not the size of the testbench. Individual viva 15 on the student's own environment.

Resources

  • Textbooks: Chris Spear and Greg Tumbush, SystemVerilog for Verification; Ray Salemi, The UVM Primer; Ray Salemi, Python for RTL Verification; Janick Bergeron, Writing Testbenches Using SystemVerilog; Ashok Mehta, SystemVerilog Assertions and Functional Coverage. The free online courses and UVM Cookbook of the Siemens Verification Academy are useful for self-study.
  • Software, all free: cocotb, pyuvm, cocotb-coverage and pytest on Icarus Verilog or Verilator; GTKWave; the open UVM library from Accellera; SymbiYosys for the formal demonstration; Git with continuous integration. For SystemVerilog classes, constraints, covergroups, assertions and UVM, the lab uses the simulator inside a free FPGA vendor tool edition (the Vivado simulator supports these at the time of writing; confirm on the installed version before the semester). It does not depend on which FPGA board was bought. EDA Playground, a free browser service, is a fall-back.
  • Industry norm and what is missed: Synopsys VCS, Cadence Xcelium and Siemens Questa, with their debug, coverage-management and formal products and commercial verification IP. Icarus Verilog cannot run class-based SystemVerilog testbenches, and Verilator's support is partial, so on open simulators the class-based work is done in Python. Students miss solver speed, class-aware debugging, coverage merging and ranking over thousands of runs, and gate-level and low-power simulation. The three vendors run university programmes; the department should apply, but the course does not depend on the result.
  • Hardware: none beyond PCs.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The usual failure is the test that cannot fail: no checker, a reference model copied from the design, an assertion that passes vacuously. Always mark against seeded faults, and have each student show every check failing once on a broken design.
  • Full UVM overwhelms mid-level students. Teach the plain layered testbench first (weeks 3–5), then present UVM as the standard names for the same parts. Students read and extend an environment; only stretch work writes one from an empty file.
  • The course rewards care more than brilliance, which suits methodical mid-level students. Give them the fixed order plan → stimulus → check → coverage, a bug-report template in simple English, and weekly checkpoint marking so that nobody goes silent during practice weeks. Keep the lab room open outside class on campus days for students without an adequate PC.
  • Assistants write UVM and cocotb boilerplate and explain simulator messages well, and junior engineers use them this way. Danger areas: expected values or a reference model generated from the RTL, which repeats the fault and passes; assertions that look right but are vacuous or one cycle off; constraints that silently exclude corner cases; SystemVerilog features or library calls the lab simulator does not have. The student derives every check from the specification. Teach the industry habit as well: customer designs are confidential and are not pasted into a public AI service.
