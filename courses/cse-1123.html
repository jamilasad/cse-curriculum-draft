Rationale and what changed

Discrete mathematics is the mathematics that programs are made of: logic for conditions, sets and relations for data, induction for loops and recursion, counting for running time, graphs for networks, and number theory for cryptography. The framework keeps it as a full course and places it beside Programming I (CSE-1121), so each topic is worked on paper first and then checked with a few lines of Python of the kind students are writing that week. Two blocks get more room than is usual: number theory up to a small RSA example, because Information Security depends on it, and probability by counting, because STAT-2313 starts from it. Left out: algebraic structures (groups, rings, lattices), generating functions, and graph theory beyond what Data Structures and Algorithms use. Simplification of Boolean circuits is left to Digital Logic Design, and automata and grammars to CSE-4751.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Build the truth table of a three-variable expression, decide whether two conditions are equivalent, and negate a quantified statement.
  • Write an induction proof of a summation or divisibility claim with the base case, hypothesis and step set out.
  • Solve a counting problem by choosing correctly between the product rule, permutations and combinations, and give the probability of an event when outcomes are equally likely.
  • Draw a graph from its adjacency list, state the degree of each vertex, and write the order in which breadth-first and depth-first search visit the vertices.
  • Compute a greatest common divisor by Euclid's algorithm and a power modulo n by repeated squaring, for small numbers.

Stretch goals

  • Prove binary search or Euclid's algorithm correct with a loop invariant and a termination argument.
  • Solve linear recurrences with constant coefficients, and state and use the master theorem.
  • Write RSA key generation, encryption and decryption in Python with its built-in big integers, and explain what an attacker would have to compute to break it.

Weekly plan — lab

None. This is a theory-only course with no paired lab. The short Python checks in the assignments are done in the student's own time on the CSE-1121 set-up.

Assessment

Theory: mid-term and final (80). The mid-term covers weeks 1–7. At least half the marks are for application and analysis; at most a fifth are for recall of definitions and laws. Typical questions:

  • A Python condition or a five-line function is given: write it in logic, simplify it, or give an input for which it is false.
  • A written proof with one wrong step is given: find the step and repair it. Write an induction proof of a given claim.
  • Counting questions set on passwords, loop iterations and hash-table collisions.
  • A graph is given as an adjacency list: draw it, trace breadth-first or depth-first search, decide whether it is connected or has a cycle.
  • Run Euclid's algorithm, find a modular inverse, decrypt a toy RSA message.

Continuous assessment (20). Four 15-minute quizzes, best three counted (10), Level 0. Two assignments (5 each), Level 1: each is a hand-worked problem set with a short Python check, for example printing a truth table, confirming a counting formula by brute force, or testing Euclid's algorithm against math.gcd. The quiz after each assignment repeats one of its problems with the numbers changed.

Resources

  • Kenneth H. Rosen, Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications (main text).
  • Susanna S. Epp, Discrete Mathematics with Applications (slower and clearer on proof; for students who struggle with Rosen).
  • Eric Lehman, F. Thomson Leighton and Albert R. Meyer, Mathematics for Computer Science (free; for stretch work).
  • Python 3, as installed for CSE-1121. No other software.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Agree the order of topics with the CSE-1121 teacher in week 1. The examples in weeks 5, 7 and 10 assume that students have met lists, loops and recursive functions in Python; if the two courses drift apart, move the example, not the topic.
  • The usual failure is copying proof patterns without following them, and weak English makes it worse. Give fixed sentence frames ("Assume …. Then …. Therefore …."), mark the logic before the language, and have students read proofs aloud line by line before they write their own.
  • For the weaker quarter, issue a weekly sheet in three tiers (routine, standard, harder) and open each class with one routine problem from the previous week.
  • AI assistants produce fluent proofs, and some are wrong. Use that: give students an AI-written proof with a flaw and ask them to mark it. At Level 1 a student may ask an assistant to explain a definition or why a step fails, not to write the answer; the follow-up quiz shows whether they did the work.

About this group of courses: Mathematics, Physics and Electrical Fundamentals

This file holds the outlines for the seven mathematics, physics and electrical courses of the programme. The seventh, MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, was added after review of the first draft. Together they replace about 40 credits of mathematics, physics, chemistry and electrical courses in the 2022 syllabus with 23 credits. A topic is kept only if a later course in the programme uses it, and each outline has a table naming the courses that do. The courses are taught as service courses by the Mathematics, Physics and EEE departments, so the outlines are written for a teacher from outside CSE, with examples, assignments and examination questions drawn from computing.
