Rationale and what changed

One course replaces Basic Electrical Engineering, Electronics, and Electrical Drive & Instrumentation with their labs: 12 credits become 4. The aim is a CSE student who can read a schematic, build and measure a small circuit, say what is inside a logic gate, and connect a sensor or a load to a microcontroller pin. Transistors are taught first as switches, which is what Digital Logic Design (S3) and Microcontrollers & Embedded Systems (S5) need, and only briefly as amplifiers. Left out: three-phase circuits, machines and drives, magnetic circuits, small-signal models and multistage amplifiers, oscillators, and industrial instrumentation. Every circuit is analysed by hand first, then simulated, then built and measured; a disagreement between the three is the main teaching material of the lab.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Calculate the voltages and currents in a two-loop resistive circuit, and confirm them by measurement to within the tolerance of the parts.
  • Build a circuit on a breadboard from a schematic and measure DC voltage, current and resistance with a multimeter without damaging the meter or the parts.
  • Display a signal on an oscilloscope, read its amplitude, period and frequency, and measure an RC time constant.
  • Choose the series resistor for an LED and the base resistor for a transistor that switches a given load from a 5 V logic signal.
  • State the voltage step of an n-bit ADC with a given reference and convert a reading to a voltage.

Stretch goals

  • Design, build and measure a two-stage sensor amplifier with a filter, and report its noise and bandwidth.
  • Draw the schematic and a single-sided printed-circuit layout of the lab project in KiCad.
  • Analyse RLC resonance and build a 555 timer oscillator (useful before the EMB track).

Assessment

Theory: mid-term and final (80). The mid-term covers weeks 1–7. At least half the marks are for application and analysis; recall is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • Analyse a given circuit for named voltages and currents, by a method of the student's choice.
  • Fault finding: a schematic and a table of measured node voltages are given; identify the faulty or wrongly placed component.
  • Design choices: the series resistor for an LED, the base resistor for a transistor that switches a relay from a 3.3 V pin, the ADC resolution needed to read a temperature to 0.5 °C.
  • Read a drawn oscilloscope trace: find the period, frequency, time constant or ripple.
  • Read a datasheet extract (logic-level thresholds, maximum pin current) and decide whether two parts can be connected directly.

Continuous assessment (20). Four 15-minute quizzes, best three counted (10), Level 0. Two assignments (5 each), Level 1: problem sets in which two problems are also simulated in LTspice, with the schematic, the simulated values and a sentence on any difference from the hand result.

Lab (100).

  • Weekly lab work and lab record (30): each record has the schematic, calculated values, measured values and a note on any difference.
  • Two supervised practical tests at the bench, Level 0 (30): weeks 6 and 13; each student builds and measures alone.
  • Lab project (25): a small analogue interface circuit built in pairs on a breadboard in weeks 8–14, such as a light-operated lamp switch with a relay, a temperature alarm with a comparator and buzzer, or a battery-level indicator with LEDs. Deliverables are the working circuit, calculations, a simulation, measurements and a two-page report in English.
  • Individual viva (15): week 14. A student who cannot explain their own circuit does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Charles K. Alexander and Matthew N. O. Sadiku, Fundamentals of Electric Circuits (weeks 1–7).
  • Robert L. Boylestad and Louis Nashelsky, Electronic Devices and Circuit Theory (weeks 8–13).
  • Paul Scherz and Simon Monk, Practical Electronics for Inventors (practical reference for the lab).
  • Adel S. Sedra and Kenneth C. Smith, Microelectronic Circuits (stretch).
  • LTspice (free) or ngspice (open source) for simulation; KiCad (open source) for schematics; Tinkercad Circuits (free, in a browser) for breadboard practice away from the lab.
  • Per bench of two students: breadboard and jumper wires, digital multimeter, DC supply or 9 V battery. Shared between benches if necessary: two-channel oscilloscope and function generator. Parts kit: resistors, capacitors, 1N4007 and 1N4148 diodes, LEDs, Zener diodes, BC547 transistors, 2N7000 MOSFETs, LM358 op-amps, 7805 regulators, light-dependent resistors, thermistors, relays, small DC motors. One pre-programmed microcontroller board for the week 12 ADC station.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • EEE teachers will find this outline thin beside the three courses it replaces. The cut is deliberate. The test for depth is what Digital Logic Design and Microcontrollers & Embedded Systems use, so three-phase circuits, machines and small-signal amplifier analysis should not return.
  • Many students can solve a circuit on paper and cannot build or measure one. Wrong breadboard rows, a meter left in current mode and an untriggered oscilloscope are the usual causes. Pairs swap the builder and measurer roles every week, and the practical tests are taken alone.
  • PHY-1201 runs in the same semester and reaches the p–n junction only in its week 13, so week 8 here needs a 20-minute qualitative account of the junction. Phasors are the main barrier for weaker students: MATH-1107 does not teach complex numbers, so week 6 revises them from HSC mathematics, and AC work stays with first-order circuits.
  • Simulators and AI assistants solve any textbook circuit. The examination therefore leans on fault finding, datasheets and measured traces, and the lab mark on the student's own readings, the bench tests and the viva. At Level 1 a student may ask an assistant to explain a concept or a simulator error, not to produce the lab record.

About this group of courses: Mathematics, Physics and Electrical Fundamentals

This file holds the outlines for the seven mathematics, physics and electrical courses of the programme. The seventh, MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, was added after review of the first draft. Together they replace about 40 credits of mathematics, physics, chemistry and electrical courses in the 2022 syllabus with 23 credits. A topic is kept only if a later course in the programme uses it, and each outline has a table naming the courses that do. The courses are taught as service courses by the Mathematics, Physics and EEE departments, so the outlines are written for a teacher from outside CSE, with examples, assignments and examination questions drawn from computing.
