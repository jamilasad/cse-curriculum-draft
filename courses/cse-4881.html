Rationale and what changed

The core (CSE-3653, CSE-3654) gives every student the concepts of distributed systems and one experience of putting an application in a container and deploying it through a pipeline. This course is for those who will own services after deployment. Clients increasingly expect the team that builds a service to run it as well, often with on-call cover across time zones, and operations work rewards methodical people whatever their CGPA. The course goes from "deployed once" to "kept within a stated reliability target": orchestration in depth, communication between services under failure, infrastructure as code, safe release, observability, service-level objectives, incident response, capacity, cost and platform security. It is new, with no predecessor in the 2022 syllabus. Left out: building the cloud itself (virtualisation, data-centre operations; INF track), a tour of one vendor's services, and security governance (SEC track).

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Deploy a given two-service application with a database to a Kubernetes cluster from manifests they write (Deployment, Service, configuration, probes, resource requests) and reach it through an ingress.
  • Find and fix the fault in a broken deployment (wrong image, failing probe, missing configuration, insufficient resources) using events, descriptions and logs, within 30 minutes.
  • Carry out a rolling update and a rollback, and show from metrics whether the service stayed available.
  • From a dashboard and logs, say whether a service is meeting a given objective and how much error budget is left.
  • Write a one-page postmortem of a drill: impact, timeline, contributing causes, actions with owners, and no blame.

Stretch goals

  • Automated canary analysis with Argo Rollouts, promoting or rolling back on service-level metrics.
  • A service mesh (Linkerd) for mutual TLS and per-route metrics, with its overhead measured.
  • A chaos experiment with a written hypothesis; or an accepted contribution to a cloud-native open-source project.

Weekly plan — theory

Delivery: weeks 1–6 are streamed live and recorded, because students doing Industry Practice as a six-week block are off campus; weeks 7–14 are taught on campus on one of the two timetabled days. Quizzes are Level 0 and are held only in on-campus weeks.

Weekly plan — lab

CSE-4882 Cloud-Native Engineering & Site Reliability Lab · Lab · 1 credits · 2 contact hours a week

How the lab works with limited campus time:

  • The environment travels with the student. Every exercise runs from the course repository on a local Kubernetes cluster (kind or k3d) on a laptop with 8 GB of memory. Students without such a laptop use lab machines on campus days, or a namespace on a small department cluster (k3s on three or four ordinary lab PCs) reached remotely.
  • Each week has three parts: about one hour of preparation at the student's own time (reading, pulling images, the first half of the exercise); the two scheduled hours, kept for what needs a teacher (check-off, fault-finding, drills, tests); and submission by pull request.
  • Weeks 1–6, marked [R], can be done fully off campus: the session is run in the lab and by video call at once, and off-campus students are checked off by pull request and a five-minute live or recorded demonstration. Weeks 7–14, marked [C], need attendance. Both practical tests, the game day and the viva fall in those weeks.
  • No student needs a paid cloud account or a bank card. Infrastructure as code is practised against the local cluster; where the department has education credits, an instructor-controlled account with budgets and automatic teardown is used, and otherwise vendor services are shown by demonstration.
  • With the host company's written permission, a student may base the week-10 objectives and the theory assignment on a service at their Industry Practice workplace, with identifying details removed.

Assessment

Theory. Written mid-term and final, 80 marks. Question types:

  • Given manifests and printed output of events, descriptions and logs, say why the workload is not serving and correct the manifest.
  • Given a Dockerfile, improve its size, build caching and security, and explain each change.
  • Case: a chain of three services with stated timeouts and retries; work out the load on the last service when it slows down, then redesign.
  • Given 30 days of request and latency figures, compute the indicator, compare it with the objective, compute the remaining error budget and decide whether to release.
  • Given ten alerts, say which should wake someone and why; rewrite one as a burn-rate alert.
  • Case: a release that changes a table used by the running version; write the steps, including rollback.
  • Given an incident timeline, write the contributing causes and three actions; or rewrite a postmortem that blames a person.
  • Capacity and cost: from given prices and load, estimate the nodes needed and the monthly bill, and compare a managed database with a self-hosted one.
  • Recall, at most a fifth of the marks: roles of Kubernetes objects; definitions of indicator, objective, agreement, recovery point and recovery time.

Continuous assessment (20): three Level 0 quizzes in on-campus weeks (10); one individual assignment (10), a written analysis of a published postmortem from a real company: what failed, how it was detected, and which practices from the course would have limited it.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and record 30, the record being the repository and check-offs. Practical tests in weeks 8 and 13, 15 each. Project 25: teams of two or three take one application to production readiness on the cluster. The team's capstone system is encouraged; the reference application, extended, is the alternative. Required: all infrastructure and delivery defined in Git; a pipeline with one progressive deployment strategy and a tested rollback; dashboards; two objectives with burn-rate alerts and runbooks; the game-day postmortem; a capacity and cost estimate; a security checklist with scan results. Viva 15, on campus: the examiner breaks something in the student's deployment and asks them to find it and explain their reasoning. A student who cannot explain their own work does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Betsy Beyer, Chris Jones, Jennifer Petoff and Niall Richard Murphy (eds.), Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems; Betsy Beyer and others (eds.), The Site Reliability Workbook. Both are free to read online and are the main texts for weeks 9–13.
  • Brendan Burns, Joe Beda, Kelsey Hightower and Lachlan Evenson, Kubernetes: Up and Running; or Marko Lukša, Kubernetes in Action.
  • Sam Newman, Building Microservices. Michael Nygard, Release It!
  • Yevgeniy Brikman, Terraform: Up & Running. Alex Hidalgo, Implementing Service Level Objectives.
  • Charity Majors, Liz Fong-Jones and George Miranda, Observability Engineering. Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble and Gene Kim, Accelerate.
  • Official documentation for Kubernetes, Prometheus and OpenTelemetry; the AWS Well-Architected Framework as a reference for weeks 12–14.
  • Tools: Docker Engine or Podman; kind or k3d, and k3s for the department cluster; kubectl, Helm, Kustomize; OpenTofu; Argo CD; Prometheus, Alertmanager, Grafana, Loki, OpenTelemetry, Jaeger; k6; Toxiproxy; RabbitMQ or NATS; Trivy.
  • Hardware: no purchase. A laptop or lab PC with 8 GB of memory (16 GB is more comfortable); three or four existing lab PCs as the department cluster, with a pull-through registry cache so that images are not downloaded repeatedly over the campus link.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Assistants make manifests, queries and infrastructure code free to produce, so students deploy things they cannot read. Both practical tests and the viva are fault-finding on a live system because that cannot be done by generation. In labs, have students say what a command will show before they run it.
  • Campus time is scarce. Publish the preparation a week ahead, list the images to pull while on campus Wi-Fi, keep check-offs to five minutes, and get from the Industry Practice coordinator the list of students on a six-week block before week 1. Never put a supervised test in weeks 1–6.
  • The number of tools is the main risk for weaker students. Keep to one tool for each job, and publish a working catch-up branch every week so that a student who failed last week's lab can still do this week's. The minimum competencies are all operation with kubectl and a dashboard, which careful mid-level students can master.
  • Teach a checking habit for AI at Level 2: read the plan or diff before applying, try in a scratch namespace first, never paste secrets into a tool, and give an assistant or agent read-only credentials unless a person approves the change.
