Rationale and what changed

The 2022 Project/Thesis becomes a two-semester, 8-credit project in the student's track with a real client, user group or dataset. It is the programme's main evidence that graduates can solve a complex engineering problem as the accreditation body defines one, and the main evidence for PO8. Capstone I ends with an approved proposal, a design and a working prototype of the riskiest part; Capstone II ends with a deployed or demonstrable system, a report and an individual defence. Left out: projects invented by the team for itself, projects that repeat a tutorial, and reports written at the end from memory.

All 2022 courses and their fates

Shared design (applies to CSE-4700 and CSE-4800)

Teams and clients. Teams of three or four students of one track, formed in week 1 within a section group as the university's sectioning requires; the coordinator places anyone unplaced. One member of another track may join where the project needs it. Each team has a named client or user group outside the team, or a real dataset with a named problem owner who will judge the result; a tutorial dataset does not qualify. Clients come from a project bank kept by the capstone committee: partner firms, university offices, schools, clinics, shops, farms and local government offices near the campus, NGOs, alumni start-ups and teachers' research groups. Students may bring their own client. A client may host up to three teams on different problems and signs a one-page form: contact person, problem, data access, who may use and publish the result, and thirty minutes every two weeks, which may be online.

Typical projects by track. SWE: a deployed web or mobile system for a client. AID: a model or data product evaluated against a baseline on the client's data and served through an application. SEC: an authorised security assessment and hardening of a real system, or a detection tool evaluated by measurement. INF: an infrastructure design, migration or automation for a real organisation, with monitoring and a runbook. EMB: a device with firmware and a back end, tested in the field.

Complex engineering problem. Every proposal carries a one-page complexity declaration. The project must show WP1 and at least three of WP2–WP7; the panel checks the claim at the proposal defence and again in the final report.

Supervision at intake scale. The scheduled hour is a cluster session: one supervisor meets up to five teams of the same track together. In Capstone I each team has about ten minutes to report and show its work and the remaining time is a common clinic; in Capstone II the two hours give each team about twenty minutes with a demonstration of the week's increment. Teams also submit a five-minute weekly form (done, next, blocked, hours per member). All teams use one Git hosting organisation and one board tool so that records can be read uniformly.

A teacher who takes one Capstone I cluster and one Capstone II cluster carries three scheduled hours a week and up to ten teams; 20 of the department's roughly 46 teachers cover the largest intake on that basis. Cluster hours count as teaching load. SWE is the largest track, so any teacher with project experience may supervise an SWE cluster, with a track specialist holding a weekly technical clinic. A capstone committee (the coordinator and one teacher per track) keeps the project bank, approves proposals, forms panels, moderates marks and hears appeals. Alumni and partner engineers are invited as online co-mentors, about one session a month per cluster.

Panels. Milestone marks are never set by the supervisor alone. Proposal defence: track panels of three, 15 minutes a team; six panels clear 100 teams in one day. Prototype review: two supervisors sit together and review each other's clusters, 30 minutes a team. Final defence: two examiners, at least one not the supervisor, 45 minutes a team; eight panels clear 100 teams in two days.

Separating individual from team marks. Of 100 marks, 40 are individual: contribution evidence 20 and the individual viva or defence 20. The other 60 are team marks, and each member receives the team mark multiplied by a contribution factor. The factor is 1.0 unless three sources agree that a member did less than a fair share: the repository and board record (commits, merged pull requests, reviews given, tickets closed, read for substance and not counted by lines); a confidential peer assessment twice a semester, in which each member rates every teammate from 1 to 5 on share of work, quality, reliability, communication and help given; and the supervisor's weekly record of who reported and showed what. The supervisor may then set 0.8, 0.6 or 0.4, confirmed by the committee, to which the student may appeal. A member with no meaningful contribution receives no team marks and fails. The factor never exceeds 1.0; strong contributors are rewarded through the individual marks. A student who cannot explain their own committed work in the viva or defence does not pass, whatever the team mark.

AI-use rules. Level 2 applies to all development and writing; Level 3 applies where the product itself is an AI system. Each repository holds a usage log (AI-USAGE.md): date, member, tool and model, purpose, what was accepted or changed, how it was verified, and the commit. The member who commits a line answers for it in the viva. Client data, credentials and unpublished client code are not pasted into public AI services without the client's written consent. In reports, AI may correct language, and this is declared; every cited source must have been read by the team, and an invented reference is academic misconduct. Spending on cloud and AI services is budgeted in the plan and reported. Defences and vivas are at Level 0.

Thesis alternative. For students aiming at graduate study, a research thesis may replace the product. It is done alone or in pairs, on application in weeks 1–2 with a one-page research question and a supervisor who publishes in the area and has agreed; as a guide, no more than one student in ten, limited by supervisors. Courses, credits, milestone dates, marks structure, repository, usage log and individual defence are the same; what is delivered changes. The proposal is a research proposal with a review of at least fifteen peer-reviewed sources and a method. The prototype is a reproduction of a published baseline or a pilot study on the real dataset. Capstone II delivers completed experiments, an analysis, and a thesis in the form of a paper (eight to ten pages in IEEE conference format, with code and data in an appendix); submission to a venue is encouraged and not required for the grade. WP1, WP3 and WP4 are normally shown, and WP6 through the data owner or the group the research serves. A thesis with no workable method by the design review converts to a product project on the same topic.

Block students in semester 8. Team members on a six-week Industry Practice block attend cluster sessions online in weeks 1–6 and carry a lighter share in the first two sprints, made up afterwards; contribution is judged over the whole semester. Teams are advised to choose the same Industry Practice timetable. Hours and work counted for Industry Practice cannot be counted for the capstone.

Minimum competency

  • State the project's problem, client and success measure in two minutes without notes.
  • Open the repository and explain a piece of their own work line by line, and a teammate's part in outline.
  • Explain one design decision: the alternatives, the reason for the choice, and its cost.
  • Show their own tasks on the plan: the estimate, the actual time and what comes next.

Stretch goals

  • A prototype already in use by the client before the end of the semester.
  • A design trade-off settled by a measured experiment instead of an argument.
  • For thesis students: a baseline reproduced to within the published figures.

Notes for the supervisor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The commonest failure is a project with no real client, discovered at the defence. Do not let a team past week 2 without a named contact who has answered a message.
  • Scope is the second. A mid-level team should be steered to a small problem solved completely; the complexity attributes are met by depth, not by a long feature list.
  • Read the repository before each session, not only the weekly form. A member with no commits by week 9 needs a conversation then, not a reduced factor in week 14.
  • AI tools let a team produce a polished proposal and a large codebase quickly. Ask "why" questions in every session and have a different member drive the demonstration each week.

About this group of courses: Professional Practice, Capstone and Industry Practice

These six courses, all in semesters 7 and 8, carry most of the programme's evidence for outcomes PO6 to PO12; the technical courses carry PO1 to PO5. Each PO claimed here is tied to a named piece of marked work that can be kept on file for an accreditation visit. The courses feed one another: CSE-4753 and CSE-4760 run beside Capstone Project I and supply its plan, cost model, proposal and presentations, and CSE-4805 runs beside Capstone Project II and Industry Practice and takes its cases from them. Everything is specified for an intake of five to eleven sections twice a year (taken here as 200 to 300 students), so the supervision, panel and placement arithmetic is shown.
