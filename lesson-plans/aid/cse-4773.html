Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Write a PyTorch training loop without a high-level trainer (data loader, model, loss, optimiser, training and evaluation modes, validation) and train a small classifier with it.
  • Look at a pair of training and validation curves and name the likely fault (learning rate too high, underfitting, overfitting, leakage) and one fix.
  • Compute scaled dot-product attention for three tokens by hand, and say what each block in a transformer diagram does.
  • Load a pre-trained encoder and its tokeniser, fine-tune it on a small Bangla or English text-classification set, and report accuracy and macro-F1 against a majority-class and a TF-IDF baseline.
  • For a described use case, say whether to prompt, retrieve or fine-tune, and give two reasons.

2. Class pattern (assumption to confirm)

These follow README assumptions A1–A5.

  • Theory. 3 contact hours a week, given as two 90-minute classes. That makes 28 classes in 14 teaching weeks. Classes are numbered 1–28; Class 2k−1 and Class 2k fall in week k.
  • Lab. One 2-hour session a week: 14 sessions.
  • Examinations. The mid-term (weeks 1–7) is held in an examination week after week 7. The final is held after week 14. Neither uses a class slot. The outline says nothing different.
  • Quizzes. Four Level 0 quizzes of 20 minutes, at the start of Class 6 (week 3), Class 12 (week 6), Class 18 (week 9) and Class 24 (week 12). The best three count.
  • If the mid-term takes a class slot. Class 14 contains a 25-minute review block. Drop it. Teach the pre-training objectives in the last 15 minutes of Class 13 (shorten its pair task) and the "what each family is good for" card sort in the first 20 minutes of Class 15.
  • Re-split into three 60-minute classes. Use README A2. Each 90-minute plan below is written in blocks that allow the cut at minute 60 of the first class and minute 30 of the second.

3.2 Class entries

Class 1 (Week 1): Layers, activations, loss functions and the computational graph

  • Outline topics covered: From the core course to deep networks: layers, activations, loss functions; the computational graph.
  • CLO / PO: CLO1 / PO1
  • Timed plan (90 min):
MinActivity
0–10Opening: course map, the four track threads, AI-use levels in this course, why tests and viva are Level 0.
10–20Readiness check (unmarked, Level 0, on paper, self-marked): chain rule for f(g(x)); the shape of Wx for W of shape 3×2; softmax of (0, 0).
20–45Short lecture: a layer as an affine map plus a nonlinearity; why stacked linear layers collapse into one; ReLU, sigmoid, tanh and GELU with their derivatives; output layers and matching losses (squared error for regression, softmax with cross-entropy for classes).

4. Lab lesson plan

4.1 How the lab runs

  • Compute (from the outline). The department has no GPU cluster. Every exercise is sized for one free notebook GPU session (training runs of about 20 minutes or less) or the CPU of a lab PC. Students save checkpoints. The instructor keeps a trained checkpoint for every lab and caches model weights and datasets on the lab file server before class. No session depends on a large download over the lab network. Every GPU lab has a CPU fallback, given in its entry.
  • AI use. Lab work is at Level 3: the model is the thing being built and measured. Coding assistants may be used under the Level 2 conditions (usage logged in AI-USAGE.md, output tested, every line defended). By-hand work in weeks 1, 2 and 5 is Level 1. Practical tests (weeks 7 and 13) and the viva (week 14) are Level 0.
  • Marking. Regular sessions (weeks 1–6 and 8–12, eleven in all) are marked by README rubric R1 (0–3 each; best 10 count; 30 marks). The tests use R2 and the schemes in section 6.4. The project and viva use section 6.5.
  • Pairs. Weekly lab work is done in pairs where the sheet says so, but each student keeps their own record and answers spot questions alone. Pairs change at week 8, when project pairs form.

S3. Application: forward pass and one gradient step (Apply, CLO1, 6 marks). A network has input x = (1, 2), one hidden layer of two ReLU units and one linear output. W₁ = [[0.5, −0.5], [0.2, 0.1]] (one row per hidden unit), b₁ = (0, 0), w₂ = (1.0, 2.0), b₂ = 0. Target y = 1. Loss L = ½(ŷ − y)². Learning rate η = 0.05. (a) Compute the forward pass and L. (b) Compute all gradients. (c) Compute the updated parameters. (d) Compute the new loss and comment.

Worked answer.

  • (a) z₁ = W₁x = (0.5 − 1.0, 0.2 + 0.2) = (−0.5, 0.4). h = ReLU(z₁) = (0, 0.4). ŷ = 1.0·0 + 2.0·0.4 + 0 = 0.8. L = ½(0.8 − 1)² = 0.02. (1.5 marks)
  • (b) ∂L/∂ŷ = ŷ − y = −0.2. ∂L/∂w₂ = −0.2·h = (0, −0.08). ∂L/∂b₂ = −0.2. ∂L/∂h = −0.2·w₂ = (−0.2, −0.4). ∂L/∂z₁ = (−0.2·0, −0.4·1) = (0, −0.4), because ReLU′(−0.5) = 0. ∂L/∂W₁ = (∂L/∂z₁)xᵀ = [[0, 0], [−0.4, −0.8]]. ∂L/∂b₁ = (0, −0.4). (2.5 marks)
  • (c) w₂ = (1.0, 2.004); b₂ = 0.01; W₁ = [[0.5, −0.5], [0.22, 0.14]]; b₁ = (0, 0.02). (1 mark)
  • (d) New z₁ for unit 2 = 0.22 + 0.28 + 0.02 = 0.52; ŷ = 2.004 × 0.52 + 0.01 ≈ 1.052; L ≈ ½(0.052)² ≈ 0.0014. The loss fell from 0.02. Unit 1 received no gradient because its ReLU input was negative. (1 mark)

Marking notes: carry errors through; do not penalise the same slip twice. Extension for strong students: repeat with η = 0.1 and show that the step overshoots (ŷ ≈ 1.305, L ≈ 0.047, higher than before).

S4. Application: attention for three tokens (Apply, CLO3, 6 marks). Tokens: "আমি", "ভাত", "খাই". d_k = 4, so √d_k = 2. q₁ = (1, 0, 1, 0), q₂ = (0, 2, 0, 0), q₃ = (1, 1, 0, 0). k₁ = (1, 0, 1, 0), k₂ = (0, 1, 0, 1), k₃ = (1, 1, 1, 1). v₁ = (1, 0), v₂ = (0, 1), v₃ = (1, 1).   • Divide by 2: row 1 (1, 0, 1); row 2 (0, 1, 1); row 3 (0.5, 0.5, 1). (0.5 mark)

S5. Application: parameter count (Apply, CLO3, 3 marks). A transformer layer has d_model = 768, 12 heads, d_ff = 3072, biases everywhere and two layer norms. Count its parameters. Worked answer. Attention: four projections (Q, K, V, output), each 768 × 768 + 768 = 590,592, so 2,362,368. Feed-forward: 768 × 3072 + 3072 + 3072 × 768 + 768 = 4,722,432. Layer norms: 2 × (768 + 768) = 3,072. Total 7,087,872 (about 7.1 million). The number of heads does not change the count. Marking: 1 mark for each part. Check for discussion: twelve such layers give about 85 million; with the token-embedding table this is why a "base" encoder has about 110 million parameters.

S6. Application: LoRA and memory (Apply, CLO4, 6 marks). (a) A 12-layer encoder with d_model = 768. LoRA at rank r = 8 is applied to the query and value projections of every layer, and a new 5-class head is trained. How many trainable parameters? What share of a 110-million-parameter model is this? (b) What memory is needed for the weights of a 7-billion-parameter decoder at 16-bit and at 4-bit precision? (c) Can the 7-billion-parameter model be fully fine-tuned with Adam on a GPU with 16 GB of memory? Explain. Worked answer.

  • (a) Each adapted matrix adds r(d_in + d_out) = 8 × (768 + 768) = 12,288. Two per layer: 24,576. Twelve layers: 294,912. Head: 768 × 5 + 5 = 3,845. Total 298,757, about 0.27% of 110 million. (2.5 marks)
  • (b) 16-bit: 2 bytes × 7 × 10⁹ = 14 GB. 4-bit: 0.5 byte × 7 × 10⁹ = 3.5 GB, plus a small overhead for the quantisation scales, plus the attention cache and activations at run time. (2 marks)
  • (c) No. The 16-bit weights alone take 14 GB; gradients and the two Adam moments add much more (full fine-tuning in 32-bit with Adam needs about 16 bytes per parameter, about 112 GB). Use LoRA on a 4-bit base instead. (1.5 marks)

6.7 CLO attainment

The method is README section 5.3; this is how it applies here.

  • Item map. Built from the tables in 6.3, 6.4 and 6.6: every mid-term and final question part, each quiz, each A1 problem, each A2 criterion, each weekly session, each test part, each project criterion and each viva criterion, with its CLO and PO.
  • Student CLO score. Marks obtained on the items of a CLO ÷ the maximum marks of those items × 100. Theory and lab are calculated separately. A student attains the CLO at 50% or more (proposed threshold).
  • Course attainment. The share of students attaining each CLO, theory and lab reported separately; target 60% (proposed).

  • Action when a CLO is below target (examples). CLO3 low: more spreadsheet practice on attention and an extra clinic before Quiz 2. CLO2 low: a second "broken script" exercise before test 1. 7. Support for weaker students

Below 40% at any point leads to a meeting with the teacher and a written plan of two or three actions (for example: attend two clinics; redo the Lab 2 checklist with the instructor; do the by-hand homework and bring it to office hours).

9. Issues for the committee

These are reported, not fixed. The plan above works around each one as stated.

  1. Practical test 2 may not fit on a lab-PC CPU. The outline asks students, at Level 0 on a lab PC with cached weights, to adapt a small pre-trained encoder to a text dataset "sized for CPU" and to answer written questions, inside one 2-hour session. Fine-tuning a 100–300-million-parameter encoder on a CPU is slow. The time depends heavily on the lab PCs, which this plan has not measured. The plan assumes a run of 25 minutes or less on the slowest PC, achieved by a small encoder, short texts and a modest number of examples. If that cannot be achieved, the committee must decide whether a frozen encoder with a trained head (or only the top layer unfrozen) counts as "adapting" the model for this test and for the minimum competency.
  2. Budget and terms for hosted APIs. Lab 12 and the project's prompted model use a hosted API "on a free tier or under a spending cap for each pair". The framework's resource section names no such budget (README issue 2). Free tiers also limit the number of requests: Lab 12 needs several hundred calls per pair. If the department provides no capped key, the local quantised model becomes the prompted model, and the cost comparison in Lab 12 has to use published prices rather than measured spending. The committee should also decide whose account and data terms students use.
  3. Dependence on free notebook GPU services. Labs 4, 6, 8 and 9 and the project assume a free notebook GPU. These services need a personal account with an outside provider, some ask for phone verification before giving GPU access, and quotas change without notice. The CPU fallbacks in this plan (frozen backbones, smaller models, skipping the 4-bit part of Lab 9) change what is learned. The committee should decide whether the fallbacks are acceptable as equivalent, or whether a small shared GPU machine should be budgeted.
  4. Assessment congestion in weeks 7, 13 and 14 for AID students. CSE-4774 and CSE-4784 both hold practical test 1 in week 7, practical test 2 in week 13 and the viva in week 14 (their outlines). Capstone I holds its proposal defence in week 7 and its prototype review with individual viva in week 13 (CSE-4700 weekly plan). An AID student therefore faces three Level 0 assessments in each of weeks 7 and 13, plus the mid-term after week 7, and two lab vivas and the Capstone I progress report in week 14. At minimum the timetable must put them on different days; the committee may wish to move one of the lab tests.
  5. A1 is Level 2 although it rehearses Level 0 skills. The outline's header sets assignments at Level 2, so the handwritten problem set on backpropagation and attention may be done with AI help. Its 5 marks then reward completion more than individual skill. The framework allows a stricter level for named work. The committee may wish to set A1 at Level 1, or keep Level 2 and rely on the explanation checks in this plan.
  6. Order of lab week 11 and theory week 12. Lab 11 builds a probe set for failure types one week before the theory class on how language models fail and how to probe them. The plan handles this with a one-page failure-type sheet in Lab 11 and a re-labelling task after Class 23. No change to the outline is needed unless the committee prefers to swap the two lab weeks.
