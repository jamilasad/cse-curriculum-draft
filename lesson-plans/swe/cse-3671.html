1. Course facts

Copied from the outline. Nothing here is changed.

Credits and contactTheory 3 cr, 3 h/week; Lab 1 cr, 2 h/week
SemesterS6
CategoryTrack SWE (Track Course I)
PrerequisitesCSE-2325 Object-Oriented Programming & Design; CSE-2423 Database Systems; CSE-2433 Software Engineering; CSE-2434 Software Engineering Studio. Taken alongside CSE-3651, CSE-3653 and CSE-3660
AI-use levelExaminations and quizzes: 0. Assignments: 2. Lab work: 2, except weeks 1–3 (code reading unaided, Level 0). Lab project: 2. Lab tests and viva: 0.

Course learning outcomes

CLOOn completing the course the student canBloomPO
1Describe the architecture of an existing system from its code: modules, dependencies, data flow and the style in use, recorded as context, container and component diagramsAnalysePO2
2Judge module structure by cohesion, coupling, information hiding and dependency direction, and propose a refactoring with its benefit and costEvaluatePO2, PO3
3Model a business domain (entities, value objects, aggregates, bounded contexts) and derive from it a consistent data model and API contractCreatePO3
4Compare architectural styles against stated quality requirements and justify a choice, saying what would change the decisionEvaluatePO3
5Analyse a design for performance, reliability and security weaknesses, and estimate load and capacity by back-of-envelope arithmetic and measurementAnalysePO2, PO4
6Write architecture decision records and take part in a design review, giving and answering written criticismEvaluatePO9

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • In an unfamiliar module of 20–50 files, trace one request from entry point to database and back, and draw a correct component diagram with dependency directions, within 60 minutes.
  • Point to three concrete coupling or cohesion problems in given code and say for each which change it makes hard.
  • From a one-page requirement, produce a domain model, a relational schema and a REST contract (resources, methods, status codes, error format) that agree with one another.
  • Write a one-page ADR with context, at least two options, the decision, and consequences including one that is negative.
  • Make a small change in the lab codebase that passes its tests and a review, and explain every changed line.

2. Class pattern (assumption to confirm)

  • Theory: 3 contact hours a week, given as two 90-minute classes (Class A and Class B). That makes 28 classes in 14 teaching weeks. Week W holds Class 2W−1 and Class 2W.
  • Lab: one 2-hour session a week, 14 sessions. Where possible the lab falls after the week's two theory classes (README A3).
  • Examinations: the mid-term (weeks 1–7) is held in the mid-semester examination week, after week 7. The final (whole course, more weight on weeks 8–14, as the outline says) is held after week 14. Neither uses teaching weeks.
  • Quizzes: four Level 0 quizzes of 15 minutes, at the start of Classes 5, 11, 19 and 25. The best three count.
  • Re-split: if the department uses three 60-minute classes, keep each week's content and order. Break each 90-minute plan at its main activity, and move the formative check to the end of each 60-minute class (README A2).
  • Timing across semester 6: weeks 10–12 come late on purpose. CSE-3653 (replication and failure) and CSE-3651 (threat modelling) must have covered those ideas first. The two teachers confirm this in week 1 (section 8).

3. Theory lesson plan

Classes follow the outline's 14-week theory plan in order. Readings name a book and the topic of the chapter (README A10). "Handout" means a sheet the teacher prepares; its content is described in the entry.

Week 1 — What architecture is; drivers; trade-offs; the ADR

Class 1 — Week 1: What architecture is, and what drives it

  • CLO / PO: CLO 4 (PO3). CLO 6 (PO9) is introduced in Class 2.
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: How the course runs: theory, the lab codebase, the assessment calendar (section 6.2) and the AI-use table (README 4.1). Quizzes and examinations are at Level 0.
    • 10–30 min: What architecture is. Three parts:
      • structure: the parts of a system and how they depend on each other;
      • quality attributes: performance, availability, modifiability, security, testability, usability;
      • decisions that are costly to reverse.
        Contrast: the choice of database and the colour of a button.
    • 30–50 min: Drivers of architecture: functional requirements, quality attributes and constraints. Worked example: a mobile top-up service for a small reseller. Then a sorting exercise: students sort 12 printed statements into the three kinds.
    • 50–70 min: Think-pair-share: "Name one decision in your semester-4 studio application that would now be expensive to change. Why?" Pairs share. The teacher writes the answers on the board and groups them by quality attribute.
    • 70–82 min: Every choice is a trade-off. Two short examples:
      • a cache makes reads faster but can show stale data;
      • one database is simple but is one point of failure.
    • 82–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture with examples; sorting exercise in pairs; think-pair-share using the students' own projects.
  • Materials:
    • Richards and Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture: the opening chapters on what architecture is and on architecture characteristics.
    • Bass, Clements and Kazman, Software Architecture in Practice: the chapter on what software architecture is (reference).
    • Handout: 12 requirement statements for the sorting exercise.
  • Formative check: exit slip. Classify three new statements as functional requirement, quality attribute or constraint. For each quality attribute, name it.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): list three quality attributes that mattered in your semester-4 studio application. For each, name one place in the code where it shows. Bring your repository link to Class 2.

Class 2 — Week 1: Trade-offs and the architecture decision record

  • CLO / PO: CLO 4 (PO3); CLO 6 (PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap. Two students read out their homework. The class names the trade-off in each.
    • 10–30 min: Recording a decision: the ADR format (README template 6.1). The parts are context, options, decision and consequences, including a negative one. Why record decisions: people leave, and code shows what was done but not why. Show one good ADR and one poor ADR side by side.
    • 30–60 min: Case: the students' own semester-4 studio applications. In groups of four, choose one real decision from a member's project. Examples: "we kept sessions in the database", "we used one table for all kinds of user". Write it as an ADR, stating honestly the options the team had at the time.
    • 60–80 min: Structured critique. Two groups read out their ADR. The class checks it with four questions:
      1. Are there two real options?
      2. Is the context specific?
      3. Is there a negative consequence?
      4. Does it say what would change the decision?
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: worked example (good and poor ADR); group writing; structured critique with a checklist.
  • Materials:
    • Richards and Ford: the chapter on architecture decisions (ADRs).
    • README template 6.1.
    • Handout: the four-question ADR checklist.
  • Formative check: each student marks a third, poor ADR against the four questions and hands in the sheet.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): rewrite your group's ADR on your own, in the template, in at most one page. This is practice for the assignment.

Week 2 — Reading a codebase you did not write; tracing; the C4 model

Class 3 — Week 2: Reading a codebase: where to start

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Why reading matters. Most professional work changes code that someone else wrote, often years ago.
    • 10–35 min: Where to start:
      • the README and contribution guide;
      • build files (pom.xml or build.gradle, package.json, pyproject.toml), which show modules, dependencies and how to run;
      • entry points: main, routes and controllers, scheduled jobs, message listeners;
      • the directory layout.
        Live demonstration on the lab codebase. The teacher thinks aloud while reading.
    • 35–55 min: Tests as documentation: a test shows how a class is meant to be used. Version history as evidence: git log on a file, git log -S "text" to find when something appeared, git blame, and commit messages linked to issues. Live demonstration.
    • 55–80 min: Code-reading exercise in pairs. Use the printed directory tree, build file and two tests of one module of the lab codebase. Worksheet questions:
      1. What are the top-level modules?
      2. Where does a web request enter?
      3. Which test shows how the module handles an invalid input?
      4. Which file has changed most often in the last year? (from a printed git log summary)
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: live code reading with think-aloud; pair worksheet.
  • Materials:
    • Feathers, Working Effectively with Legacy Code: the chapters on understanding code well enough to change it.
    • Tools: Git; IntelliJ IDEA Community Edition or Visual Studio Code.
    • Handout: reading worksheet 1 (also used in lab session 1).
  • Formative check: the teacher collects worksheets from three pairs at random and reviews the answers with the class. Common errors are noted for Class 4.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): in your semester-4 repository, find the entry point of one feature and list the files a request passes through, in order.

Class 4 — Week 2: Tracing one request; the C4 model

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap of common errors from Class 3.
    • 10–35 min: Tracing one request end to end: route, controller, service, repository, SQL, and back to the response. Tools: debugger breakpoints and text search. Record the trace as a sequence sketch: boxes for objects, arrows for calls, numbered. Live demonstration on one request of the lab codebase.
    • 35–60 min: Describing what you found with the C4 model:
      • context: the system and the people and systems around it;
      • container: deployable units and databases;
      • component: the main parts inside one container.
        Two rules for this course:
      1. Every box has a repository path.
      2. Every arrow shows dependency direction and has a label.
        Worked example: the three diagrams for a small sample application.
    • 60–80 min: Pairs draw a context diagram and a container diagram of the lab codebase from the traced request. They swap with another pair, who checks each box against the printed repository listing.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check. Quiz 1 is announced for Class 5.
  • TLA: live demonstration with the debugger; worked example; pair drawing with peer checking.
  • Materials:
    • Richards and Ford: the chapter on diagramming and presenting architecture.
    • Tools: PlantUML or Mermaid.
    • Handout: C4 notation sheet with the two course rules.
  • Formative check: the teacher shows a component diagram with three planted errors (a box with no code behind it, an arrow pointing the wrong way, a missing database). Students find them in two minutes.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): draw the component diagram of your semester-4 application's main feature in PlantUML or Mermaid. It becomes part of the assignment.

Week 3 — Modularity I

Class 5 — Week 3: Quiz 1; information hiding and cohesion

  • CLO / PO: CLO 2 (PO2, PO3); Quiz 1 assesses CLO 1.
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–15 min: Quiz 1 (Level 0, 10 marks raw): reading and tracing, weeks 1–2 (sample in section 6.4).
    • 15–40 min: Information hiding. A module hides a design decision that is likely to change: a date format, the database, the payment gateway. Information leakage is the same knowledge in two places, so a change must be made twice. Two short code examples.
    • 40–60 min: Cohesion. Things that change together should live together. Strong cohesion: a class with one clear job. Weak cohesion: a "utility drawer" class of unrelated functions. Students classify three printed classes and say why.
    • 60–80 min: Think-pair-share on a printed module of about 80 lines:
      1. Which decision is not hidden?
      2. Which change would touch many files?
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: Level 0 quiz; short lecture with code examples; classification exercise; think-pair-share.
  • Materials:
    • Ousterhout, A Philosophy of Software Design: the chapters on information hiding and leakage.
    • Richards and Ford: the chapter on modularity (cohesion, coupling).
    • Handout: three classes for the cohesion exercise, and the 80-line module.
  • Formative check: one-minute paper: "Name one design decision your semester-4 code hid well, and one it leaked."
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): find two pieces of leaked knowledge in your semester-4 project. For each, say which change would have to be made in more than one place.

Class 6 — Week 3: Kinds of coupling; deep and shallow modules; interfaces; leaky abstractions

  • CLO / PO: CLO 2 (PO2, PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: the hidden and leaked decisions from the one-minute papers.
    • 10–35 min: Kinds of coupling, and how each looks in code. Each is shown as a 5-line snippet:
      • data coupling: pass only the values needed;
      • stamp coupling: pass a whole object for one field;
      • control coupling: a flag argument that changes behaviour;
      • common coupling: shared global state;
      • content coupling: reaching into another module's internals;
      • temporal coupling: calls that must happen in a set order.
    • 35–55 min: Deep and shallow modules: a deep module has a simple interface and does a lot behind it. Interfaces as contracts: inputs, outputs, errors and preconditions. Leaky abstractions: an ORM that forces you to know the SQL it writes; a "repository" that returns database cursors.
    • 55–80 min: Code-reading exercise in pairs. A printed pair of classes. Students:
      1. name the coupling;
      2. say which change it makes hard;
      3. propose a refactoring, with its benefit and its cost.
        Two pairs present.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture with snippets; code-reading exercise; pair presentations with teacher feedback.
  • Materials:
    • Ousterhout: the chapters on deep modules and on general-purpose modules.
    • Richards and Ford: the chapter on modularity.
    • Handout: six coupling snippets; the class pair.
  • Formative check: match five new snippets to five kinds of coupling. Answers are shown on the screen; students mark their own.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): in the lab codebase, find one deep module and one shallow one. Justify each in three sentences.

Week 4 — Modularity II

Class 7 — Week 4: Dependency direction, dependency inversion, cycles, stable and volatile parts

  • CLO / PO: CLO 2 (PO2, PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Quiz 1 returned with the model answer; three common errors discussed.
    • 10–35 min: Dependency direction. High-level policy should not depend on low-level detail. Dependency inversion between modules: the domain module defines an interface (for example PaymentGateway), and an infrastructure module implements it (for example MobileWalletAdapter). Worked example with a before-and-after diagram and code.
    • 35–55 min: Cycles. Why they hurt: no part can be built, tested or released alone. Three ways to break one: move a class, extract an interface, or merge the two modules. Stable and volatile parts: depend in the direction of stability, and keep volatile parts at the edge.
    • 55–75 min: Worked exercise. A printed list of four packages and their imports contains one cycle. Students break the cycle in two different ways and compare the results.
    • 75–85 min: Assignment released (section 5.1). Walk-through of the brief, the rubric, Level 2 and the usage log.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: worked example (faded: teacher does the first cycle, students the second); assignment walk-through.
  • Materials:
    • Richards and Ford: the chapters on modularity and on component-based thinking.
    • Ousterhout: the chapter on different layers having different abstractions.
    • Handout: package lists for the exercise; assignment brief.
  • Formative check: students redraw the arrows of a three-package example after dependency inversion. Their neighbour checks the drawing.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): begin the assignment. Draw the context and container diagrams.

Class 8 — Week 4: Package boundaries; evidence from tools and from Git history

  • CLO / PO: CLO 2 (PO2, PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: one student explains dependency inversion with the class-7 example.
    • 10–30 min: Package boundaries. Package by layer or by feature. Public and internal parts. What a module exports, and why a small export list helps.
    • 30–55 min: Evidence from tools, by live demonstration on the lab codebase:
      • jdeps (Java) or dependency-cruiser (TypeScript) draws the module dependency graph;
      • fan-in and fan-out;
      • SonarQube Community Build reports duplication and cycles.
    • 55–75 min: Evidence from Git history: files that change together. Demonstration with git log --name-only and a short counting script, given to students. What change coupling shows that the import graph cannot.
    • 75–85 min: Pairs read a printed dependency graph and a change-coupling table, and rank three structural problems by the harm each does.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: live tool demonstration; data-reading exercise in pairs.
  • Materials:
    • Richards and Ford: the chapter on modularity (measuring modularity).
    • Tools: jdeps or dependency-cruiser; SonarQube Community Build; Git.
    • Handout: printed graph and change-coupling table; the counting script.
  • Formative check: pairs hand in their ranked list. The teacher picks three lists to discuss at the start of Class 9.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): run the dependency tool on your semester-4 project. Keep the output for the assignment.

Week 5 — Domain modelling I

Class 9 — Week 5: Shared vocabulary; entities and value objects; aggregates and invariants

  • CLO / PO: CLO 3 (PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Three ranked lists from Class 8 discussed. Why domain modelling: the model in the code should use the client's language.
    • 10–30 min: A shared vocabulary with the client. Keep a glossary. Watch for words that clients use in both Bangla and English (for example "due", "instalment", "batch"). Write each term once, with one meaning.
    • 30–50 min: Entities and value objects:
      • an entity has an identity that lasts (Patient, Student);
      • a value object is defined by its values and does not change (Money, Address, DateRange).
        Why money should be a value object, not a double.
    • 50–75 min: Aggregates and the invariants they protect. An aggregate is a group of objects changed as one unit, through its root. Worked example: a clinic's DoctorSchedule aggregate protects "a slot cannot be booked twice". Faded example: students define the aggregate and invariant for a pharmacy order ("total quantity of a controlled medicine per order is limited").
    • 75–85 min: Formative check.
    • 85–90 min: Homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; worked example then faded example; students apply the terms to a new case.
  • Materials:
    • Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design: the chapters on analysing business domains, discovering domain knowledge, and tackling complex business logic (entities, value objects, aggregates).
    • Handout: clinic and pharmacy cases.
  • Formative check: classify eight nouns from a short case as entity, value object or not in the model. Name one invariant.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): write a glossary of ten terms for your semester-4 project's domain, one line each.

Class 10 — Week 5: Domain events; event storming; anaemic and rich models

  • CLO / PO: CLO 3 (PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: two glossaries read out; the class finds a term with two meanings.
    • 10–25 min: Domain events: something that happened that the business cares about, named in the past tense: AppointmentBooked, PaymentReceived, BatchFull.
    • 25–65 min: Event storming as a workshop, done on paper. Groups of five, with sticky notes or coloured A4 strips:
      • orange: events;
      • blue: commands;
      • yellow: actors and aggregates;
      • pink: open questions.
        Case: admission and fee payment at a coaching centre. The teacher acts as the client and answers questions. The time line is built left to right.
    • 65–80 min: Anaemic and rich models. The same rule ("a batch cannot take more than 30 students") written in a service class and in the entity. Compare in code. When an anaemic model is acceptable: simple data entry with no rules.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: workshop simulation (event storming); code comparison.
  • Materials:
    • Khononov: the chapter on EventStorming; the chapters on implementing simple and complex business logic.
    • Sticky notes or coloured paper; A3 sheets.
  • Formative check: each group names one aggregate and its invariant from its wall. The teacher challenges one.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): photograph your group's wall. Write five commands and the events each causes. This prepares lab session 8.

Week 6 — Domain modelling II

Class 11 — Week 6: Quiz 2; bounded contexts and their relationships

  • CLO / PO: CLO 3 (PO3); Quiz 2 assesses CLO 2.
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–15 min: Quiz 2 (Level 0, 10 marks raw): coupling, cohesion and dependency direction, weeks 3–5 (sample in section 6.4).
    • 15–40 min: Bounded contexts. One word can have two meanings: "customer" in sales and in support; "student" in admissions and in exams. A model is valid inside a boundary. Signs of a boundary: the language changes, a different team owns it, it changes at a different rate.
    • 40–60 min: Relationships between contexts, drawn as a context map: partnership, shared kernel, customer–supplier, conformist, anticorruption layer, open-host service with a published language. Example: a hospital's billing and laboratory contexts.
    • 60–80 min: Case discussion in groups: a university system with admissions, accounts and examinations, and an old student-records system that cannot change. Each group draws a context map and marks where an anticorruption layer is needed.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: Level 0 quiz; short lecture; case discussion in groups; peer critique.
  • Materials:
    • Khononov: the chapters on bounded contexts and on integrating bounded contexts.
    • Handout: university case.
  • Formative check: groups swap maps. Each group writes two questions about the other's map. The teacher reads one pair aloud.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): for the coaching-centre case, mark two bounded contexts and the relationship between them.

Class 12 — Week 6: From domain model to tables

  • CLO / PO: CLO 3 (PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Quiz 2 returned with the model answer.
    • 10–35 min: Aggregate and transaction boundaries. One transaction changes one aggregate. Aggregates refer to each other by ID, not by object. Mapping to tables:
      • an entity becomes a table;
      • a value object becomes columns or an embedded type;
      • a collection inside an aggregate becomes a child table.
    • 35–55 min: Object-relational mapping choices (JPA and Hibernate, Django ORM, TypeORM). What they hide: lazy loading, cascades, generated SQL. When to write SQL directly: reports, bulk updates, tuned queries.
    • 55–75 min: When the relational model should lead: reporting-heavy systems, data shared by many applications, a legacy schema you cannot change. Worked example: the coaching-centre model mapped to a schema by the teacher. Faded part: students add fee instalments.
    • 75–85 min: Formative check.
    • 85–90 min: Homework; the mid-term scope (weeks 1–7) is announced.
  • TLA: worked example then faded example; schema-model comparison.
  • Materials:
    • Khononov: the chapters on aggregate design and implementing complex business logic.
    • Handout: coaching-centre model and partial schema.
  • Formative check: given a domain model and a schema, find two places where they disagree.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): write the schema (CREATE TABLE statements) for the aggregate you defined in Class 11 homework.

Week 7 — API design

Class 13 — Week 7: Resources and HTTP semantics; idempotency; pagination and filtering; a uniform error format

  • CLO / PO: CLO 3 (PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: an API is a contract that other people build on.
    • 10–35 min: Resources and HTTP semantics:
      • resources are nouns;
      • GET is safe, PUT and DELETE are idempotent, POST is not;
      • status codes: 200, 201, 204, 400, 401, 403, 404, 409, 422, 500 — when each is right.
        Idempotency: why a retried POST can charge twice, and the idempotency key that prevents it.
    • 35–55 min: Pagination and filtering:
      • offset pagination and cursor pagination;
      • filter and sort parameters;
      • a maximum page size.
        A uniform error format: one JSON shape for every error (type, title, status, detail, field errors), in the style of the "problem details" format.
    • 55–80 min: API critique in pairs. A printed API of eight endpoints contains six faults. Pairs find each fault and write the fix. Answers are reviewed together.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; design critique in pairs; whole-class review.
  • Materials:
    • Tools: Swagger Editor (to show a contract).
    • Handout: the faulty API; a status-code table.
  • Formative check: for three described requests, write the correct status code and say whether the request is safe to retry.
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): write the endpoints for the coaching-centre case: resources, methods and status codes.

Class 14 — Week 7: Versioning and compatibility; OpenAPI; REST, RPC and GraphQL; mid-term review

  • CLO / PO: CLO 3 (PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–25 min: Versioning and backward compatibility:
      • adding a field is safe; removing or renaming one breaks clients;
      • the tolerant reader;
      • deprecation with a date;
      • a version in the path or in a header.
        Why every observable behaviour becomes someone's dependency: field order, error text, timing. Example: a client that parses an error message.
    • 25–45 min: OpenAPI as the contract. Live: write a small contract in Swagger Editor. Contract-first and code-first. Generated client types.

(a) Code reading (mid-term Q2 kind, 10 marks). A shortened listing; the real paper has two or three printed pages.

// package shop.web
public class OrderController {
    private final OrderService service = new OrderService();
    public String placeOrder(Request req) {
        Order o = service.place(req.param("customerId"), req.param("items"));
        return "{\"id\":" + o.id + ",\"total\":" + o.total + "}";
    }
}
// package shop.service
public class OrderService {
    public static boolean TEST_MODE = false;
    public Order place(String customerId, String itemsCsv) {
        Order o = new Order();
        o.customerId = customerId;
        o.items = ItemParser.parse(itemsCsv);
        for (Item i : o.items) o.total += Db.priceOf(i.code) * i.qty;
        OrderRepository.save(o);
        String email = Db.query("select email from customer where id=" + customerId);
        Mailer.send(email, "Order placed");
        return o;
    }
}
// package shop.repo
public class OrderRepository {
    public static void save(Order o) {
        if (o.total > 10000) o.total = o.total * 0.95;   // discount on large orders
        Db.insert("orders", o);
    }
}
// package shop.domain
public class Order { public int id; public String customerId; public List<Item> items; public double total; }
// package shop.util
public class Mailer {
    public static void send(String to, String text) {
        if (OrderService.TEST_MODE) return;
        SmtpClient.send(to, text);
    }
}

Questions:

  • (a) Draw the components and their dependencies, with direction (5).
  • (b) Name two coupling or cohesion problems and the change each makes hard (3).
  • (c) A new rule: customers with Bangladeshi mobile numbers get an SMS instead of an email. Where does this change go, and why (2)?

Marking notes:

  • (a) Components: web, service, repo, domain, util, plus Db and SMTP as external. Up to 3 marks for correct arrows:
    • web → service;
    • service → repo, util, domain, Db;
    • repo → Db, domain;
    • util → service.
      1 mark for marking the service ↔ util cycle. 1 mark for showing Db as a shared global used by two layers.
  • (b) Any two of the following, 1.5 marks each (0.5 for the problem, 0.5 for the evidence line, 0.5 for the change made hard):
    • the discount rule sits in the repository, so a change to pricing rules means editing persistence code;
    • the service queries the database directly with string concatenation: a layer bypass and common coupling through Db, and also an injection risk; changing the customer table breaks the service;

(b) Design choice (final Q2 kind, 12 marks).

A clinic chain has 40 branches. Reception staff book and view appointments; doctors work at more than one branch; head office wants monthly reports. Branch internet often fails for hours at a time. The development team is five people, one with operations experience. The budget is small.

Option A: one central web application, built as a modular monolith, with one database on a cloud server; branches use a browser.

Option B: microservices (appointments, patients, billing, reporting, notifications) on a container platform.

Option C: a server in every branch running the same modular application with a local database, synchronising with a central server when connected.

(a) Name the four quality attributes that matter most here, each with a reason (3). (b) Evaluate each option against them (4). (c) Choose and justify (3). (d) State one fact that would change your decision (2).

Marking notes:

  • (a) 3 marks for four of: availability at the branch during outages; consistency (no doctor double-booked across branches); operability by a team of five; cost; modifiability. Reasons must come from the case.
  • (b) 4 marks. The answer should cover:
    • B: five people cannot operate five services and their pipelines, and B does nothing for availability at the branch, so it fails on operability and cost;
    • A: simplest to build and operate, but fails at the branch during an outage;
    • C: works during an outage, but brings 40 servers to maintain, upgrades across branches, and synchronisation conflicts (one doctor booked at two branches while offline).
  • (c) 3 marks for a choice argued from (a) and (b). A or C can earn full marks. Example: A with a mobile-data backup link and a cached copy of the day's schedule; or C if outages are long and the team accepts the operating cost. B earns at most 1 mark unless the argument is exceptional.

(c) Modelling: derive aggregates and a schema (mid-term Q3 kind, 8 marks).

A coaching centre runs batches for university admission tests. A batch has a subject, a teacher, a schedule and at most 30 seats. A student enrols in a batch; the enrolment is confirmed only when the first instalment is paid. Fees are paid in up to three instalments. A student may move to another batch of the same subject if it has a free seat. Each week the office prints the students with overdue instalments.

(a) Identify the aggregates, their roots and one invariant each (4). (b) Give a relational schema with keys and the constraints that protect the invariants (4).

Marking notes:

  • (a) 4 marks. 2 marks for sensible aggregates with roots; 2 marks for invariants placed where they can be enforced. A strong answer:
    • Batch is an aggregate root that holds its seat allocations (invariant: at most 30 seats; one seat per student);
    • FeeAccount, or Enrolment with its instalments, is a second aggregate (invariants: at most three instalments; they sum to the fee);
    • "confirmed when the first instalment is paid" crosses aggregates: handled by a domain event (InstalmentPaid) or by putting the instalments inside Enrolment;
    • Student is a separate aggregate.
      Accept other designs if the invariants are protected and the choice is explained.
  • (b) 4 marks:
    • 2 for tables and keys: student, batch(capacity), enrolment(batch_id, student_id, status) with UNIQUE(batch_id, student_id), instalment(enrolment_id, number, amount, due_date, paid_at);
    • 1 for constraints that reflect invariants (CHECK (number BETWEEN 1 AND 3), the unique enrolment; capacity checked in the aggregate or by a locked count);

(d) Modelling: find the faults in an API (mid-term Q4a kind, 4 marks).

(e) Estimation (final Q3 kind, 8 marks). The numbers below are invented for the question and are given to the student.

A national school-fee system sends notifications to guardians. There are 1.2 million students; each guardian gets on average 6 notifications a month. 70% of a month's notifications go out in the first 5 days. On those days the busiest hour carries 20% of the day's notifications. Each notification record is 600 bytes and is kept for 2 years. In a test, one database server handled 900 inserts per second with p95 under 50 ms.

(a) Average notifications per second over a month (2). (b) Peak notifications per second (3). (c) Storage for 2 years (2). (d) Is one database server enough for the inserts? (1)

Answer:

  • (a) 1.2 million × 6 = 7.2 million a month. 30 days × 86,400 s = 2,592,000 s. 7.2 million ÷ 2,592,000 ≈ 2.8 per second.
  • (b) 70% of 7.2 million = 5.04 million over 5 days = 1.008 million a day. 20% in the busiest hour = 201,600 an hour. 201,600 ÷ 3,600 = 56 per second.
  • (c) 7.2 million × 24 months = 172.8 million records. × 600 bytes ≈ 104 GB before indexes. Allow more for indexes; a student who says 150–200 GB with indexes is also right.
  • (d) Peak 56 inserts per second against a measured 900: yes, with about sixteen times headroom for inserts. Note that reads and status updates add load.

Marking notes: method marks for each step even if an arithmetic slip carries forward; full marks need units.

(f) Decision records: rewrite a poor ADR (final Q6 kind, 6 marks).

ADR 3: Use microservices.

Context: We want the system to be modern and scalable.

Decision: We will build the clinic system as microservices because large companies use them.

Consequences: The system will be scalable.

(a) List four faults in this ADR (2). (b) Rewrite it for these facts: a team of five, 40 branches, about 2,000 appointments a day, a small budget (4).

Marking notes:

  • (a) 0.5 marks each, for any four of:
    • the context has no facts (team, load, requirements);
    • no options are compared;
    • the reason is an appeal to authority;
    • there is no negative consequence;
    • it does not say what would change the decision;
    • "scalable" is not measured.
  • (b) 4 marks, 1 for each of:
    1. context with the given facts and the quality attributes that matter;
    2. at least two options with their costs (for example a modular monolith, service-based, microservices);
    3. a decision with reasons tied to the context (a modular monolith is likely);
    4. at least one negative consequence (one release unit; modules can erode, so they need automated rules) and what would change the decision (for example the team grows to three teams, or one module needs separate scaling).

(g) Recall (mid-term Q1 and final Q1 kind).

  • "Name the three kinds of architecture driver and give one example of each." (2) Answer: functional requirement; quality attribute; constraint. Examples from any domain.
  • "Name four kinds of coupling." (2) Any four of: data, stamp, control, common, content, temporal.
  • "Give two characteristics each of the layered style and the microservices style." (4)

7. Support for weaker students

The intake is about 15% strong, 60% mid-level and 25% below average (README section 10 lists the measures used in all four courses). For this course, reading a large codebase in English is the main barrier. The outline's instructor notes are taken further as follows.

  1. A guided start.
    • Session 1 opens with a guided tour of the codebase.
    • Each of sessions 1–3 has a reading worksheet with numbered questions, so students know what to look for.
    • A glossary of the codebase's own terms (for example "loan product", "disbursement", "stream", "topic") is given in week 1, with a short Bangla gloss where it helps.
  2. Checkable diagrams. The rule "every box has a repository path" turns a vague drawing into something a student can check alone. It also stops the common failure the outline names: drawing the system the student thinks it should be. The peer check in session 3 practises it before it is marked.
  3. Practice before the test.
    • Practical test 1 repeats the activities of sessions 2 and 3 on a new module.
    • In week 6, a practice version is released: an older test on a different module, with its marking scheme.
    • Students who scored 0 or 1 in sessions 2 or 3 are invited to the clinic (point 4) before week 7.
  4. A code-reading clinic. One optional hour a week in weeks 2–7, with the lab instructor. The instructor reads one more module aloud with the students, thinking aloud, and the students then trace one request together. Discussion may be in Bangla.
  5. Sentence frames for English writing. For ADRs and review comments, give frames:
    • "We chose X over Y because …"
    • "The cost of this choice is …"
    • "This would change if …"
    • "This line … which makes … hard, because …"
      The writing rubric (README 5.5) marks meaning, not grammar.
  6. Pairing with care from week 8.
    • The instructor forms the pairs and does not put two weak students together.
    • Each student owns a slice and leads one ADR, so a weaker student cannot hide or be left out.
    • Check-off questions go to each member in turn.
  7. One step at a time in the project. The five milestones (sessions 8–12) break the project into weekly pieces, each checked in the session. A pair that falls behind is seen in the next session, not at the end.
  8. The microservices trap. Students who choose microservices because they sound modern must first list what must then be operated, and by whom (Class 17 exercise; outline instructor note). This is a concrete check, not a lecture.
  9. Early warning. After practical test 1 and the mid-term, any student below 40% in either gets a 30-minute meeting and a written plan of two or three tasks (README 10.5). Usual tasks: two more traced requests; one more component diagram checked by the instructor; one ADR rewritten.
  10. Adviser contact. The outline warns that a student who struggled in the semester-4 studio should expect extra hours of code reading each week. The teacher tells the student's adviser in week 4 if the student's check-offs in sessions 1–3 were all 0 or 1.
  11. The strongest students. Optional stretch work from the outline. It earns no marks beyond the rubric's top level, but it can go in a portfolio:
    • a pull request to the upstream open-source project, following its own rules;
    • a strangler-fig extraction with before-and-after dependency graphs;
    • two architecture rules in CI with a report of existing violations.
