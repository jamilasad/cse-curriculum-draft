Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Deploy a given two-service application with a database to a Kubernetes cluster from manifests they write (Deployment, Service, configuration, probes, resource requests) and reach it through an ingress.
  • Find and fix the fault in a broken deployment (wrong image, failing probe, missing configuration, insufficient resources) using events, descriptions and logs, within 30 minutes.
  • Carry out a rolling update and a rollback, and show from metrics whether the service stayed available.
  • From a dashboard and logs, say whether a service is meeting a given objective and how much error budget is left.
  • Write a one-page postmortem of a drill: impact, timeline, contributing causes, actions with owners, and no blame.

2. Class pattern (assumption to confirm)

The general pattern is in README section 3:

  • Theory: 3 contact hours a week, given as two 90-minute classes (Class A and Class B). That makes 28 classes in 14 teaching weeks.
  • Lab: one 2-hour session a week, 14 sessions.
  • Written examinations: the mid-term and final are held outside the 14 teaching weeks. The mid-term covers weeks 1–7 and is sat on campus after week 7, as the outline says.
  • Re-split: the department may re-split theory into three 60-minute classes. Keep each week's content and order. Break each plan at its main activity.

Semester-8 delivery pattern

Framework section 10 and the outline set this pattern. Students doing Industry Practice as a six-week block are off campus in weeks 1–6. Other students work three days a week for the whole semester.

How the remote classes work in weeks 1–6:

  • Live session.
    • The class runs on the university's video platform and is recorded.
    • The teacher uses screen-sharing for demonstrations.
    • Short polls and the chat check that students are following.
    • Breakout rooms of three or four students work in a shared document.
    • A "chat waterfall" works like this: everyone types an answer, then all press Enter together, so that students do not copy the first answer.
  • For students who watch the recording.
    • The recording, the slides and the breakout task are posted on the course page on the same day.
    • The online formative check stays open for 72 hours.
    • Breakout tasks can be done alone and posted to the course forum.
    • The teacher answers forum questions within two working days.
  • Bandwidth. Slides and handouts are posted as PDF before the class. A student on a weak connection can follow with the audio only.

The quiz rule (README 5.6) applies with one change for this course: the three quizzes are all in on-campus weeks, at the start of Class 13 (week 7), Class 19 (week 10) and Class 25 (week 13).

3. Theory lesson plan

Week 1 [R] — From "deployed once" to "run in production"

Delivery: live online and recorded. The formative check is an online form, ungraded, open for 72 hours.

Class 1 — Week 1: From "deployed once" to "run in production"

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO3, PO5); CLO 4 (PO4, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: How this semester works. Weeks 1–6 are remote and weeks 7–14 on campus. Where the recordings, the forum and the catch-up branches are. The AI-use table (README 4.1). The assessment calendar: no quiz or test before week 7.
    • 10–20 min: Opening poll and chat waterfall: "Your CSE-3654 application crashed at 2 a.m. Who would have noticed, and when?"
    • 20–45 min: Short lecture. What changes between "deployed once" and "run in production": real users depend on it, changes keep arriving, and failure is normal. The twelve-factor ideas that matter most here:
      • one codebase, many deploys;
      • configuration in the environment;
      • backing services as attached resources;
      • separate build, release and run stages;
      • stateless, disposable processes that start fast and shut down cleanly;
      • logs as a stream of events;
      • development and production kept alike.
        Then immutable images and declarative configuration.
    • 45–65 min: Live demonstration, screen-shared. A small service reads its configuration from a file inside the image, writes logs to a file and ignores the stop signal. The teacher shows three problems:
      • one image must be built per environment;
      • logs are lost when the container dies;
      • requests are lost on shutdown.
        Then the teacher fixes each one live: environment variables, logging to standard output, and handling SIGTERM.
    • 65–80 min: Breakout rooms. Each room gets a half-page description of a service. The room lists which twelve-factor ideas it breaks and what goes wrong in production because of each. Answers go in the shared document.
    • 80–90 min: Summary; formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; live demonstration by screen-sharing; poll and chat waterfall; breakout rooms with a shared document.
  • Materials: Burns, Beda, Hightower and Evenson, Kubernetes: Up and Running, introductory chapter (velocity, immutability, declarative configuration, self-healing systems). Newman, Building Microservices, chapter on deployment. Tool: Docker.
  • Formative check: Five online questions. Example: "A service needs one database password in staging and another in production. Where should the password come from, and why not from the image?"
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Read the introductory chapter of Kubernetes: Up and Running. For your own CSE-3654 application, write down two twelve-factor ideas it breaks and how you would fix each.

Class 2 — Week 1: Site reliability engineering; reliability as a feature with a cost

  • CLO / PO: CLO 4 (PO4, PO5); CLO 1 (PO3, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap poll on Class 1 (three questions).
    • 10–35 min: Short lecture. What site reliability engineering (SRE) is: software engineering applied to operations work. Toil, and why SRE teams cap it. How SRE relates to traditional operations and to DevOps. DevOps is a set of practices and a culture; SRE is one concrete way of carrying them out. Who carries the pager.
    • 35–55 min: Worked example. Availability "nines" as arithmetic: 99%, 99.9% and 99.99% over 30 days allow 432, 43.2 and 4.32 minutes of failure. The teacher then makes three points:
      • each extra nine costs more engineering time and money;
      • users cannot see reliability that is better than their own phone network;
      • the error budget is the amount of unreliability the business accepts (taught fully in week 10).
    • 55–75 min: Breakout case. Three services: a university result portal, a mobile-wallet top-up service and an internal leave-application system. For each, choose a target availability and give one reason from user impact and one from cost.
    • 75–85 min: Rooms report in the chat. The teacher compares the answers and corrects the arithmetic.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; worked arithmetic on a shared screen; breakout case; report-back in the chat.
  • Materials: Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy (eds.), Site Reliability Engineering: introduction, "Embracing Risk" and "Eliminating Toil". Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate: the argument that speed and stability go together (optional).
  • Formative check: Online: "How many minutes of failure does 99.5% allow in 30 days? In one week?" (216 minutes; 50.4 minutes.)
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Read "Embracing Risk". Write 150 words: which availability target would you set for your capstone system, and why?

Week 2 [R] — Containers in depth

Class 3 — Week 2: How containers work: namespaces, control groups, layered file systems

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO3, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Poll: "A container is (a) a small virtual machine, (b) an ordinary process with limits and a private view, (c) a package format." Discuss the answers.
    • 10–35 min: Short lecture, linked to CSE-3541. A container is a process with:
      • private views of the system, made with namespaces: process IDs, mounts, network, host name, inter-process communication and users;
      • limits enforced by control groups: CPU, memory and number of processes;
      • a layered file system: read-only image layers with a thin writable layer on top.
        Why containers start fast. What they do not isolate: all containers share one kernel.
    • 35–60 min: Live demonstration in a Linux virtual machine, screen-shared. (On macOS and Windows, Docker runs inside a hidden virtual machine, so the host cannot see the container's process.)
      • Run a container and find its process from the host with ps.
      • Read its namespaces under /proc/<pid>/ns.
      • Run it with --memory 64m and a memory-hungry program. It is killed with exit code 137.
      • Show the writable layer with docker diff.
      • Create a process-ID namespace by hand with unshare --pid --fork --mount-proc.
    • 60–75 min: Predict, then check. The teacher posts five commands. Rooms write the expected output in the shared document before the teacher runs them.
    • 75–85 min: Discussion: what this means for security (root inside a container) and for Kubernetes resource limits (next week).
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: poll; short lecture; live demonstration; predict-then-check in breakout rooms.
  • Materials: Kubernetes: Up and Running, chapter on creating and running containers; or Lukša, Kubernetes in Action, chapter introducing containers. Tools: Docker Engine or Podman.
  • Formative check: Online: "A container started with --memory 64m exits with code 137 after using 70 MB. What happened, and which kernel feature did it?"
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Run the demonstration commands in the course's Linux virtual machine image. Write down one output that surprised you and explain it.

Class 4 — Week 2: Building good images; registries, tags and digests

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO3, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: three questions in the chat.
    • 10–30 min: Short lecture:
      • image layers and the build cache, and why the order of instructions matters;
      • the .dockerignore file;
      • multi-stage builds: a build stage with compilers and test tools, and a small runtime stage;
      • small base images;
      • reproducible builds: pinned versions and lock files.
    • 30–50 min: Live coding. Start from a poor Dockerfile (like sample question K2 in section 6.4). Improve it one step at a time. After each step, measure the image size and the rebuild time after a one-line code change.
    • 50–60 min: Running as non-root: the USER instruction, file ownership, and what an attacker gains from root in a container.
    • 60–75 min: Registries. Tags can move; digests cannot. The latest problem. Pinning by digest in production manifests. The OCI standards for image format, runtime and distribution, and why one image runs under Docker, Podman or containerd.
    • 75–85 min: Breakout: review a second Dockerfile in the shared document. Write comments in the format of README template 6.3.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; live coding with measurements; peer review in breakout rooms.
  • Materials: Kubernetes: Up and Running, chapter on creating and running containers (image layering, multi-stage builds, registries). Tools: Docker Engine or Podman; Trivy (shown, used in week 12).
  • Formative check: Online: "Your manifest uses orders:1.4. Someone pushes a different image under the same tag. What happens the next time a pod restarts? How do you prevent it?"
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Improve one Dockerfile from your CSE-3654 project. Record the size before and after. (This prepares lab session 1.)

Week 3 [R] — Kubernetes architecture and workloads

Class 5 — Week 3: The control plane, desired state and workload objects

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO3, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Poll on images and digests (from Class 4).
    • 10–35 min: Short lecture with a diagram:
      • the control plane: API server, etcd, scheduler and controller manager;
      • the nodes: kubelet, container runtime and kube-proxy;
      • every change goes through the API server;
      • desired state and the reconciliation loop: observe, compare, act.
    • 35–55 min: Live demonstration on a local k3d cluster:
      • apply a Deployment, then delete a pod and watch a new one appear (kubectl get pods -w);
      • scale the Deployment;
      • show the ReplicaSet;
      • compare spec (what we asked for) with status (what exists).
    • 55–75 min: Short lecture, then a breakout task. Workload objects:
      • Pod, and why we rarely create one directly;
      • Deployment, for stateless services;
      • StatefulSet: stable names, a volume per replica, ordered start;
      • Job and CronJob: run to completion.
        The rooms match five workloads to object types with one reason each: a web API, a PostgreSQL replica set, a nightly report, a database migration and a queue worker.
    • 75–85 min: Rooms report; the teacher corrects common mistakes.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture with a diagram; live demonstration; matching task in breakout rooms.
  • Materials: Kubernetes: Up and Running, chapters on Pods, ReplicaSets, Deployments, Jobs and StatefulSets (or the matching chapters of Kubernetes in Action). Kubernetes official documentation: workloads and controllers. Tools: k3d, kubectl.
  • Formative check: Online: "You delete one pod of a Deployment that has three replicas. List what happens, in order, and which component does each step."
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Write a Deployment manifest for one service of your CSE-3654 project. Do not apply it yet; you will compare it with your lab work.

Class 6 — Week 3: Probes, resources, scheduling and reading events

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO3, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap in the chat: "What does the reconciliation loop do when a node dies?"
    • 10–30 min: Probes:
      • liveness: restart the container;
      • readiness: take the pod out of the Service's endpoints;
      • startup: give a slow-starting service time.
        Common mistakes: a liveness probe that checks the database, so a database problem restarts every pod; using one endpoint for both probes.
    • 30–45 min: Resource requests and limits. Requests decide scheduling. Limits are enforced by control groups: CPU is throttled, memory over the limit is killed (OOMKilled). Quality-of-service classes in outline.
    • 45–55 min: Scheduling. How the scheduler picks a node. Why a pod stays Pending. Node affinity and taints in outline only.
    • 55–80 min: Debugging walk-through with printed outputs on the shared screen. Five cases: ImagePullBackOff, CrashLoopBackOff, Pending (insufficient CPU), OOMKilled, and Running but not Ready. For each case: which command shows the evidence (get, describe, logs --previous, get events --sort-by=.lastTimestamp) and which line proves the cause. Each breakout room then diagnoses two more outputs and posts the answer.
    • 80–85 min: The debugging order to learn by heart: get → describe (events) → logs → logs of the previous container → exec.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; worked diagnosis of real outputs; diagnosis in breakout rooms.
  • Materials: Kubernetes: Up and Running (Pods chapter: health checks and resource management). Kubernetes official documentation: probes; resource management for pods and containers. Tool: kubectl.
  • Formative check: Online: "A pod shows OOMKilled and has a memory limit of 256Mi. Give two possible fixes, and say how you would choose between them."
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Make your own one-page debugging checklist from today's five cases. It is for practice only. In practical tests only the reference sheet issued by the instructor is allowed (README 5.2).

Week 4 [R] — Kubernetes networking, configuration and storage

Class 7 — Week 4: Services and DNS, ingress and the Gateway API, network policy

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO3, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap poll on probes and requests.
    • 10–35 min: Services:
      • a stable virtual IP address and a DNS name;
      • selectors and endpoints;
      • the ClusterIP, NodePort and LoadBalancer types;
      • DNS names of the form name.namespace.svc.cluster.local;
      • how traffic reaches a pod, in outline.
    • 35–50 min: Ingress and the Gateway API. Routing HTTP traffic from outside by host name and path. The Gateway API as the newer model with separate roles (GatewayClass, Gateway, HTTPRoute). An ingress or gateway needs a controller running in the cluster.
    • 50–65 min: Network policy. By default every pod can reach every pod. The usual pattern is to deny all, then allow the paths you need. Selection is by pod labels and namespaces. A policy only works if the cluster's network plugin enforces it.
    • 65–80 min: Breakout task. Draw the path of one browser request from the internet to the orders pod and on to PostgreSQL. Then write, in words or YAML, the smallest set of network policies that allows only web → orders → database.
    • 80–90 min: Rooms post their drawings; formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; drawing and policy-writing task in breakout rooms.
  • Materials: Kubernetes: Up and Running, chapters on service discovery and on HTTP load balancing with Ingress. Kubernetes official documentation: Services, Ingress, Gateway API, Network Policies.
  • Formative check: Online: "A Service has no endpoints. Give two possible causes and the command that shows each."
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Write a default-deny policy and one allow policy for your capstone system's namespace.

Class 8 — Week 4: ConfigMaps and Secrets, volumes, where the database lives, Helm and Kustomize

  • CLO / PO: CLO 1 (PO3, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: one question on Services, answered in a chat waterfall.
    • 10–30 min: ConfigMaps and Secrets, used as environment variables or as files. A Secret is only base64-encoded, not encrypted, unless the cluster is set up for encryption. Who can read Secrets (preview of RBAC in week 14). Why Secrets must stay out of Git.
    • 30–50 min: Volumes, PersistentVolumeClaims, StorageClasses and dynamic provisioning. What happens to data when a pod moves to another node. Volume claims in a StatefulSet.
    • 50–65 min: Short structured debate in breakout rooms: should the database run inside the cluster? Three options: a managed database, a database operator, or a plain StatefulSet. Compare backups, upgrades, failover and the skills the team needs. Vote in the chat. Note: in this course's lab the database runs inside the cluster, because there is no budget for a managed one.
    • 65–85 min: Packaging. Helm: charts, values, templates, releases. Kustomize: a base plus overlays and patches. Live demonstration: the same application with a development overlay and a staging overlay that differ in replicas and configuration. Then helm template to show what a chart really produces.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; structured debate and vote; live demonstration.
  • Materials: Kubernetes: Up and Running, chapters on ConfigMaps and Secrets, storage, and Helm or Kustomize where the edition covers them. Kubernetes official documentation: ConfigMaps, Secrets, Persistent Volumes, Kustomize. Tools: Helm, Kustomize.
  • Formative check: Online: "A classmate committed a Secret manifest with a real password to Git. Is it safe because it is base64-encoded? List what you do now." (Expected: it is not safe; rotate the password; remove it from the repository; check who had access.)
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Make development and staging overlays for one of your services with Kustomize.

Week 5 [R] — Service-to-service communication I: synchronous

Class 9 — Week 5: REST and gRPC, service discovery, timeouts and deadlines

  • CLO / PO: CLO 2 (PO2, PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Poll: "How many outbound network calls does one 'place order' request make in your capstone system?"
    • 10–30 min: REST compared with gRPC:
      • HTTP and JSON against HTTP/2 and Protocol Buffers;
      • how each states its contract;
      • streaming;
      • browser support;
      • when to use each: a public API against internal calls at high volume.
    • 30–45 min: Service discovery and load balancing through DNS and Services. Client-side and server-side balancing. Why long-lived gRPC connections can load replicas unevenly.
    • 45–70 min: Worked example on the shared screen: timeouts and deadlines.
      • Every remote call needs a timeout.
      • A timeout is chosen from the dependency's measured 99th-percentile latency, not guessed.
      • A deadline is passed down the chain, so each call knows how much time is left.
      • Example chain: user → web (3 s budget) → orders → inventory. Set each timeout so that an inner call always gives up before the outer one.
    • 70–85 min: Breakout task. Given a call graph with the median and 99th-percentile latency of each hop, set a timeout for each hop and justify each in one line.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: poll; short lecture; worked example; timeout-setting task in breakout rooms.
  • Materials: Nygard, Release It!, chapters on stability antipatterns (integration points) and stability patterns (timeouts). Newman, Building Microservices, chapters on communication styles and on resiliency.
  • Formative check: Online: "inventory has a 99th-percentile latency of 300 ms. orders has 2 s in total from its caller and must call inventory and then payments. What timeout do you set for inventory, and why?"
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): List every outbound call in your capstone or CSE-3654 service and its timeout. Write "none" where there is none.

Class 10 — Week 5: Retries, retry storms, circuit breakers and idempotency keys

  • CLO / PO: CLO 2 (PO2, PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: one timeout question in a chat waterfall.
    • 10–30 min: Retries:
      • retry only transient faults, and only idempotent operations;
      • exponential backoff;
      • jitter, and why retries without it arrive together in spikes;
      • retry budgets.
    • 30–50 min: Worked example on the shared screen: retry storms. Retries at several layers multiply. Three layers, each making three attempts, give 27 calls to the last service. Combine this with a dependency that is already slow and work out the load.
    • 50–65 min: Circuit breakers: the closed, open and half-open states; failing fast; a fallback answer. Bulkheads in outline.
    • 65–80 min: Live demonstration with curl: an idempotency key. The client sends a key with POST /orders. The server stores the result under that key, so a repeated request returns the stored result and creates nothing new. A unique constraint in the database is the last line of defence.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; worked arithmetic; live demonstration.
  • Materials: Release It!, chapters on stability patterns (circuit breaker, bulkheads, fail fast) and stability antipatterns (cascading failures). Building Microservices, chapter on resiliency.
  • Formative check: Online: "Two layers each retry twice (three attempts in all). The last service starts timing out. How many calls does it receive for each user request?" (9.)
  • Homework (Level 2, unmarked): Design idempotency for one state-changing endpoint of your capstone system: where the key comes from, where it is stored, and for how long.

Week 6 [R] — Service-to-service communication II: asynchronous; the service mesh

Class 11 — Week 6: Queues and logs, delivery guarantees, ordering, duplicates, dead-letter queues

  • CLO / PO: CLO 2 (PO2, PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Poll: "Which work in your capstone system does not need to finish before the user gets an answer?"
    • 10–30 min: Why asynchronous communication: decoupling in time, smoothing load, taking slow work off the request path. A queue (RabbitMQ) deletes a message once it is acknowledged. A log keeps messages, and each consumer keeps its own position in it (NATS with JetStream).
    • 30–55 min: Delivery guarantees:
      • at-most-once;
      • at-least-once;

4. Lab lesson plan

4.1 How the lab runs

These rules come from the outline:

  • The environment travels with the student. Every exercise runs from the course repository on a local Kubernetes cluster on a laptop with 8 GB of memory. The outline allows kind or k3d. This plan assumes k3d because:
    • it runs k3s, the same distribution as the department cluster;
    • k3s includes an ingress controller, a storage provisioner, a metrics server (needed for autoscaling in week 12) and a network-policy controller, so there is less to install.
      Students without such a laptop use lab machines on campus days, or a namespace on the department cluster (k3s on three or four lab PCs), reached remotely.
  • Each week has three parts:
    • about one hour of preparation in the student's own time: reading, pulling images, and the first part of the exercise;
    • the two scheduled hours, kept for what needs a teacher: check-off, fault-finding, drills and tests;
    • submission by pull request.
  • Weeks 1–6 [R] can be done fully off campus. The session runs in the lab and by video call at the same time. An off-campus student is checked off from their pull request and a five-minute demonstration: live on the video call, or recorded and linked in the pull request.
  • Weeks 7–14 [C] need attendance.
  • No paid cloud account and no bank card are needed. Infrastructure as code is practised against the local cluster. Where the department has education credits, an instructor-controlled account with budgets and automatic teardown is used. Otherwise vendor services are shown by demonstration.
  • Industry Practice option. With the host company's written permission, a student may base the week-10 objectives on a service at their workplace, with identifying details removed.

The reference application. The lab instructor prepares it before the semester (section 8) and keeps it in the course repository. It has:

  • three services: web (front end), orders (API) and inventory (API called by orders), so that there is a call chain to break in week 4;
  • a PostgreSQL database;
  • from week 5, a RabbitMQ or NATS broker and a worker.

Catch-up branch. Every week the instructor publishes a branch catchup/week-NN that contains a working solution to the previous session. A student who failed last week's lab starts this week's lab from that branch, so no student is blocked.

AI use in every session.

  • Lab work and the project are at Level 2: keep the usage log (README 6.2).
  • Practical tests (weeks 8 and 13) and the viva (week 14) are at Level 0.
  • The checking habit taught every week, from the outline:
    • say what a command will show before you run it;
    • read the plan or the diff before you apply it;
    • try a change in a scratch namespace first;
    • never paste a secret, token or kubeconfig into an AI tool;
    • give an assistant or agent read-only credentials unless a person approves the change.

Check-off. Each regular session is checked by README 5.1 (score 0, 1 or 2). In this course the regular sessions are 1–7, 9, 10 and 12: ten sessions, 20 marks, with no scaling needed. Session 11, the game day, is assessed through the postmortem in the project (section 5.2) and its timeline in the lab record. Attendance at the game day is required.

Session 1 — Week 1: Local cluster, multi-stage Dockerfiles, non-root images, registry [R]

  • Aims: Set up the environment used all semester. Build small, safe images for the three services of the reference application.
  • Before the session:
    • Install Docker Engine (or Podman), k3d and kubectl, following the course set-up sheet.
    • Clone the course repository.
    • Pull the images listed on the sheet (base images for the three services, the k3s node image, the local registry image) while on good Wi-Fi.
    • Read the Dockerfile section of Kubernetes: Up and Running.
  • Activities (120 min):
    1. 0–10 min: Briefing in the lab and on the video call. Today's aim, the check-off question, and the AI rule (Level 2, log it).
    2. 10–30 min: Create the cluster with a local registry. Check it with kubectl get nodes. Off-campus students post a screenshot in the session channel; the instructor helps anyone stuck.
    3. 30–70 min: Write a multi-stage Dockerfile for each of the three services. Copy the dependency files before the source code so that the build cache works. Add a .dockerignore.
    4. 70–90 min: Run each image as non-root and prove it with docker run --rm <image> id. Make a table of image sizes: the given single-stage image against yours.
    5. 90–105 min: Tag each image with a version, push it to the local registry, and record its digest.
    6. 105–120 min: Check-off. On campus: at the student's machine. Off campus: from the pull request, plus a five-minute demonstration on the video call or a recording linked in the pull request.
  • Deliverable: Three Dockerfiles; image-size table; image digests in lab-record.md.
  • AI-use level: 2. An assistant may suggest Dockerfile lines. The student must explain each line at check-off.
  • How it is checked: README 5.1 check-off. Score 2 if the images build, run as non-root and the size table is complete, and the student answers: "Why does copying the dependency file before the source code make rebuilds faster?"

Session 2 — Week 2: Hand-written manifests; repairing planted faults [R]

  • Aims: Deploy the reference application from manifests the student writes. Learn the debugging order on real faults.
  • Before the session: Read the Pods and Deployments chapters of Kubernetes: Up and Running. Write a first draft of the orders Deployment.
  • Activities (120 min):
    1. 0–15 min: Briefing. Probes and resource requests are taught in theory Class 6, which may come after this session, so the instructor explains them here in 10 minutes with one example manifest.
    2. 15–55 min: Write and apply manifests for the three services and the database: Deployments, Services, a ConfigMap, a Secret, readiness and liveness probes, and resource requests. Reach web with kubectl port-forward.
    3. 55–60 min: Commit and push.
    4. 60–100 min: Run ./faults.sh week2. The script plants three faults: a bad image tag, a failing probe and a missing configuration key. For each fault, keep a fault diary: symptom, command run, the line of output that is the evidence, and the fix. Before running each command, write what you expect it to show.
    5. 100–120 min: Check-off (on campus, or pull request plus demonstration for off-campus students).
  • Deliverable: Manifests in the repository; fault diary in lab-record.md.
  • AI-use level: 2. Diagnose the planted faults without AI first; practical test 1 is at Level 0. Log any AI use.
  • How it is checked: Check-off. Score 2 if the application runs, all three faults are repaired and the diary has evidence lines, and the student answers: "What does kubectl describe show that kubectl get does not?"

Session 3 — Week 3: Ingress, persistent storage, migration Job, network policy, two environments [R]

  • Aims: Make the application reachable from outside, keep its data, and restrict its network paths. Package it for two environments.
  • Before the session: Read the storage and Ingress chapters of Kubernetes: Up and Running. Pull the images listed on the sheet.
  • Activities (120 min):
    1. 0–10 min: Briefing. Services, ingress, network policy, storage and packaging are taught in theory week 4, so the instructor gives a 10-minute overview with the target diagram.
    2. 10–30 min: Route a host name to web through the ingress controller that comes with k3s. Test with curl.
    3. 30–50 min: Give PostgreSQL a PersistentVolumeClaim. Write a row, delete the pod, and show the row is still there.
    4. 50–65 min: Run the schema migration as a Kubernetes Job before orders starts.
    5. 65–85 min: Network policy. Deny all, then allow web → orders → inventory and orders → database. Test with a temporary pod (kubectl run --rm -it) that must be refused, and one path that must work. Save the output.
    6. 85–105 min: Package the application for development and staging with Kustomize overlays (or Helm values): different replicas and configuration.
    7. 105–120 min: Check-off.

  • AI-use level: 2.
  • How it is checked: Check-off. Score 2 if data survives a pod restart, the policy test shows both a refusal and a success, and both overlays render. Question: "Your network policy is applied but nothing is blocked. What do you check first?"

Session 4 — Week 4: Failure between services: timeouts, retries, idempotency, retry storms [R]

  • Aims: See a slow dependency break a service. Fix it with timeouts, careful retries and an idempotency key. Measure before and after.
  • Before the session: Read the timeouts and circuit-breaker sections of Release It!. Pull the Toxiproxy and k6 images.
  • Activities (120 min):
    1. 0–10 min: Briefing. Timeouts and retries are taught in theory week 5, so the instructor shows the retry arithmetic on one slide.
    2. 10–25 min: Put Toxiproxy between orders and inventory. Take a k6 baseline: throughput, 95th-percentile latency and error rate.
    3. 25–45 min: Inject 2 seconds of latency. Watch requests hang, because no timeout is set. Add timeouts and measure again.
    4. 45–70 min: Add retries with exponential backoff and jitter to the inventory call. Add an idempotency key to POST /orders. Send the same request twice and show that only one order is created.
    5. 70–95 min: Reproduce a retry storm: switch on retries in both web and orders, inject errors, and count the calls that inventory receives (from its request counter). Then remove the storm: retry at one layer only, with backoff.
    6. 95–105 min: Fill in the measurement table: before, with timeouts, with retries, storm, after.
    7. 105–120 min: Check-off.
  • Deliverable: Measurement table with k6 summaries; code changes in a pull request.
  • AI-use level: 2.
  • How it is checked: Check-off. Score 2 if the table shows the storm and its removal with numbers, and the duplicate request creates one order. Question: "Why must the retry not be at both layers?"

Session 5 — Week 5: Queue and worker, dead-letter handling, crash during work [R]

  • Aims: Take a slow operation off the request path. Show that a crash causes no lost and no duplicated effect.
  • Before the session: Read the broker's getting-started guide (RabbitMQ or NATS, as chosen). Pull the broker image.
  • Activities (120 min):
    1. 0–10 min: Briefing. Delivery guarantees are taught in theory week 6, so the instructor explains at-least-once delivery and acknowledgement with one diagram.
    2. 10–25 min: Deploy the broker from the course manifests.
    3. 25–55 min: orders publishes a "send receipt" message instead of sending the receipt itself. The worker consumes the message, records the effect, and acknowledges only after the effect is recorded.
    4. 55–75 min: Dead-letter handling. Limit the delivery attempts and send failing messages to a dead-letter queue. Test it with a malformed message.
    5. 75–100 min: Kill the worker mid-task with kubectl delete pod while messages are being processed. Show from the database that no receipt is lost and none is recorded twice. The consumer remembers processed message IDs.
    6. 100–110 min: Record a screen video of the demonstration, at most five minutes. Off-campus students link it in the pull request.
    7. 110–120 min: Check-off.
  • Deliverable: Recorded demonstration; short note (half a page): which guarantee the design gives and why.
  • AI-use level: 2.
  • How it is checked: Check-off. Score 2 if the recording shows the kill and the database check, and the note names the guarantee correctly. Question: "At which line of your worker would a crash cause a duplicate, if you had not stored processed IDs?"

Session 6 — Week 6: Infrastructure as code with OpenTofu; GitOps with Argo CD [R]

Session 7 — Week 7: Releases under load; rollback; blue–green or canary; expand and contract [C]

K3. A chain of services under failure (8 marks, CLO 2, Analyse)

Users → A → B → C. Each user request makes one call from A to B, and each B request makes one call from B to C.

  • A calls B with a 2-second timeout and up to 3 retries (4 attempts).
  • B calls C with a 2-second timeout and up to 3 retries (4 attempts).
  • Normal load is 50 user requests per second.
  • C becomes slow: every call to C now takes 5 seconds.

(a) How many calls per second now reach C? Show your working. (3) (b) How long does a user wait before A gives up? Does B stop working on a request when A gives up? What is the effect? (1) (c) Redesign the timeouts and retries. (2) (d) Separately: B writes an order to its database and then publishes an "order placed" message. The process crashes between the two steps. What goes wrong, and how do you fix it? (2)

Answer and marking notes.

  • (a) Attempts are retries plus one, so 4 at each layer (1). Every attempt times out, so each user request causes 4 × 4 = 16 calls to C (1). 50 × 16 = 800 calls per second, sixteen times the normal load on a service that is already slow (1).
  • (b) About 8 seconds or more: four attempts of 2 seconds each, plus any backoff. B does not know that A has given up, so it keeps calling C for up to 8 seconds per attempt. This is wasted work that keeps C overloaded and prevents its recovery (1).
  • (c) Any two of these, 1 mark each with a reason:
    • retry at one layer only;
    • exponential backoff with jitter;
    • a retry budget (for example, retries at most 10% of requests);
    • inner timeouts shorter than outer ones, with the deadline passed down so that B stops when A's time is up;
    • a circuit breaker on B → C so that it fails fast.
  • (d) The dual-write problem: the order exists but no message is sent, so later steps never happen (1). The fix is the outbox: write the event to an outbox table in the same transaction, have a relay publish it, and make consumers ignore duplicates by event ID (1).

K4. Objective, error budget and a release decision (8 marks, CLO 4, Evaluate)

The checkout service has this objective: 99.5% of valid requests in a rolling 30 days succeed (no 5xx status) and complete within 400 ms. The error-budget policy says: if less than 25% of the budget is left, only fixes and reliability work may be released.

Figures for the last 30 days:

  • 12,000,000 valid requests;
  • 21,000 returned a 5xx status;
  • 27,000 others took longer than 400 ms;
  • in the last 7 days alone, 30,000 requests were bad.

(a) Compute the indicator over 30 days. (2) (b) Compute the error budget in requests, how much is used and how much is left. (3) (c) Compute the burn rate over the last 7 days. (1) (d) The team wants to release a new feature tomorrow. Decide and justify. (2)

Answer and marking notes.

  • (a) Bad requests = 21,000 + 27,000 = 48,000. Good = 11,952,000. Indicator = 11,952,000 ÷ 12,000,000 = 99.6% (2; 1 if the slow requests are left out).
  • (b) Budget = 0.5% × 12,000,000 = 60,000 bad requests (1). Used: 48,000, which is 80% (1). Left: 12,000, which is 20% (1).
  • (c) At an even pace, 7 days may use 60,000 × 7 ÷ 30 = 14,000. 30,000 ÷ 14,000 ≈ 2.1 (1).
  • (d) Do not release the feature. 20% is below the policy's 25% (1). At the recent pace (about 4,300 bad requests a day) the remaining 12,000 last under three days. Fix the cause of the recent bad requests first. If the business insists, escalate as the policy says and release by canary (1).

K6. A release that changes a table used by the running version (6 marks, CLO 3, Apply 4 and Understand 2)

Version 1 of the customers service reads and writes the column phone. Version 2 must use a new column mobile_number that holds the same number with the +880 country code. The service runs 4 replicas with rolling updates and must stay available.

(a) Write the release steps. For each step, say how you would roll back. (4) (b) Explain why you cannot simply rename the column in one release. (2)

Answer and marking notes.

  • (a) The steps, with rollback:
    1. A migration adds a nullable mobile_number column. Rollback: drop it, since no code uses it yet.
    2. Release v1.1, which writes both columns and still reads phone. Rollback: back to v1, which still works.
    3. A backfill Job converts old rows in batches. It is idempotent and can be re-run.
    4. Release v2, which reads mobile_number and still writes both. Rollback: back to v1.1, which is safe.
    5. Release v2.1, which stops writing phone. From here a rollback to v1.x is unsafe, because phone grows stale. So this step waits until the team is confident.
    6. A later migration drops phone, after the rollback window. Its only rollback is a restore from backup, which is why it comes last.

Marks: expand first (1); dual write and backfill (1); switch the reads, with the contract step last (1); a rollback stated for each step (1).

  • (b) During a rolling update, old and new replicas run at the same time. An old replica would fail on the renamed column, and a rollback would be impossible (2).

K7. From an incident timeline to causes and actions (8 marks, CLO 5, Evaluate)

A fictional timeline. Times are in Bangladesh time (UTC+6).

  • 14:02 Engineer A deploys v3.8 of payments-api. The change reduces the database connection pool from 50 to 5 "to save memory". One reviewer approved it with the comment "looks fine".
  • 14:10 The 99th-percentile latency rises from 300 ms to 4 s. No alert fires: the only alerts are on CPU and pod restarts.
  • 14:40 The client's support team in New York emails about failed payments. The email is seen at 14:55.
  • 14:58 On-call engineer B is phoned and restarts all pods. Nothing improves.
  • 15:20 Engineer C reads the logs, sees "timeout waiting for connection from pool", and rolls back to v3.7.
  • 15:28 Latency is normal again. The first status update reaches the client at 15:45.

The draft postmortem says: "Root cause: Engineer A changed the pool size carelessly. Action: Engineer A must be more careful in future."

(a) Write at least four contributing causes, without blame. (4) (b) Write three actions. Give each a type (prevent, detect or mitigate), an owner role and a due date. (3) (c) Rewrite the draft's "root cause" sentence in blameless form. (1)

Marking notes.

  • (a) 1 mark each, up to 4. Any cause containing blame words ("careless", "should have known") earns 0. Acceptable causes include:
    • the configuration change was not tested under realistic load;
    • the review checklist does not cover configuration values;
    • there was no alert on the user-facing objective, so users were harmed for 38 minutes before anyone acted;
    • detection depended on a client email across time zones;
    • pods were restarted before anyone asked "what changed?", because there was no runbook;
    • the release was not progressive, so all traffic met the fault at once;
    • pool saturation was not on any dashboard;
    • there was no rule for updating the client.
  • (b) 1 mark each for an action with its type, owner role and date; 0.5 if the owner or date is missing. Examples:
    • a burn-rate alert on the payments latency objective (detect; SRE lead; two weeks);
    • canary releases for payments-api with an automatic abort on the error rate (prevent; team lead; one month);
    • a runbook whose first step is "check recent deployments; roll back first" (mitigate; on-call owner; one week);

K8. Capacity and cost (7 marks, CLO 6, Evaluate)

Example prices, invented for this question:

  • A node with 4 vCPU and 16 GB costs USD 0.20 an hour on demand. One month is 730 hours.
  • A managed PostgreSQL instance (2 vCPU, 8 GB, with a standby) costs USD 0.25 an hour, plus USD 0.15 per GB-month of storage.

Load-test result: one api pod, requesting 0.5 vCPU and 1 GB, serves 150 requests per second at the latency objective. The forecast peak is 1,200 requests per second. Each node keeps 0.5 vCPU and 2 GB for the system. The service must survive the loss of one node at peak.

(a) How many api pods are needed at peak, with 30% headroom? (2) (b) How many nodes? Show your working. (2) (c) What is the monthly cost of the nodes? (1) (d) The database needs 100 GB. Compare the managed database with running PostgreSQL yourself in the cluster, and recommend one for a team of four engineers. (2)

Answer and marking notes.

  • (a) 1,200 ÷ 150 = 8 pods (1). With 30% headroom: 8 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 11.4, so 12 pods (1).
  • (b) The pods need 12 × 0.5 = 6 vCPU and 12 GB. Each node offers 3.5 vCPU and 14 GB, so CPU decides: 6 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 1.7, so 2 nodes (1). To survive the loss of one node: 3 nodes (1).
  • (c) 3 × 0.20 × 730 = USD 438 a month (1).
  • (d) Managed: 0.25 × 730 = USD 182.50, plus 100 × 0.15 = USD 15, so USD 197.50 a month, with standby, backups and patching done by the provider (1). Self-hosted needs node capacity (perhaps one more node, USD 146) and storage, plus engineer time for backups, upgrades, failover and restore drills. For four engineers with no database specialist, the risk of losing data outweighs the saving. Recommend the managed database, unless cost dominates or a data-residency rule requires otherwise. A reasoned comparison that includes engineer time and risk, with a recommendation, earns the mark (1).

K9. Recall (mid-term Q1, 5 marks; final Q1, 6 marks; Remember)

Mid-term examples (1 mark each):

  1. What does a StatefulSet give that a Deployment does not?
  2. What is the difference between an image tag and an image digest?
  3. Give one difference between a ConfigMap and a Secret.
  4. What happens to a pod when its readiness probe fails? What does not happen?
  5. State two of the twelve-factor ideas.

Final examples:

  1. Define service-level indicator, objective and agreement. (2 marks; CLO 4)
  2. Define recovery point objective and recovery time objective. (2; CLO 6)
  3. State the role of a Job and of a Service. (2; CLO 1)

Answer notes.

  1. Stable names, ordered start and a volume claim for each replica.
  2. A tag is a name that can be moved to another image; a digest is a hash of the content and never changes.
  3. A Secret is meant for sensitive values and is handled more carefully (RBAC, optional encryption at rest); both supply configuration as environment variables or files.
  4. The pod is removed from the Service endpoints. It is not restarted.
  5. Any two, for example configuration in the environment, disposable processes, logs as event streams.
  6. Indicator: a measured ratio of good events to valid events. Objective: a target for the indicator over a window. Agreement: a contract with consequences if the target is missed.
  7. RPO: the most data, measured in time, that may be lost. RTO: the longest time allowed to restore service.
  8. A Job runs pods to completion. A Service gives a stable name and address for a set of pods.

K10. Reviewing an infrastructure change (6 marks, CLO 3, Evaluate)

A pull request titled "rename namespace" contains this plan output:

  # kubernetes_namespace.shop must be replaced
-/+ resource "kubernetes_namespace" "shop" {
      ~ metadata {
          ~ name = "shop" -> "shop-prod" # forces replacement
        }
    }

Plan: 1 to add, 0 to change, 1 to destroy.

At the same time, Argo CD shows the orders application as OutOfSync. The live Deployment has 6 replicas; Git says 3.

(a) What happens to the running application and its database volume if this plan is applied? (2) (b) Would you approve? Write two review comments in the README 6.3 format. (2) (c) What does the OutOfSync status tell you, and what should happen next? (2)

Marking notes.

  • (a) Deleting a namespace deletes everything in it: Deployments, Services, Secrets and volume claims. Depending on the reclaim policy, the database volume and its data are deleted. Users see an outage (2).
  • (b) Do not approve (1 for two well-formed comments). Examples:
    • [blocking] The plan destroys a namespace that holds the database. Instead: create the new namespace, restore a backup into it, switch traffic, then remove the old one.
    • [question] Where is the backup, and how was the restore tested?
  • (c) Someone changed the replica count by hand: this is drift (1). If self-heal is on, Argo CD will set it back to 3. If the manual scale-up was an emergency response, record the reason and make the change through a pull request. Find out who made it, and why, from the audit log (1).

9. Issues for the committee

These are reported, not fixed. The outline is unchanged.

  1. The lab runs ahead of the theory. Sessions 1–7 each use a topic that the theory teaches about one week later:
    • images in session 1, theory week 2;
    • probes and requests in session 2, theory week 3;
    • ingress, storage and policy in session 3, theory week 4;
    • timeouts and retries in session 4, theory week 5;
    • queues in session 5, theory week 6;
    • infrastructure as code in session 6, theory week 7;
    • deployment strategies in session 7, theory week 8.

Session 12 also does cost and security before theory weeks 13–14. This plan adds a short briefing at the start of each session and preparation reading. The committee could instead move each lab topic one week later, or bring the theory forward. Either change would alter the outline.

  1. "On one of the two timetabled days" is ambiguous. The outline says weeks 7–14 theory is taught on campus on one of the two days. This plan assumes both 90-minute classes may fall on the same day. With the 2-hour lab, that may be 5 hours of this course on one day. The timetable and the travel cost need a decision.
  2. The semester-8 rule and the examination period. By the letter of the rule, every supervised test, Level 0 quiz and viva falls in weeks 7–14. But three things fall after week 14: the final examination, a make-up practical test for documented absence (README 5.2), and any viva held in the examination period. The committee should confirm that the on-campus examination period is allowed. If not, make-ups must also fit in weeks 7–14.
  3. Week-14 viva time (README T1). This viva needs about 12 minutes per student, because the examiner breaks the student's own deployment and the student must find the fault. For 30 students that is about 6 examiner-hours, or 3 hours with two examiners. A 2-hour session fits about 18 students. Because of issue 3, the plan proposes a second viva slot inside week 14, on the other timetabled day. This needs a room and a second examiner.
  4. Capstone system used as the project (README T7). Capstone II also deploys (week 8), hardens and monitors (week 12) the same system. Section 5.2 proposes a split in which CSE-4882 marks how the system is operated, and Capstone II marks whether it is used and handed over. The capstone committee must agree to it.
  5. Students on a six-week block. Their lab check-offs in weeks 1–6 rest on pull requests and recorded demonstrations, which are weaker evidence than a live check. The live classes may also clash with their working hours. This plan relies on recordings and evening clinics. The committee should decide whether host companies are asked to release students for the live lab hour.
  6. 8 GB laptops. From week 9 the observability stack, the broker and three services together are tight on an 8 GB machine. The outline itself says 16 GB is more comfortable. This plan moves that work to the department cluster where needed. The cluster's capacity for a full section must be tested before the semester.
  7. Cloud accounts and payment cards. No student needs a paid account, as the outline requires. Where there are no education credits, students practise infrastructure as code and cost only against the local cluster and an invented price sheet. CLO 3 and CLO 6 are then met without touching a real cloud. The committee should decide whether a small department budget for an instructor-controlled account is worth providing.
  8. The game day is not given a check-off score. To keep the check-offs at exactly 20 marks with no scaling, and to avoid marking the game day twice, session 11 is assessed only through the postmortem (project) and the lab record. The committee should confirm this reading of README 5.1.
  9. No quiz before week 7. All three quizzes fall in the second half, and quiz 1 comes just before the mid-term. Students in weeks 1–6 get only ungraded online checks. This follows the rule, but the committee may want to know that the first marked feedback on theory arrives in week 7.
