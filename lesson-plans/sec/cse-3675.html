1.3 Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  1. Given a packet capture, list the hosts and services and identify one planted attack (a scan, ARP spoofing or password guessing) in Wireshark, pointing to the packets that prove it.
  2. Write a default-deny nftables rule set that allows a stated list of services, and prove with a scan that it works.
  3. Search central logs to answer five factual questions about an event: which account, from which address, when, how many attempts, and whether it succeeded.
  4. Triage a given alert as a true or false positive, citing the evidence, and write a ticket of ten lines or fewer that a colleague on the next shift could act on.
  5. State what the rules of engagement allow and forbid, and why scanning a system without permission is an offence.

Copied from the outline without change.

2. Class pattern (assumption to confirm)

  • Theory: 3 contact hours a week as two 90-minute classes (A and B): 28 classes in 14 teaching weeks.
  • Lab: one 2-hour session a week: 14 sessions.
  • Mid-term and final are held outside the 14 teaching weeks (mid-term after week 7) unless the outline says otherwise. The outline says nothing different for this course.
  • The department may re-split theory into three 60-minute classes; README section 3 gives the mapping.

Notes for this course:

  • Put class A and class B on different days. The lab works best after class A of the same week, because each lab uses that week's topic. Every lab sheet starts with a short recap, so a timetable that puts the lab first still works.
  • The four Level 0 quizzes sit inside classes 5, 12, 19 and 25 (weeks 3, 6, 10 and 13). The teacher may move a quiz by one class; the content stays the same.
  • The rules-of-engagement gate quiz in lab 1 is a pass/fail condition for lab access. It is not one of the four theory quizzes and carries no marks.
  • CSE-3651 Information Security runs in the same semester. This course uses its material (cryptography, access-control models, vulnerability classes) and does not teach it again. Weeks 1–7 rely only on Computer Networks and Operating Systems. Where a class needs a CSE-3651 idea before CSE-3651 reaches it, the class gives a two-minute working definition and says so.
  • "By hand first": students read packets and logs by hand before they use the SIEM (weeks 2–7 before weeks 9–10), and write firewall and detection rules by hand before they use rule sets written by others (weeks 6 and 8).

3.2 Classes

Teacher-made items used below: slides for each class; the one-page protocol sheet (Ethernet, ARP, IPv4, TCP, UDP, DNS, HTTP, TLS handshake, common ports); the one-page nftables sheet; the one-page log sheet (Linux log locations, the main Windows event IDs, UTC conversion for Bangladesh, UTC+6); lab-generated captures and log extracts; printed case sheets. All captures and logs come from the track range. None contains real users' traffic.

Class 1 (Week 1, A): The SOC, tiers and shifts; assets, threats and the attack lifecycle

  • CLO (PO): CLO4 (PO2, PO9); CLO6 (PO7)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Course map: what the course is for, the four SEC track courses, how theory and lab marks work, AI-use levels, the legal boundary in one sentence.
    • 10–30 min: Mini-lecture: what a security operations centre does (monitor, triage, escalate, respond, report); tier 1, tier 2 and tier 3 analysts and the detection engineer; shifts and handover; who runs SOCs (banks, MFS providers, telecom operators and managed security providers, as the track overview states).
    • 30–45 min: Card sort in pairs: twelve SOC tasks placed under the tier that normally does them.
    • 45–70 min: Assets, threats, vulnerabilities and the attack lifecycle. Worked example: a bank branch staff account is phished; follow it from the first email to data theft. At each stage ask: what could the defender see, and in which log?
    • 70–82 min: Think-pair-share: for a 40-person software firm, list three assets, two threats and the place an attack would first show up.
    • 82–90 min: Exit ticket; homework.
  • TLA: Short lecture with a worked example; card sort; think-pair-share.
  • Materials and readings: Slides; Richard Bejtlich, The Practice of Network Security Monitoring (why prevention eventually fails; the defender's process); Chris Sanders and Jason Smith, Applied Network Security Monitoring (the NSM cycle of collection, detection and analysis).
  • Formative check: Exit ticket: "Name one task of a tier 1 analyst and one reason a tier 1 analyst escalates an alert."
  • Homework: Read the opening part of Bejtlich on network security monitoring. Write five lines: what a SOC analyst does in the first hour of a shift. AI Level 2 (record any AI use in the T6 log).

Class 2 (Week 1, B): MITRE ATT&CK as a shared vocabulary; the legal and ethical boundary

  • CLO (PO): CLO6 (PO7); CLO4 (PO2, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–8 min: Recap of the attack lifecycle from class 1.
    • 8–28 min: MITRE ATT&CK: tactics (the attacker's goal at a stage), techniques and sub-techniques (how), procedures (a real group's way); why a shared vocabulary makes tickets and handovers clearer. Worked example: map the class 1 bank case to five tactics.
    • 28–40 min: Pairs map six described attacker actions to tactics on a printed extract of the Enterprise matrix.
    • 40–65 min: The legal and ethical boundary. Authorisation and scope. Accessing, scanning or interfering with a system without its owner's permission is a criminal offence in Bangladesh, as in most countries, and good intentions are not a defence. What rules of engagement contain: permitted targets, prohibited actions, data-handling rules, the consequence of a breach. Privacy of the people being monitored: monitor for security purposes only, read no more than the task needs, keep what you see confidential. Walk through T1 schedule A.
    • 65–82 min: Case discussion in groups of four. (a) A student scans the campus Wi-Fi "to help the IT office". (b) An analyst reads a colleague's personal messages in a proxy log out of curiosity. (c) A friend asks you to "test" the website of his shop. For each: is it allowed, who must authorise it, and what should you do?
    • 82–90 min: Exit check; homework.
  • TLA: Worked example; matrix mapping in pairs; case discussion (Bangla allowed in discussion; answers written in English).
  • Materials and readings: MITRE ATT&CK (printed Enterprise matrix extract); T1 schedule A; the track overview section "Lab environment and the legal and ethical boundary".
  • Formative check: Show of hands on three true/false statements, for example "Scanning a public website is legal if I do not change anything" (false: authorisation is needed).
  • Homework: Read T1 schedule A and bring one question to lab 1. Write one paragraph: "Why must a SOC protect the privacy of the staff whose activity it monitors?" AI Level 2.

Class 3 (Week 2, A): Reading traffic I: Ethernet, ARP, IP, TCP and UDP in a capture

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Diagnostic check, ungraded: ten quick questions on layers, ports and the TCP handshake. The teacher notes who needs the review most.
    • 10–30 min: Walk through the one-page protocol sheet: the Ethernet header (source and destination MAC), ARP request and reply, the IPv4 fields that matter to a defender (addresses, TTL, protocol, fragmentation flags), the TCP header (ports, flags, sequence and acknowledgement numbers), UDP.
    • 30–55 min: Live demonstration in Wireshark on a small capture: ARP resolution, the three-way handshake, data, FIN and RST; the packet list, details and bytes panes; adding a column.
    • 55–75 min: Pairs work on a printed packet list: find the handshake, the reset, the host that started each connection.
    • 75–85 min: Cold-call check on the printed list.
    • 85–90 min: Homework.
  • TLA: Diagnostic check; worked demonstration; packet reading in pairs; cold-call.
  • Materials and readings: Chris Sanders, Practical Packet Analysis (the TCP/IP refresher and Wireshark basics); one-page protocol sheet; capture A (lab-generated, a few hundred packets).
  • Formative check: Mini-whiteboard: "Packet 14 has SYN and ACK set. Which host sent packet 13, and which flags did packet 13 carry?"
  • Homework: Label ten printed packets with protocol, direction and purpose. Do it unaided first: Quiz 1 is Level 0. AI Level 2.

Class 4 (Week 2, B): Reading traffic II: DNS, HTTP and the TLS handshake; filters, streams and normal traffic

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Go through two homework packets that many got wrong.
    • 10–30 min: DNS query and response; an HTTP request and response; the TLS handshake as it appears in a capture (ClientHello with the server name, ServerHello, certificate, then encrypted application data). What a defender can and cannot see once traffic is encrypted. The cryptography itself is taught in CSE-3651.
    • 30–45 min: Capture filters (tcpdump syntax, decided before capture) and display filters (Wireshark, applied afterwards); examples: one host, one port, SYN-only packets, DNS, HTTP requests.
    • 45–60 min: Following a TCP stream; the Conversations and Protocol Hierarchy statistics.
    • 60–80 min: "What is normal?" Pairs examine a ten-minute capture of a small office network (lab-generated): list hosts, services and the top talkers, and write a three-line baseline.
    • 80–90 min: Exit ticket; homework.
  • TLA: Mini-lecture with live demonstration; guided practice; pair analysis.
  • Materials and readings: Sanders, Practical Packet Analysis (filters, following streams, statistics); Bejtlich, The Practice of Network Security Monitoring (full content and session data); capture B (lab-generated).
  • Formative check: Exit ticket: write the display filter for "all DNS queries sent by host H2" and for "TCP packets with only the SYN flag set".
  • Homework: Write eight display filters for stated needs; test them on capture B in lab 2. Unaided first. AI Level 2.

Class 5 (Week 3, A): Reconnaissance I: host discovery; SYN, connect and UDP scans. Quiz 1. Assignment 1 released

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–15 min: Quiz 1 (Level 0, 10 marks, weeks 1–2): SOC roles, ATT&CK tactics, rules of engagement, reading printed packets.
    • 15–35 min: Host discovery (ARP and ICMP sweeps, TCP probes). The SYN (half-open) scan, the connect scan and the UDP scan: the packet exchange for an open, a closed and a filtered port, drawn as ladder diagrams.
    • 35–50 min: Students draw ladder diagrams for four cases: SYN scan to an open port, a closed port and a filtered port; UDP scan to a closed port (ICMP port unreachable).
    • 50–72 min: Printed excerpt of a SYN scan: identify the scanner, the target and the open ports from the SYN/ACK replies.
    • 72–87 min: Assignment 1 brief (capture-analysis worksheet, section 5.1): task, variant captures, evidence rules, AI log.
    • 87–90 min: Wrap-up.
  • TLA: Level 0 quiz; lecture with ladder diagrams; diagram drawing; packet reading.
  • Materials and readings: Sanders, Practical Packet Analysis (TCP flags and scanning traffic); Nmap reference guide (offline copy); printed scan excerpt.
  • Formative check: Cards up: "For a SYN scan, which reply means the port is closed?" (RST, usually with ACK.)
  • Homework: Start Assignment 1 (due class 10). AI Level 2 with the T6 log and disclosure statement.

Class 6 (Week 3, B): Reconnaissance II: service and version detection; scans seen by the defender and in firewall logs

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap with two quiz 1 items that many missed.
    • 10–30 min: Service and version detection; operating-system hints; banner grabbing; why version detection makes more noise; timing options, and why a slow scan still shows up over a longer window.
    • 30–50 min: The defender's view: firewall log lines for blocked probes; many destination ports from one source in a short time; how a SIEM rule would count them; intrusion detection signatures for scanners.
    • 50–75 min: Log reading: thirty firewall log lines (lab-generated). Which source is scanning, which ports were probed, did any probe get through?
    • 75–85 min: Discussion: harmless scanning (the IT team's vulnerability scanner, monitoring tools) and why an up-to-date asset list matters.
    • 85–90 min: Exit ticket.
  • TLA: Mini-lecture; log reading in pairs; whole-class discussion.
  • Materials and readings: Sanders and Smith, Applied Network Security Monitoring (the analyst's view of reconnaissance); William Stallings, Network Security Essentials (intrusion detection basics); firewall log sheet.
  • Formative check: Exit ticket: "Write, in words, a rule that would detect a SYN scan in firewall logs, and name one source of false positives."
  • Homework: Assignment 1: list the hosts and services in your variant capture. AI Level 2.

Class 7 (Week 4, A): Attacks on the local network I: ARP spoofing, MAC flooding and IP spoofing

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap of ARP from week 2.
    • 10–35 min: ARP spoofing step by step: unsolicited ARP replies, poisoned caches, the attacker in the middle. What shows in a capture (one IP address claimed by two MAC addresses; Wireshark's duplicate-address warning) and in the ARP tables.
    • 35–50 min: MAC flooding: the switch's MAC address table and what happens when it fills. IP spoofing: forged source addresses, why replies go elsewhere, where it is used (denial of service and reflection).
    • 50–75 min: Packet reading: a printed capture with ARP spoofing. Find the attacker's MAC address, the two victims, the first poisoned reply, and the packets that show traffic passing through the attacker.
    • 75–85 min: Think-pair-share: "Your ARP table shows the gateway's IP address with a new MAC address. List three checks before you call it an attack."
    • 85–90 min: Exit check.
  • TLA: Lecture with step-by-step diagrams; packet reading; think-pair-share.
  • Materials and readings: Sanders, Practical Packet Analysis (ARP cache poisoning); Stallings, Network Security Essentials (threats on the local network); printed capture.
  • Formative check: Mini-whiteboard: write two ARP-table entries that show spoofing.
  • Homework: Read Sanders on ARP cache poisoning. Assignment 1: look for ARP spoofing in your variant. AI Level 2.

Class 8 (Week 4, B): Attacks on the local network II: rogue DHCP and VLAN hopping; the switch features that stop them

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2); CLO2 (PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap of class 7 with one printed ARP excerpt.
    • 10–30 min: Rogue DHCP (a false gateway and DNS server handed to clients); VLAN hopping by switch spoofing and by double tagging, at concept level.
    • 30–55 min: Switch features: port security (limit and fix MAC addresses on a port), DHCP snooping (trusted and untrusted ports, the binding table), dynamic ARP inspection (checks ARP against the binding table); good practice: shut unused ports, do not use the default VLAN for users. Which defence stops which attack, built up as a matrix on the board.
    • 55–75 min: Pairs complete the attack-versus-defence matrix, then decide for one branch switch which ports are trusted for DHCP and which need port security.
    • 75–85 min: Note for students: the lab range uses virtual switches, so these features appear here as diagrams and configuration examples only. Switch configuration in depth belongs to the INF track.
    • 85–90 min: Exit ticket.
  • TLA: Lecture; matrix building; small design task in pairs.
  • Materials and readings: Stallings, Network Security Essentials (network access control); Mike Chapple and David Seidl, CompTIA Security+ Study Guide (network attacks and secure network design), for students preparing for the certificate.
  • Formative check: Exit ticket: "Which feature stops rogue DHCP, and which data does dynamic ARP inspection depend on?"
  • Homework: One paragraph: why dynamic ARP inspection needs DHCP snooping. Continue Assignment 1. AI Level 2.

Class 9 (Week 5, A): Attacks on services I: SYN flood and amplification; DNS spoofing and DNS tunnelling

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: the TCP handshake and the half-open state.
    • 10–30 min: SYN flood: half-open connections fill the server's queue; SYN cookies as a concept. Amplification denial of service: a spoofed source, a small request, a large reply from a reflector (open DNS resolvers and similar services); what the victim's network sees.
    • 30–50 min: DNS spoofing: forged responses and cache poisoning as concepts, and local spoofing from a man-in-the-middle position (week 4). DNS tunnelling: data hidden in long subdomain labels and in TXT records. Signs: long, random-looking names; many unique subdomains of one domain; a steady query rate.
    • 50–75 min: Pairs analyse two printed excerpts: (a) capture statistics during a SYN flood; (b) a DNS query log with tunnelling. Identify each attack and cite the lines that show it.
    • 75–85 min: Each pair writes one detection idea in words for each attack.
    • 85–90 min: Exit check.
  • TLA: Mini-lecture; excerpt analysis in pairs; detection ideas shared on the board.
  • Materials and readings: Sanders and Smith, Applied Network Security Monitoring (DNS analysis; statistical data); Stallings, Network Security Essentials (denial-of-service attacks); printed excerpts.
  • Formative check: Six DNS names on a slide: which look like tunnelling, and why?
  • Homework: Final checks on Assignment 1. AI Level 2.

Class 10 (Week 5, B): Attacks on services II: man-in-the-middle and TLS downgrade; password guessing against SSH and remote desktop. Assignment 1 due

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO1, PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Assignment 1 submitted at the start of class. The teacher names three common errors seen while marking drafts in the lab.
    • 10–30 min: Man-in-the-middle in practice: the position comes from ARP or DNS spoofing; what TLS protects and what it does not. TLS downgrade and stripping as concepts; certificate warnings; why browsers enforce HTTPS for some sites. Protocol details stay in CSE-3651.
    • 30–50 min: Password guessing against SSH and remote desktop: online guessing, password spraying, credential stuffing. What the Linux authentication log and the Windows Security log show (failed logons, then a success; event IDs 4625 and 4624, logon type 10 for remote desktop). Lockout, rate limits and key-based login.
    • 50–75 min: Log reading: twenty-five Linux authentication log lines and ten Windows logon events (printed, lab-generated). Answer five questions: which account, from which address, when, how many attempts, and did it succeed? This rehearses minimum competency 3.
    • 75–85 min: Discussion: what makes this a real attack and not a user who forgot a password?
    • 85–90 min: Homework.
  • TLA: Lecture; structured log reading; discussion.
  • Materials and readings: Sanders and Smith, Applied Network Security Monitoring; Chapple and Seidl, CompTIA Security+ Study Guide (password attacks); one-page log sheet.
  • Formative check: The five questions themselves; the teacher checks answers from five pairs aloud.
  • Homework: Practice set for Quiz 2 (weeks 3–5): ten packet and log questions. Unaided first. AI Level 2.

Class 11 (Week 6, A): Firewalls I: packet filtering, stateful inspection, default deny, rule order, NAT, host and network firewalls

  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: the attacks of weeks 3–5 and which ones a firewall can stop.
    • 10–35 min: Packet filtering compared with stateful inspection (connection tracking: new, established, related); default deny; first-match rule order; source NAT for outbound traffic and destination NAT (port forwarding); host firewalls compared with network firewalls; ingress and egress filtering.
    • 35–60 min: Worked example: the policy for a small web server. Allow SSH from the management host only, HTTP and HTTPS from anywhere, return traffic of established connections; drop and log everything else. Write the rules as a table first, then check each against the policy.
    • 60–82 min: Packet walk: five packets against a printed rule table. For each: pass or drop, and by which rule?

  • AI-use level: Level 2.
  • How it is checked: R1, weight 3. The teaching assistant checks three citations per student at the bench; the rest from the repository.

Lab 3 (Week 3): Nmap scans while capturing

  • Aims: Run each scan type against a range target, then recognise it in a capture and in the target's logs.
  • Activities and timing (120 min):
    1. 0–10 min: Briefing; each pair copies the permitted target addresses from the range guide into the record before running anything; start a capture on the attacker's range interface.
    2. 10–25 min: Host discovery against the range address block; note start and end times.
    3. 25–55 min: SYN scan, connect scan and a UDP scan of the most common ports on range target T1, one at a time, with times noted. Roles: one student scans, the other watches the capture; then swap.
    4. 55–70 min: Service and version detection on T1.
    5. 70–100 min: Find each scan in the capture with filters and in the target's logs; annotate the packets that show which scan type it was (for example, a SYN followed by the scanner's RST, not an ACK, for a SYN scan).
    6. 100–115 min: Write the scan report: each scan, its evidence (T3), and one sentence on how a defender would notice it.

Lab 4 (Week 4): ARP spoofing and DNS spoofing inside the range

  • Aims: Carry out ARP and DNS spoofing between range machines, then detect both from ARP tables and captures, and state the defences.
  • Activities and timing (120 min):
    1. 0–10 min: Briefing; roles: attacker, victim, observer (rotate in a group of three, or two roles in a pair).
    2. 10–25 min: "Before" evidence: ARP tables on the victim and the range gateway machine; a baseline capture; resolve a range-only name and note the answer.
    3. 25–55 min: ARP spoofing with Ettercap or Bettercap between the victim and the range gateway machine; confirm that the victim's traffic passes the attacker; capture.
    4. 55–75 min: DNS spoofing of one name in the range's own DNS zone so that it points to the attacker's web page; the victim browses to it; capture.
    5. 75–95 min: Detect both: changed ARP entries, one IP address claimed by two MAC addresses, DNS replies from the wrong source. Stop the attack and collect "after" evidence.
    6. 95–110 min: Write the note on the defence: dynamic ARP inspection with DHCP snooping, static ARP entries for critical hosts, protection of DNS answers, and TLS with valid certificates.
    7. 110–120 min: Restore all machines; bench sign-off.
  • Deliverable: Evidence before and after; note on the defence.
  • AI-use level: Level 2.
  • How it is checked: R1, weight 3: before and after evidence for both attacks, each with a "what it shows" sentence; the defence note names the defence for each attack.
  • Safety and legal boundary: Spoof only range machines and names in the range's own DNS zone. The "gateway" is a range virtual machine, never a real router. Spoofing tools are never started on an interface that touches the campus network.

Lab 5 (Week 5): Password guessing and SYN flood; find both in the logs by hand

  • Aims: Generate password guessing and a SYN flood against a range target, and find both by hand with grep and simple shell tools.
  • Activities and timing (120 min):
    1. 0–10 min: Briefing; the instructor supplies a short wordlist and the lab accounts; the flood rate and duration are set by the instructor.
    2. 10–35 min: Hydra against SSH on range target T1 with the supplied wordlist; one lab account has a weak password. Note start and end times.
    3. 35–50 min: hping3 SYN flood against T1's web port for about one minute at the instructor's rate; observe the effect from the victim's client machine.
    4. 50–95 min: Log analysis by hand: grep, cut, sort and uniq -c on the authentication log to answer the five questions (account, source address, time, number of attempts, success or not); the SYN flood seen in capture statistics and in the target's half-open connections (ss).
    5. 95–115 min: Write the log extracts, each followed by a one-line explanation.
    6. 115–120 min: Restore snapshots; bench sign-off.
  • Deliverable: Log extracts with a one-line explanation of each.
  • AI-use level: Level 2.
  • How it is checked: R1, weight 3: the five answers are right and each cites the log lines; the flood is shown by numbers from the capture or the target.
  • Safety and legal boundary: Flood only the target machine inside your own PC's host-only network, for the set time and rate. Never direct any traffic at the department's shared servers. Use only the supplied wordlist and lab accounts.

Lab 6 (Week 6): Host firewall with nftables, then a three-zone network firewall

  • Aims: Write a default-deny host firewall by hand, then build a three-zone network firewall, and prove both with scans.
  • Activities and timing (120 min):

    6. 112–120 min: Export both rule sets; restore snapshots; bench sign-off.
  • Deliverable: Rule sets and test evidence.
  • AI-use level: Level 2. Write part A by hand first: test 1 is Level 0.
  • How it is checked: R1, weight 3: default deny present; every allowed flow matches the stated policy; the expected-against-observed table has no unexplained difference.
  • Safety and legal boundary: All firewall interfaces attach only to internal or host-only networks. The firewall has no interface on the campus network.

6.9 CLO attainment: calculation and records

The method is the one in README section 8, applied as follows.

  1. Tagging. Every question part, quiz item, rubric criterion, test task and viva criterion carries one CLO tag, as in sections 6.4, 6.6, 6.7 and 6.8.
  2. Student CLO score. For each student and each CLO: marks obtained on items tagged with that CLO, divided by the maximum marks of those items, times 100. All four quizzes count for attainment, not only the best three. An item missed for an approved reason is left out of both the numerator and the maximum; an item missed without reason counts as zero.
  3. Student attainment. A student attains a CLO at 50% or more (proposal for the department to confirm).
  4. Course attainment. The course attains a CLO when at least 60% of the students who sat the final assessment attain it (proposal).
  5. Separate codes. Attainment is calculated separately for CSE-3675 (theory) and CSE-3676 (lab), and both are reported in the course file.
  6. PO contribution. Each CLO's attainment is reported against its POs from the outline (for example, CLO4 feeds PO2 and PO9). The department's OBE committee combines course results into programme results.

The record. One spreadsheet per section: a "marks" sheet with one row per student and one column per assessed item, with three header rows giving the item, its CLO and its maximum; a "CLO" sheet that calculates each student's CLO scores; a "summary" sheet with the percentage of students attaining each CLO for theory and lab. The spreadsheet is filled as marks are entered, not at the end.

Evidence kept for accreditation. Question papers with CLO and Bloom tags and model answers; three marked scripts of each examination, quiz and test (high, middle, low); the two assignment briefs with three marked samples each; the practical test variants and marking schemes; project reports and viva forms of three pairs; the rules-of-engagement register with signed agreements; a sample of AI-use logs; the attainment spreadsheet; and the end-of-course report.

Closing the loop. The end-of-course report lists each CLO below target, the likely reason (for example "CLO4 tickets lacked scope"), and one change for the next offering (for example "add a second triage drill in week 11"). The next offering's report states whether the change worked.

7. Support for weaker students

The intake is about 15% strong, 60% mid-level and 25% below average. The outline notes that weaker students usually lack fluency in TCP/IP, not security ideas, and that SOC work rewards method, patience and clear writing more than fast programming. The course is built to use that.

For the mid-level 60%: method over speed.

  • Every analytical task follows the same three steps: find the evidence, say what it shows in one sentence, decide. The T3 findings sheet and the T4 ticket give the same structure every week, so the method becomes a habit.
  • Playbooks and checklists are given, not withheld. Real SOC analysts use them; a student who follows a playbook carefully and writes a clear ticket is doing the job well.
  • Triage is practised three times before it is tested: the class 21 drill, the class 22 ticket rewrite and the lab 11 shift. Test 2 then asks for the same tasks.
  • Short writing is rewarded: a ten-line ticket that the next shift can act on scores higher than a long one.
  • The optional CompTIA Security+ certificate (framework section 13 lists ISC2 CC and CompTIA Security+ against CSE-3651 and this course) gives a student evidence that does not depend on CGPA. The teacher tells students in week 1 which weeks match which parts of the Chapple and Seidl study guide.

For the 25% below average.

  • Week 2 is a deliberate TCP/IP review. The diagnostic check in class 3 identifies who needs it most; those students get extra practice captures in the first two weeks.
  • One-page sheets (protocol, nftables, log) are given in week 2, 6 and 9. Students may make their own A4 reference sheet for each practical test, so recall does not block application.
  • Captures are kept small in weeks 2–6. Each lab sheet starts with guided steps and ends with an unguided step; the floor tasks are the minimum competencies, and stretch tasks are marked as optional.
  • Pairs in weeks 3–6, with individual records. The lab instructor forms the pairs so that a weaker student works with a mid-level one, not with the strongest, and rotates them after week 4.
  • Early warning: after Quiz 1 (week 3) and after test 1 (week 7), students below 40% are invited to a weekly one-hour clinic with the teaching assistant in the open lab hours. The clinic repeats the floor tasks with fresh data.
  • A practice variant of each supervised test is released one week before it (weeks 6 and 11) as homework.
  • Discussion in Bangla is welcome in class and lab; written work is in English and is marked for clarity, not for grammar (R2 gives 2 of 20 marks to structure and English).

For the strong 15%.

  • The outline's stretch goals are offered as optional extensions inside the project: one detection written both as a Sigma rule and as a native Wazuh rule and measured against a week of baseline logs; mapping the lab's detections to ATT&CK and closing one of the three largest gaps.
  • They may act as peer helpers in the clinic, explaining but not doing the work.

9. Issues for the committee

  1. How the range reaches the Wazuh server. The track overview describes VirtualBox machines on a host-only network with no route to the campus network, but lab 9 and the project send events to one department Wazuh server. A host-only network cannot reach another machine. The department must build an isolated lab segment (a separate switch or VLAN) that links the lab PCs' virtual machines to the server and has no route to the campus network or the internet, and the range guide must describe it.
  2. Memory on 8 GB lab PCs. The outline says 8 GB runs the attacker and one target. Lab 6 (firewall plus three zones) and the project network (firewall, sensor, attacker, DMZ server, inside target and a Windows machine with Sysmon) need more. This plan lets partners split lab 6, allows a Linux router instead of OPNsense, and makes the Windows machine optional on 8 GB PCs. The department should decide whether to fit 16 GB in the SEC lab PCs.
  3. Week 14 cannot hold the demonstrations and all vivas. About 15 demonstrations and 30 individual vivas of about eight minutes need about five and a half examiner-hours. The 2-hour lab slot is not enough even with two examiners. An extra slot in week 14 or in the examination period, and a second examiner, must be timetabled.
  4. Overlap with CSE-3652. The CSE-3652 week-11 lab (Nmap scanning, sniffing, ARP spoofing in the isolated lab, firewall rules) repeats CSE-3676 weeks 3–6 for SEC students in the same semester. Students also sign two rules-of-engagement agreements in week 1 for two labs on similar ranges. The committee may want SEC students to do an extension task in CSE-3652 week 11, and one combined agreement.
  5. Gates that override marks. The outline makes the viva a pass gate, and this plan makes the rules-of-engagement quiz a gate for lab access. The committee must decide what result a student receives who fails the viva gate, how many gate-quiz attempts are allowed (this plan proposes three, the third after a one-to-one briefing), and check both against the university's grading rules.
  6. Switch defences in week 4. Port security, DHCP snooping and dynamic ARP inspection are taught by diagram only, because the range uses virtual switches. The outline does not require a lab demonstration. If the department wants one, it needs managed switches (the INF track is buying a few) or a network emulator that is not in this course's resource list.
