1.3 Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  1. Image a storage device or virtual disk without altering it, compute and verify its hash, and fill in a chain-of-custody record.
  2. From a supplied disk image, recover a deleted file and list user logons and recently opened files with their timestamps, using Autopsy or the Sleuth Kit.
  3. From a memory image, list processes and network connections with Volatility 3 and point out one suspicious process with a reason.
  4. Build a timeline of at least ten events in UTC from two sources, each event citing its evidence.
  5. Write a one-page incident summary that states what happened, what is confirmed, what is unknown and what to do next.

2. Class pattern (assumption to confirm)

  • Theory: 3 contact hours a week as two 90-minute classes (A and B): 28 classes in 14 teaching weeks.
  • Lab: one 2-hour session a week: 14 sessions.
  • Mid-term and final are held outside the 14 teaching weeks (mid-term after week 7), as the outline does not say otherwise.
  • The department may re-split theory into three 60-minute classes; README section 3 gives the mapping.

Notes for this course:

  • Evidence sets and practice files are different things. Evidence sets are every disk image, memory image, capture, log set and sample issued in the lab or for the project. They carry a hash and a custody record, they stay on lab machines and the department evidence store, and they are never copied to personal devices or uploaded anywhere. Practice files are small teacher-made excerpts (a page of log lines, a printed tool output, a tiny practice image) marked "practice" in their file name. Students may take practice files home for homework. Every homework task below uses practice files only.
  • Tabletop classes (classes 4, 24 and 27) need a room where groups of five can sit together. Discussion may be in Bangla; decision logs, role sheets and notes are written in English.
  • Lab machines run two department virtual machines: a Linux forensic workstation (the Sleuth Kit, Autopsy, Volatility 3, Plaso, Zeek, YARA, PhotoRec, RegRipper, dc3dd, Guymager) and a Windows analysis machine (FTK Imager, the Sysinternals suite, Event Viewer, NetworkMiner) with its network adapter disabled. The SIFT Workstation is an acceptable ready-made alternative to the department's Linux image. Evidence images are kept under 8 GB.
  • Evidence check-out. Each session that uses evidence starts with check-out from the evidence store and hash verification, and ends with check-in, using the log in Appendix B.
  • Time. UTC is used in every findings sheet, timeline and report. The one-page UTC conversion sheet is issued in week 1.
  • Assumed sizes (README): theory section up to 40 students; lab section about 30, one lab instructor, a teaching assistant in test, project and viva weeks.

3.2 Classes

Week 1

Class 1 (Week 1, A): Incidents, the lifecycle and the response team

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Course map. CSE-3675 ended when the analyst escalated an alert; this course starts there. What students will produce: findings sheets, timelines, an incident report. Marks, AI levels and the evidence rule on one slide.
    • 10–30: Event, alert and incident. Card sort in pairs: a single failed logon, 500 failed logons in ten minutes, a ransom note on a file server, a lost staff laptop, a staff member copying the customer list to a personal drive. Which are incidents, and who decides to declare one.
    • 30–55: The lifecycle after NIST SP 800-61: preparation; detection and analysis; containment, eradication and recovery; post-incident work. Why it is a loop. One worked example walked through all four phases: a phished staff account, from the SOC ticket to the lessons-learned meeting. One slide shows that the current revision of SP 800-61 describes the same work through the NIST Cybersecurity Framework functions, and how the four phases map onto them.
    • 55–75: The response team: incident lead, analysts, IT operations, communications, management, legal liaison. The hand-over from the SOC: what a good escalation ticket from CSE-3675 gives the team (time, host, account, evidence, what was already done) and what the team asks next.
    • 75–85: Formative check.
    • 85–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture with one worked example; card sort in pairs; think-pair-share on "who should have the authority to declare an incident?"
  • Materials and readings: NIST SP 800-61 (the lifecycle and the team); Gerard Johansen, Digital Forensics and Incident Response (the incident-response process); teacher-made card set; course calendar.
  • Formative check: exit ticket: name the four phases in order and place three actions in the right phase (reset the password; image the laptop; hold the review meeting).
  • Homework: read the lifecycle section of NIST SP 800-61. Write five lines: which phase do small organisations most often skip, and what does it cost them later? AI Level 2.

Class 2 (Week 1, B): Authority, privacy, lawyers and law enforcement; the course agreement

  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO5, PO7); CLO1 (PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: the four phases, cold-called.
    • 10–30: Authority to investigate. The owner of a system, through a manager with authority, orders an investigation in writing and sets its scope. Going beyond the scope weakens the case and can be an offence: examining another person's device or account without authority is an offence. Three short scenarios: a manager asks you to "just check" an employee's personal phone; a colleague offers you the password of a suspect's mailbox; a client asks you to find out who the attacker is "at any cost".
    • 30–50: Privacy. Evidence holds staff and customer data. Need-to-know, collecting only what the question needs, secure storage, and what to do when you find personal data that has nothing to do with the case (record that it exists, do not read further, report to the incident lead).
    • 50–65: When lawyers and law enforcement are brought in, in general terms: a crime is suspected, a regulator or client must be told, a court case is likely, or a staff member is the suspect. The organisation's legal adviser decides; the investigator preserves evidence so that this choice stays open. This course gives principles, not legal advice.
    • 65–80: The course's rules-of-engagement and evidence-handling agreement (README T1, schedule C), clause by clause: permitted evidence, prohibited actions, no evidence or evidence values (hashes, addresses, file names) to any external AI or scanning service, harmless simulators only, the consequence of a breach. Students sign it in Lab 1.
    • 80–87: Formative check.
    • 87–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: case discussion in groups of four on the three scenarios, then whole-class answers; guided reading of the agreement. If possible the university's legal officer or a practitioner joins for 20 minutes of the 50–65 segment.
  • Materials and readings: NIST SP 800-86 (the forensic process and its legal considerations); Jason Luttgens, Matthew Pepe and Kevin Mandia, Incident Response & Computer Forensics (the parts on legal issues and working with counsel); README T1 schedule C.
  • Formative check: three yes-or-no questions on mini-whiteboards, each with a one-line reason ("May you image a staff member's personal phone because the manager asked you to?").
  • Homework: read T1 schedule C and bring one question to Lab 1. Write five lines: what you would do if you found an unrelated private photo collection on a work laptop you were examining. AI Level 2.

Week 2

Class 3 (Week 2, A): Plans, playbooks, first questions and the order of volatility

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO2)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: authority and scope.
    • 10–35: Preparation. A response plan (scope, roles, contacts, severity levels, how to communicate) and playbooks (one per incident type). The "jump kit": a forensic workstation, clean and labelled drives, custody forms, contact list. Logging that must exist before the incident (link to CSE-3675 weeks 9–10).
    • 35–55: The first questions. What was seen, by whom, when and in which time zone; which systems and accounts; what has already been done; is it still happening; who else knows. Role-play: the teacher plays a branch manager phoning in; students ask questions in turn; the class writes the answers in a first-contact record.
    • 55–75: Order of volatility: CPU state and caches, memory, network state and connections, running processes, disk, remote logs, backups and archives. Why the order matters. Pair exercise: order eight evidence sources for a running Windows server.
    • 75–85: Formative check.
    • 85–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: short lecture; role-play of a first call; ordering exercise in pairs.
  • Materials and readings: NIST SP 800-61 (preparation); NIST SP 800-86 (data collection and the order of volatility); Johansen (preparation and first response); teacher-made first-contact record.
  • Formative check: each student orders eight cards by volatility, then compares with a neighbour; the teacher shows the answer and asks two pairs to justify a disputed card.
  • Homework: write the first ten questions you would ask when a staff member reports "I clicked a link and typed my password" (one page). AI Level 2.

Class 4 (Week 2, B): Live response or power off; containment and its cost; short tabletop

  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO2); CLO6 (PO8, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: order of volatility.
    • 10–25: Live response or power off. What is lost on power-off (memory, connections, decrypted data); what live response changes on the system (our own commands leave traces); pulling the network cable instead of the power; virtual machines can be snapshotted or suspended.
    • 25–40: Containment options and what each costs: disable the account, reset credentials, isolate a host, block an address, take a service offline. Examples: taking an MFS agent portal offline on the last day of the month; tipping off an attacker who still has other ways in. Short-term and long-term containment.
    • 40–80: Short tabletop: a phished staff account at a fictional MFS provider. Groups of five with roles (incident lead, analyst, IT operations, communications, management). Three injects, ten minutes apart: (1) a SOC ticket shows a logon to the staff member's mailbox from an unusual address soon after a phishing email; (2) the account has sent 200 internal emails with a link; (3) the account opened a share holding customer data. Each group writes every decision, its reason and its cost on a decision log (Appendix D).
    • 80–90: Debrief and formative check.
  • TLA: mini-lecture; tabletop with assigned roles; whole-class debrief comparing decisions across groups.
  • Materials and readings: NIST SP 800-61 (containment strategy); Luttgens, Pepe and Mandia (remediation and containment); tabletop pack and decision log (Appendix D).
  • Formative check: during the debrief each group reads out its first containment step and its cost; the teacher marks on the board which groups lost evidence by acting too early and which let the attacker continue by waiting.
  • Homework: half a page: which containment step did your group take first, what did it cost the business, and would you change it now? AI Level 2.

Week 3

Class 5 (Week 3, A): Forensic soundness, write blocking, image formats and hashing

  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO5, PO7)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: what live response changes.
    • 10–25: Forensic soundness: do not change the original; when a change cannot be avoided, record it; another examiner must be able to repeat the work and get the same result.
    • 25–40: Write blocking: hardware and software blockers; read-only access in Linux; testing that the blocker really blocks before trusting it.
    • 40–55: Image formats: raw (a bit-for-bit copy) and E01 (the Expert Witness format, which stores case notes and hashes with the data); physical and logical images; when each is used.
    • 55–75: Hashing: what a hash proves (the copy is identical to what was hashed before) and what it does not prove (that the content is true). MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256; why two values are often recorded. Live demonstration: hash a file, change one byte, hash again; hash an image at acquisition and again at verification.
    • 75–85: Formative check.
    • 85–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: lecture with live demonstration in the forensic workstation; predict-observe-explain on the one-byte change.
  • Materials and readings: NIST SP 800-86 (collection and integrity); Brian Carrier, File System Forensic Analysis (the opening material on acquisition); Johansen (evidence acquisition); dc3dd, Guymager and FTK Imager introduced by name.
  • Formative check: two hash lists for the same image (at acquisition and at verification) with one mismatch: what does it mean, and what is the next step?
  • Homework: read the dc3dd and Guymager pages of the lab guide. List the fields you must record when you acquire an image. Do it unaided first: Quiz 1 is next class. AI Level 2.

Class 6 (Week 3, B): Chain of custody, working copies, notes and admissibility; Quiz 1

  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO5, PO7)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–15: Quiz 1 (Level 0, 15 minutes, 10 marks): the lifecycle, containment choices, order of volatility, hashing.
    • 15–35: Chain of custody: what the record must show at every transfer (who, what, when in UTC, where stored, why, hash). Evidence labels and sealed bags. Walk through the course custody form (Appendix A).
    • 35–50: Working copies: examine a verified copy, never the original; hash the copy; record where it is.
    • 50–65: Contemporaneous notes: written at the time, in UTC, saying what was done and what was seen. Notes are part of the evidence; never rewrite them later.
    • 65–80: General principles that make evidence usable later: relevant, authentic, complete, reliable, lawfully obtained, and explained by an examiner who can describe every step. Court procedure itself is outside the course.
    • 80–87: Formative check.
    • 87–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: quiz; worked example of a filled custody form; gap-finding in pairs.
  • Materials and readings: NIST SP 800-86; Luttgens, Pepe and Mandia (evidence handling and documentation); Appendix A.
  • Formative check: a custody record with two planted gaps (a transfer with no receiving signature; two days with no storage location). Pairs find both and say why each matters.
  • Homework: fill a blank custody form for a described hand-over with three transfers (practice sheet). AI Level 2.

Week 4

Class 7 (Week 4, A): Partition tables and the NTFS master file table

  • CLO (PO): CLO3 (PO4, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: custody and working copies.
    • 10–30: Disk layout: sectors, the MBR partition table (four entries, extended partitions), GPT (protective MBR, header, entry array, backup copy at the end). Finding where a partition starts. Reading mmls output.
    • 30–60: NTFS: everything is a file; the master file table ($MFT); the parts of an MFT entry; the attributes that matter ($STANDARD_INFORMATION, $FILE_NAME, $DATA); resident and non-resident data. Worked example: reading an MFT entry from istat output.
    • 60–75: NTFS timestamps: the four times (modified, accessed, entry changed, created) in both $STANDARD_INFORMATION and $FILE_NAME; what ordinary actions change; why a $STANDARD_INFORMATION time earlier than the $FILE_NAME time can be a sign of tampering.
    • 75–85: Formative check.
    • 85–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: lecture with annotated printed tool output; in-class reading of one MFT entry in pairs.
  • Materials and readings: Carrier, File System Forensic Analysis (volume analysis; NTFS concepts); the Sleuth Kit (mmls, fsstat, istat); one-page NTFS reference sheet.
  • Formative check: from printed mmls output, give the start of the NTFS partition in sectors and in bytes.
  • Homework: answer five questions on a printed istat output (practice sheet). AI Level 2.

Class 8 (Week 4, B): Alternate data streams, the journal, deleted files, slack space and carving

  • CLO (PO): CLO3 (PO4, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: MFT entries and timestamps.
    • 10–25: Alternate data streams: extra data attached to a file; how they can hide data; the common legitimate stream that records where a downloaded file came from.
    • 25–40: The NTFS journals: $LogFile and the change journal ($UsnJrnl); what they can show (created, renamed, deleted) and why they cover only a limited period.
    • 40–60: Deleted files: what deletion does in NTFS (the entry is marked unused; the clusters become free); recovery while entry and clusters survive; orphan files; file slack and unallocated space.
    • 60–75: Carving: finding files by their header and footer when the metadata is gone. Strengths and limits: no names, no times, trouble with fragmented files. PhotoRec.
    • 75–85: Formative check.
    • 85–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: lecture; worked example with printed fls -d and icat output; think-pair-share: "a file was deleted last week; what can we still learn, and what is lost?"
  • Materials and readings: Carrier (NTFS analysis; recovering deleted files); the Sleuth Kit; PhotoRec; one-page NTFS reference sheet.
  • Formative check: three statements about deleted files, each marked true or false with one reason.
  • Homework: five lines: why a carved file has no name and no timestamps, and what other evidence could give it a time. AI Level 2.

Week 5

Class 9 (Week 5, A): ext4, authentication logs and shell history

  • CLO (PO): CLO3 (PO4, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: deleted files in NTFS.
    • 10–30: ext4 for the examiner: inodes and their timestamps (access, modify, change, and creation time on ext4); directory entries; the journal; why recovering deleted files is harder than on NTFS.
    • 30–55: Authentication logs: auth.log (or secure on some distributions) and the systemd journal; wtmp, btmp and lastlog read with last and lastb; sshd lines for accepted and failed logons and invalid users; sudo lines. Worked example: twenty lines showing password guessing followed by a successful logon and a sudo command.
    • 55–75: Shell history: .bash_history, what it records and what it misses (no times by default; can be cleared or switched off); histories of root and service accounts such as the web-server user.
    • 75–85: Formative check.
    • 85–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: log reading in pairs with highlighters; short lecture segments between readings.
  • Materials and readings: Johansen (Linux evidence); Carrier (the Ext2 and Ext3 concepts that ext4 extends); one-page Linux artefact reference sheet; one-page UTC conversion sheet.
  • Formative check: on a printed auth.log excerpt written in local time (UTC+06:00), mark the first successful logon by the attacker and give its time in UTC.
  • Homework: answer six questions with grep on the practice file auth-practice.log (who, from where, how many attempts, first success). Do it unaided first. AI Level 2.

Class 10 (Week 5, B): cron, systemd, SSH keys, web-server logs and persistence

  • CLO (PO): CLO3 (PO4, PO5)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10: Recap: reading sshd lines.
    • 10–30: Where attackers keep persistence on Linux: system and user crontabs and cron.d; systemd units and timers; authorized_keys; shell start-up files; new accounts in /etc/passwd. How to check each, and which timestamps matter.
    • 30–55: Web-server logs: Apache and Nginx access and error logs; the combined log format; finding an uploaded web shell (a POST to an upload page, then GET requests to a new script with a command parameter); status codes and response sizes. Link to CSE-4775: the same unsafe upload flaw, seen from the defender's side.
    • 55–75: Worked example: a compromised web server told from its logs (scan, upload, web-shell commands, cron persistence). Students build a six-row evidence table with the two columns "Evidence" and "What it shows" (README R3).
    • 75–85: Formative check.
    • 85–90: Summary; homework.
  • TLA: worked example; R3 table practice in pairs; teacher checks three tables aloud.
  • Materials and readings: Johansen (Linux artefacts and persistence); one-page Linux artefact reference sheet; README T3 findings sheet.
  • Formative check: a printed crontab line and a printed systemd unit file: which one is malicious, and what does it run?
  • Homework: list, in order, the places you would check for persistence on a Linux server, with the command for each (one page). AI Level 2.

Week 6

Class 11 (Week 6, A): Registry hives; event logs, event IDs and logon types

  • CLO (PO): CLO3 (PO4, PO5)

Lab 2 (Week 2): Live response by hand

  • Aims: collect volatile data from a running range machine in order of volatility, recording every command and time.
  • Activities and timing (120 min):

    2. 15–25: Clock check: record the workstation clock, the target's clock and the range time source; note any offset.
    3. 25–75: Collect, in checklist order, from the assigned Linux range target T1: date and time; uptime; logged-in users (who, w, last); processes with full command lines; network connections (ss); interfaces, routes and the ARP cache; open files of one named process (lsof); mounted file systems; scheduled jobs for each user. Lab 3 (Week 3): Imaging a USB flash drive

  • Aims: image a device without changing it, verify the image and keep an unbroken custody record.
  • Activities and timing (120 min):

    3. 20–35: Write protection: attach the drive to the forensic workstation; identify it (lsblk, kernel messages); set it read-only before anything else; test that a write attempt fails; record the test. A hardware write blocker is better practice; this lab uses the software method and says so in the record.
    4. 35–50: Hash the source device and record the value and the UTC time.
    5. 50–80: Image the drive: one partner with dc3dd (raw image with its hash and log options), the other with Guymager (E01). Record start and end times.
    6. 80–95: Verify: hash each image, compare with the source hash and the tool's log; make a working copy and verify it too.

Lab 4 (Week 4): NTFS by hand, then Autopsy; deleted files and carving

Lab 5 (Week 5): A compromised Linux web server

6.9 CLO attainment: calculation and records

The README section 8 method is applied as follows.

  • Tagging. Every question part, quiz item, rubric criterion, test task and viva criterion above carries one CLO. The tags in 6.4–6.8 are the tags used.
  • Student score per CLO. Marks obtained on items tagged with the CLO, divided by the maximum marks of those items, as a percentage. For attainment, all four quizzes count, not only the best three. Items missed for an accepted reason are left out of both the numerator and the denominator; items missed without one count as zero.
  • Student attainment. A student attains a CLO at 50% or more (proposal).
  • Course attainment. The course attains a CLO when at least 60% of the students who sat the final assessment attain it (proposal). It is calculated separately for CSE-4785 (theory) and CSE-4786 (lab).
  • Record sheet. One spreadsheet per section with one row per student and one column per assessed item; a header row gives each item's CLO, Bloom level and maximum marks. Summary columns compute each CLO percentage and attained or not. A second sheet gives the course-level attainment per CLO and the PO contribution through the 6.8 mapping.
  • Evidence kept in the course file. Question papers with CLO and Bloom tags and model answers; marking schemes; three marked scripts per assessment (high, middle, low); the two test variants with marking notes; three project reports with their rubric sheets; viva record forms; the signed agreement register; custody and evidence check-out logs; a sample of T6 AI-use logs; the record sheet; and the attainment report.
  • Closing the loop. For each CLO below target the teacher writes, within four weeks of results, the likely cause and one change for the next offering (for example: CLO5 below target, cause time-zone errors in test 2, change: a second conversion drill in week 8). The next teacher reads this note before week 1.

7. Support for weaker students

The intake is about 15% strong, 60% mid-level and 25% below average. By semester 7 every student in this course has passed the Core Competency Assessment and CSE-3675, so the gap is mostly in method, care with detail and written English, not in programming. Forensics and incident response suit mid-level students: the work rewards following a procedure, recording evidence and writing clearly, and every one of these can be taught and checked. The course makes that method visible and gives marks for it.

  1. Question-led worksheets in Labs 2–6. Each worksheet breaks a finding into small steps ("At which sector does the partition start? Which entries are marked deleted? Recover entry 64 and hash it."). The first questions are floor questions that every student must answer; the last are stretch questions.
  2. One-page references for each artefact family, issued the week it is taught: NTFS; Linux artefacts; Windows event IDs and logon types; the normal Windows process tree and the Volatility 3 plugins used; Zeek logs; UTC conversion. Students may bring their own A4 sheet to the practical tests (6.6), so the tests check use, not memory.
  3. Templates and worked examples. A filled findings sheet (Appendix B), a model one-page summary, the report template (Appendix C) and a model chain-of-custody record are shown before students produce their own.
  4. By hand first, on small evidence. Small images and short logs keep the by-hand step short enough to finish. When students later meet Autopsy and Plaso, the tool output answers a question they already understand.
  5. Pairs with individual records. Weekly labs are done in pairs; partners swap the "driver" and "recorder" roles halfway through each session; every student keeps their own lab record page (T7) and is asked about it at sign-off.
  6. Early warning. After Quiz 1 (week 3) and Test 1 (week 7), students below 40% (proposal) are invited to a clinic and agree a short plan with the lab instructor; the meeting is recorded.
  7. Clinics before tests. A one-hour clinic in weeks 6 and 11 works through a practice variant of the coming test under test conditions, then goes over the answers.
  8. Time-zone drill. From week 7, two minutes at the start of each theory class convert one timestamp to UTC; time-zone errors are the commonest cause of lost marks.
  9. Language. A glossary of terms in plain English; sentence patterns for findings ("The evidence shows ...", "This suggests ...", "We could not determine ..."); tabletop discussion may be in Bangla, while decision logs, notes and reports are in English. Spelling and grammar carry only 2 of 20 marks in R2; clarity is what counts.
  10. Open lab sessions in project weeks (section 9, issue 3) give weaker teams supervised time with the evidence, since evidence cannot be taken home.
  11. Stretch for strong students. The outline's stretch goals are offered as optional tasks: test the Lab 11 YARA rule against a larger clean file set and report false positives; collect triage data from several range machines with Velociraptor or a script and compare it with manual collection; examine the department's "cleared logs and changed timestamps" case and show which evidence survived. Strong students may also serve as voluntary lab mentors.

Appendix C: Incident report template

At most ten pages, plus appendices. Times in UTC throughout.

  1. Title block: case ID, report version, date, authors, distribution ("confidential: named readers only").
  2. Management summary (one page): what happened, in three to five sentences; the impact on the business; the current status (contained? since when?); what has been done; decisions management must take; the three most important recommendations.
  3. Scope and evidence: the questions the investigation was asked to answer; the evidence examined (a summary; the full list with hashes is in an appendix); limitations (missing logs, clock problems).
  4. Timeline: the key events in a table (UTC time, event, source, confidence); the full timeline is in an appendix.
  5. Findings: one sub-heading per finding, each with its evidence references and its owner.
  6. Impact: systems, data and business effects; what is known and what is not.
  7. Root cause: how the attacker got in and why it worked.
  8. Recommendations: immediate, short-term and long-term; each with an owner and a priority.
  9. What remains unknown: open questions and how they could be answered.
  • Appendices: full timeline; evidence list with hashes and custody references; tools and versions; glossary; T6 AI-use log.

Writing rules: state facts with their evidence; mark anything inferred as "likely" or "assumed"; never name a suspect from evidence alone; use only the fictional names in the case.

Appendix D: Tabletop pack

Rules of the exercise: stay in your role; each decision is made within the time limit and written in the decision log; the controller's word on facts is final; ask the controller for information rather than invent it; no real organisation or person is named.

Role cards (summaries)

  • Incident lead: runs the response, sets priorities, decides containment within the authority given, keeps the decision log signed.
  • Analyst: says what the evidence shows and what it does not; asks for the evidence needed; warns before an action destroys evidence.
  • Communications: drafts messages to staff, customers and the press for management approval; keeps one agreed version of the facts.
  • Management: owns business decisions (service shutdown, spending, any contact with the attacker); balances cost and risk.
  • Legal liaison: says when the legal adviser, regulator or law enforcement must be involved, in general terms; protects evidence for later use.
