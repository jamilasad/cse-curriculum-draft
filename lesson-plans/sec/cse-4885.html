1.3 Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  1. Produce a risk register of at least eight risks for a given case, each with asset, threat, vulnerability, likelihood, impact, owner and treatment, and explain the scoring.
  2. Given a policy statement, write two testable controls and name the evidence an auditor would ask for.
  3. Read a cloud identity policy in JSON, say who can do what, and rewrite it to least privilege.
  4. Run a configuration scanner on a supplied infrastructure-as-code project, fix three findings and explain each.
  5. State a recovery time objective and a recovery point objective for a given service and choose a backup approach that meets them.

Copied from the outline without change.

2. Class pattern (assumption to confirm)

  • Theory: 3 contact hours a week as two 90-minute classes (A and B): 28 classes in 14 teaching weeks.
  • Lab: one 2-hour session a week: 14 sessions.
  • Mid-term and final are held outside the 14 teaching weeks (mid-term after week 7) unless the outline says otherwise.
  • The department may re-split theory into three 60-minute classes; README section 3 gives the mapping.

Course-specific notes:

  • Two campus days. Semester 8 is timetabled on two days (framework section 10). Class A, class B and the lab are placed on those two days, with the lab on one of them. The outline asks the teacher to agree the two days with the Industry Practice coordinator before the semester starts.
  • Weeks 1–6: remote. Each theory class runs as a live online session in its timetabled slot and is recorded in full. Short recorded explainers (10–20 minutes) are posted before some classes. A student on a six-week placement block who cannot join live watches the recording in the same week and completes the online version of the formative check by Thursday night. Labs in weeks 1–6 are marked "(Remote)": document work that needs only a laptop and a spreadsheet, run as a live online clinic in the scheduled slot, with the lab room open for students who are on campus. Remote lab deliverables are due by Thursday 23:59 of the same week, not at the end of the session, so that block students can do them after work. No marked test, quiz or viva is held in weeks 1–6.
  • Weeks 7–14: on campus. All four Level 0 quizzes, both supervised practical tests (lab weeks 9 and 13), the project presentation and the viva (week 14) are on campus.
  • Mid-term. The mid-term covers weeks 1–7 and is held on campus after week 7. This depends on the examination office placing the semester-8 mid-term after the six-week block (section 9, issue 3).
  • Order of lab and theory. In two places the outline's lab runs one week ahead of the matching theory topic: asset inventory (lab 1, theory week 2) and awareness (lab 12, theory week 13). The lab sheets for those weeks carry a short primer, and the theory classes then use the students' lab work as examples.
  • Standards. ISO/IEC 27001, 27002, 27005 and ISO 22301 are paid documents; the library holds one copy of each. The teacher teaches them from their own summary slides and does not copy or circulate the text. Students on placement in weeks 1–6 cannot reach the library copy (section 9, issue 8).

3.2 Classes

The two teaching cases are used all semester (Appendix E):

  • Case A: a fictional agent-banking start-up (class exercises and labs 1–7).
  • Case B: a fictional 60-person software exporter preparing for a foreign client's security review, with its cloud environment supplied as code (project, cloud classes).

All figures in worked examples are illustrative and are labelled so on the slides.

Week 1 (remote)

Class 1 (Week 1, A): Why security is managed formally; who decides

  • Mode: Blended: live online session in the timetabled slot, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Welcome. How the course runs beside Industry Practice: remote weeks 1–6, campus weeks 7–14, where the recordings and deliverables are, Thursday-night deadlines in weeks 1–6. All course materials are already published (outline note).
    • 10–30 min: Mini-lecture: why organisations manage security formally. Security decisions spend money and accept risk, so they belong to management, not only to the IT team. Short examples of what goes wrong when nobody owns a decision: backups that nobody tests; staff accounts that nobody removes.
    • 30–50 min: The roles: board, senior management, security officer, risk owners, asset owners, staff. The three lines model: first line (operations that own and manage risk), second line (risk and compliance functions), third line (internal audit, independent).
    • 50–70 min: Online breakout groups of four or five. Each group gets six Case A decisions (for example: who approves an agent's access to customer records; who accepts the risk of having no backup site; who checks that access reviews happen). For each, name the role that decides and the line it belongs to.
    • 70–82 min: Groups report in the main room; the teacher corrects and explains.
    • 82–90 min: Formative check; preview of class 2.
  • TLA: Short lecture with worked examples; online breakout groups with a role sheet; whole-class feedback.
  • Materials and readings: Whitman and Mattord, Management of Information Security (security governance and the roles of the security function). Case A case sheet; slides; plain-English glossary (teacher-made).
  • Formative check: Online poll, ungraded: five statements ("Internal audit tests whether the access review is done"; "The branch manager owns the risk of cash-handling errors" ...); students choose first, second or third line. Block students answer the same poll form by Thursday.
  • Homework: Read the Case A sheet fully. List the five assets you think matter most to the business and say why in one line each. AI Level 2 (log any use in T6).

Class 2 (Week 1, B): Policy, standard, procedure, guideline; assurance; confidentiality

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap poll on the three lines.
    • 10–30 min: The document hierarchy with one worked example on passwords: a policy statement (what and why, approved by management), a standard (length, multi-factor authentication), a procedure (the steps to reset a password), a guideline (advice on passphrases).
    • 30–45 min: Sorting exercise in the chat or an online form: twelve sentences; students label each as policy, standard, procedure or guideline. Teacher reviews the disputed ones.
    • 45–65 min: Who demands assurance and in what form: regulators (inspections, returns, guidelines), clients (questionnaires, contract clauses, audit reports), auditors (evidence of controls). What each one actually asks to see.
    • 65–80 min: Confidentiality and professional conduct: client and employer information stays inside the engagement; the course boundary: no Industry Practice host data without the host's written consent and with names removed; no client or host documents in an external AI service. Link to the agreement signed in lab 1 (T1, schedule D).
    • 80–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Worked example; sorting exercise; short case discussion on what a client asks to see.
  • Materials and readings: Whitman and Mattord (policy, standards and practices). T1 schedule D (README). Slides.
  • Formative check: Online form, ungraded: classify five new sentences; and one line: "What must you have before you use any information from your placement host in this course?"
  • Homework: For Case A, write one policy statement, one standard and one procedure on backup (half a page in all). AI Level 2; do it unaided first.

Week 2 (remote)

Class 3 (Week 2, A): Scope and context; asset inventory; data classification

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO2, PO10)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Two anonymous student backup homework answers on screen; class says which sentence is the policy and which is the procedure.
    • 10–30 min: Risk vocabulary: asset, threat, vulnerability, likelihood, impact, risk, risk owner. Scope and context: what is in and out, the business objectives, who the risk owner is, which contracts and rules apply in general terms.
    • 30–50 min: Asset inventory: information, systems, people, suppliers, premises. Each asset has an owner and a location (on premises, cloud, supplier). Worked example: the first eight rows of a Case A asset register.
    • 50–65 min: Data classification: four levels (public, internal, confidential, restricted) with a handling rule for each. Worked examples: agent PINs, copies of customers' identity documents, transaction records, the marketing brochure.
    • 65–82 min: Breakout groups: classify ten Case A data items, name an owner for each, and write one handling rule for the "restricted" level.
    • 82–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Short lecture; worked example; group classification task; comparison with the asset registers students built in lab 1.
  • Materials and readings: Wheeler, Security Risk Management (risk vocabulary, scoping and asset profiling); NIST SP 800-30 (the "prepare for assessment" step). Asset register template and scales (Appendix D).
  • Formative check: Online poll: four data items; students choose the classification level. Ungraded.
  • Homework: Read the part of NIST SP 800-30 that describes threat sources and vulnerabilities. Bring three threats to Case A, each in one line. AI Level 2.

Class 4 (Week 2, B): Threats, vulnerabilities, scales and the risk matrix

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO2, PO10)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap; collect threats from the homework in the chat.
    • 10–25 min: Threat sources (adversarial, accidental, structural, environmental, as in NIST SP 800-30) and vulnerabilities. The standard risk sentence: "Because of [vulnerability], [threat] may [event], causing [impact] to [asset]."
    • 25–45 min: Likelihood and impact scales from 1 to 5 written in the words of the case: impact in money, customers affected, regulator attention and hours of downtime; likelihood in expected frequency. Worked example on two Case A risks.
    • 45–60 min: The risk matrix and its weaknesses: ordinal numbers multiplied as if they were measurements; many different risks squeezed into the same cell; people read the same scale differently. Hubbard and Seiersen's critique in outline.
    • 60–75 min: Process models compared: ISO/IEC 27005 and NIST SP 800-30 (prepare, conduct, communicate, maintain). Where the register fits.
    • 75–85 min: Breakout pairs: each student scores three Case A risks alone, then the pair compares and writes down why their scores differ.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Short lecture; worked scoring example; independent scoring followed by pair comparison (a small calibration exercise).
  • Materials and readings: Hubbard and Seiersen, How to Measure Anything in Cybersecurity Risk (the problems with risk matrices); NIST SP 800-30; ISO/IEC 27005 (teacher's summary of the library copy); Wheeler.
  • Formative check: Each student writes one Case A risk in the standard sentence in the online form. Ungraded; the teacher reads a sample aloud next class.
  • Homework: Write five Case A risks in the standard sentence, ready for lab 2. AI Level 2; do it unaided first (the week-9 test is Level 0).

Week 3 (remote)

Class 5 (Week 3, A): Treatment, appetite, residual risk, the register

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO2, PO10)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap: one student risk sentence improved live.
    • 10–30 min: The four treatments (reduce, transfer, avoid, accept) with a Case A example of each. Who may accept a risk, and why it must be written down.
    • 30–45 min: Risk appetite and tolerance; residual risk after treatment; why residual risk is never zero.
    • 45–60 min: Keeping the register current: owner, review date, triggers for re-assessment (new system, incident, new supplier), sign-off.
    • 60–80 min: Breakout groups: for four Case A risks, choose a treatment, estimate the residual score and justify both in two lines.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Worked examples; group decision task; teacher challenge questions ("Who signs this acceptance?").
  • Materials and readings: Wheeler (risk treatment and risk acceptance); Whitman and Mattord (risk management). Register template (Appendix D).
  • Formative check: Online poll: four short scenarios; students choose the treatment. Ungraded.
  • Homework: Add treatment, residual score, owner and review date to your lab-2 register. AI Level 2.

Class 6 (Week 3, B): Loss expectancy; cost and benefit of a control; supplier risk

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO1 (PO2, PO10)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap.
    • 10–30 min: Single loss expectancy (asset value × exposure factor), annualised rate of occurrence, annualised loss expectancy (SLE × ARO). Value of a control: ALE before − ALE after − annual cost of the control. One worked example with illustrative figures.
    • 30–45 min: Students compute two short problems in breakout pairs; answers posted and checked.
    • 45–55 min: Limits of the method: the rate is a guess. Hubbard and Seiersen's idea of estimating a range instead of a single number (the course stretch goal).
    • 55–75 min: Supplier risk: Case A's core-banking partner, cloud provider and SMS gateway. What to ask a supplier, contract clauses in general terms (security duties, breach notice, right to audit), and the supplier entry in the register.
    • 75–85 min: Breakout: name three Case A suppliers and one risk for each.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Worked calculation; paired practice; short case discussion on suppliers.
  • Materials and readings: Whitman and Mattord (cost-benefit analysis of controls); Hubbard and Seiersen (estimating ranges); Wheeler (third-party risk).
  • Formative check: One ALE calculation in the online form, with the decision "buy the control or not". Ungraded.
  • Homework: Compute ALE before and after a control for two Case A risks with the figures on the case sheet (needed in lab 3). AI Level 2; do it unaided first.

Week 4 (remote)

Class 7 (Week 4, A): Kinds of control; writing a policy that can be audited

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap of treatments: "reduce" means a control.
    • 10–30 min: The control grid: preventive, detective, corrective against administrative, technical, physical. Two Case A examples in each cell.
    • 30–50 min: What makes a policy auditable: who does what, how often, and what record it leaves. Vague and testable versions side by side: "Access must be appropriate" becomes "The head of operations reviews every user's access to the agent portal each quarter and signs the review list; removals are completed within five working days."
    • 50–70 min: Individual task: rewrite three vague sentences as testable controls and name the evidence for each.
    • 70–82 min: Breakout pairs: peer review against a four-question checklist (owner? frequency? action? evidence?).
    • 82–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Worked before-and-after examples; individual writing; peer review with a checklist.
  • Materials and readings: Whitman and Mattord (developing security policy); Davis and Schiller, IT Auditing (how auditors test a control); NIST SP 800-53 (control families as examples).
  • Formative check: Online poll: place six controls in the grid. Ungraded.
  • Homework: Read the access-control and backup policy skeletons for lab 4. AI Level 2.

Class 8 (Week 4, B): The core policy set

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap.
    • 10–40 min: Six policies, five minutes each: acceptable use, access control, data classification, backup, change management, incident response. For each: purpose, three key clauses, the usual evidence.
    • 40–70 min: Jigsaw in breakout groups: each group drafts three testable clauses for one policy for Case A.
    • 70–85 min: Groups present; the teacher marks each clause "testable" or "not yet" and says why.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Short lecture; jigsaw group drafting; teacher feedback on live examples.
  • Materials and readings: Whitman and Mattord (issue-specific and system-specific policies); NIST SP 800-53 (access control, configuration management, contingency planning and incident response families).
  • Formative check: Exit question in the form: for one clause your group wrote, name the evidence an auditor would ask for. Ungraded.
  • Homework: Improve your group's clauses. Note one clause on the use of AI tools for the acceptable-use policy (we return to it in week 14). AI Level 2.

Week 5 (remote)

Class 9 (Week 5, A): ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap.
    • 10–35 min: The management-system idea: scope, leadership, risk assessment and treatment, statement of applicability, internal audit, management review, continual improvement (plan, do, check, act). How certification works in outline: an accredited body audits, issues a certificate for a scope, then surveillance audits.
    • 35–55 min: Annex A at overview level: its four themes (organisational, people, physical, technological) and the guidance in ISO/IEC 27002.
    • 55–75 min: Statement-of-applicability exercise in breakout groups: for eight controls, decide whether each applies to Case A and write the reason.
    • 75–85 min: Discussion: a certificate shows a working management system for a stated scope; it does not prove the organisation is secure.
    • 85–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Short lecture from the teacher's own summary; group exercise; discussion.
  • Materials and readings: ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO/IEC 27002 (library copy; teacher's summary slides only, no copies of the text); Whitman and Mattord (security management models).
  • Formative check: Online poll: three true-or-false statements about certification and scope. Ungraded.
  • Homework: Read the overview of the CIS Critical Security Controls (free document). AI Level 2.

Class 10 (Week 5, B): NIST CSF; CIS Controls; choosing and mapping

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):
    • 0–10 min: Recap.
    • 10–30 min: The NIST Cybersecurity Framework functions (in version 2.0: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover); profiles in outline.
    • 30–50 min: The CIS Critical Security Controls: eighteen controls with safeguards, and implementation groups; the first group (IG1) as basic hygiene for a small firm.
    • 50–60 min: Choosing a framework: who asks for it (client, regulator), the firm's size, the cost of certification. Mapping between them so that one control set answers several demands.
    • 60–80 min: Mapping exercise: map six Case A controls to an Annex A theme, a CSF function and a CIS Control.
    • 80–90 min: Formative check.
  • TLA: Short lecture; mapping exercise in pairs; comparison of answers on a shared sheet.
  • Materials and readings: NIST Cybersecurity Framework; CIS Critical Security Controls; Whitman and Mattord.
  • Formative check: Poll: "Restoring the database from backup after an incident" belongs to which CSF function? Two more items. Ungraded.
  • Homework: Bring your six mapped controls to lab 5. AI Level 2.

Week 6 (remote)

Class 11 (Week 6, A): PCI DSS; SOC 2 reports; COBIT

  • Mode: Blended: live online, recorded.
  • CLO (PO): CLO2 (PO3, PO9); CLO3 (PO4, PO9)
  • Contents and timing (90 min):

6.5 Sample questions with answers or marking notes

Remember (mid-term Q1, 1 mark). Name the functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. Answer: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. All six: 1 mark; four or five: 0.5.

Understand (mid-term Q2a, 3 marks). Give two weaknesses of a 5×5 risk matrix and one way to reduce each. Marking notes: any two of: scores are ordinal numbers multiplied as if they were measurements; very different risks fall into the same cell; people read the scale words differently; the top and bottom of a band hide large differences. Ways to reduce: define each level in the words and units of the case; calibrate by having two people score the same risks and discuss; estimate important risks as ranges (Hubbard and Seiersen). 1 mark per weakness with its remedy; 1 mark for a correct example.

Apply (mid-term Q3a, 5 marks). Illustrative figures. A breach of Case A's customer records would cost about BDT 4,000,000 in response, compensation and lost business; a breach would expose about half of that value; such a breach is expected about once in three years without new controls. A control (multi-factor authentication plus quarterly access review) costs BDT 150,000 a year and is expected to cut the rate to once in twenty years. Is it worth buying? Answer: SLE = 4,000,000 × 0.5 = 2,000,000. ALE before = 2,000,000 × 0.33 ≈ 660,000. ALE after = 2,000,000 × 0.05 = 100,000. Value = 660,000 − 100,000 − 150,000 ≈ 410,000 a year, so buy. Marks: SLE 1; ALE before 1; ALE after 1; value 1; decision with one stated limitation (the rates are estimates) 1. Accept 0.3 or 0.33 for the rate if used consistently.

6.9 CLO attainment: calculation and records

  • Method (README section 8). Every question, rubric row and test task is tagged with one CLO, as in the tables above. For each student and CLO: CLO score = marks obtained on items tagged with that CLO ÷ maximum marks of those items × 100. For attainment, every quiz taken counts, not only the best three. An item missed with an accepted reason is left out of both the obtained and the maximum marks; an item missed without reason counts as zero.
  • Thresholds (proposal for the department to confirm). A student attains a CLO at 50%. The course attains a CLO when at least 60% of the students who sat the final assessment attain it.
  • Separate codes. Attainment is computed separately for CSE-4885 (theory) and CSE-4886 (lab), and the package report shows both side by side.
  • PO contribution. Each CLO's attainment is reported against its POs (PO2, PO3, PO4, PO5, PO9, PO10) for the department's programme-level calculation.
  • Record sheet. One spreadsheet per section: one row per student; one column per assessed item with its CLO tag and maximum marks; computed CLO scores; attained yes or no; a summary row with the percentage of students attaining each CLO.
  • Evidence kept in the course file. Question papers with CLO and Bloom tags and model answers; test variants and marking schemes; three marked scripts per assessment (high, middle, low); sample project reports; viva forms; the signed agreements register; the host consent letters; sample T6 logs; the attainment sheet; the teacher's end-of-course review.
  • Closing the loop. The end-of-course review names every CLO below target, the likely cause (for example, many students lose marks on reading identity policies) and one change for the next offering (an extra practice sheet, a worked example, a moved lab). The next teacher reads it before week 1.

7. Support for weaker students

The intake is about 15% strong, 60% mid-level and 25% below average. The outline notes that mid-level students often do well in this course, because the work rewards care and clear writing more than fast programming. The plan uses that.

  1. A template and one worked example for every document.
