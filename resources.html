Resources and planning

Equipment, labs and faculty the draft needs, from framework section 16, the track files and the Resources section of each outline.

Framework section 16. Resource implications

  • Faculty. About 46 full-time CSE teachers, most of them lecturers. New or heavily revised courses (cloud, AI application engineering, security operations, infrastructure, verification) need training time or industry adjuncts before first delivery.
  • Labs. The existing computer labs cover every software course. The microprocessor labs need modern microcontroller kits, logic analysers and sensors. The EMB track additionally needs FPGA boards. The SEC track needs one department server, or a few repurposed PCs, for a shared log-analysis platform and an evidence store. The INF track needs a few managed switches and routers (or a network emulator), two or three hypervisor hosts, a teaching rack built from retired servers, and a small budget for cloud sandbox accounts so that students without a payment card can take part.
  • Purchases. The department has confirmed that the equipment above can be bought.
  • Fewer lab sections. Total lab and studio contact falls, which eases room and teacher load.
  • Industry Practice. A coordinator and a partner list are needed a year before the first cohort reaches semester 8.

Equipment and lab environment by track

The track overview has no separate equipment section; see the hardware lines below.

AID: AI & Data

Compute, tools and AI use

  • Compute. The department has ordinary PC labs and no GPU cluster. The labs are planned around free notebook services, small models, parameter-efficient methods and hosted APIs under a spending cap. Each lab plan states what it runs on. Nothing in the track needs a model trained from scratch at scale.
  • Tools. Content is vendor-neutral. Model names, library versions and free quotas change every year, so the outlines name families and practices and give current tools only as examples. For each offering the instructor publishes a one-page list of the tools and models used that semester.
  • AI use. Lab work in CSE-3673, CSE-4773 and CSE-4883 is at Level 3: an AI system is the thing being built and measured. CSE-4783 works on data rather than on AI systems, so its lab work is at Level 2, except week 11, where AI-written SQL is the thing measured. Take-home assignments are at Level 2. AI coding assistants may be used in lab work under the Level 2 conditions (usage logged, output tested, the student defends every line). Exercises marked "by hand" are Level 1. All quizzes, written examinations, supervised practical tests and vivas are Level 0.

SEC: Cybersecurity

Lab environment and the legal and ethical boundary

All four labs use one shared environment, called the track range in the outlines below. Each lab PC runs department-supplied virtual machines in VirtualBox on a host-only network with no route to the campus network or the internet. Heavier shared services (the SIEM server, the evidence file store) run on one department server or a few repurposed PCs. Targets are restored from snapshots after each session. Students may run the same images on their own laptops under the same rules.

Accessing, scanning or interfering with a computer system without its owner's permission is a criminal offence in Bangladesh, as it is in most countries, and good intentions are not a defence. Written authorisation with a defined scope is what separates a security test from an offence. Each course therefore:

  • states this boundary in its first week;
  • requires a signed rules-of-engagement agreement before lab access, listing the permitted targets (the range's addresses and supplied images), the prohibited actions (any scan or attack on the campus network, other people's devices or any internet host; removing samples or evidence sets; using lab techniques elsewhere), the data-handling rules, and the consequence of a breach (loss of lab access and referral under the university's disciplinary rules);
  • runs offensive exercises only against systems the department owns.

For this reason the header table of each package below has one row that the general template does not have, "Legal and ethical boundary".

INF: Systems, Networks & Infrastructure

Lab environment and equipment

Most of the track runs in the existing computer labs: virtual machines in VirtualBox or KVM on lab PCs (Rocky Linux or AlmaLinux, and Ubuntu Server), with a department server hosting shared machines for PCs too small to run three VMs; network emulation in Cisco Packet Tracer for Cisco-style command practice and in GNS3 or containerlab with FRRouting and Linux hosts for larger and multi-vendor topologies; and free cloud tiers and education programmes for CSE-4787, used with spending alerts. The lab network segment has no route to the campus network, so that a student's stray DHCP server or switching loop disturbs nobody else.

AI use. All four labs run at Level 2: AI as pair programmer, with use logged, output tested and every line defended. Quizzes, written examinations, supervised practical tests and vivas are at Level 0. In each lab the first weeks are done unaided, so that the student has a mental model before an assistant writes anything.

Where assistants help in this field: explaining tool messages, timing reports and fault registers; writing testbench stimulus, build scripts and boilerplate (UVM classes, MQTT clients, dashboards); summarising a datasheet section that the student then checks against the datasheet.

Where their output is especially dangerous:

  • HDL that simulates but does not synthesise, or synthesises into different hardware. Delays and initial blocks in design code, incomplete assignments that create latches, blocking assignments in clocked blocks, a signal driven from two places. The simulation passes; the board does not work.
  • Timing and clock-domain crossings. An assistant cannot see the timing report. It suggests constraints with invented syntax, or a timing exception that hides a real violation. A missing synchroniser fails rarely and at random on hardware, and never in simulation.
  • Concurrency in firmware. Variables shared between an interrupt and a task with no protection, blocking calls inside interrupt handlers, guessed stack sizes and priorities. The code runs on the bench and fails under load or after some hours.
  • Invented hardware facts. Register addresses, bit positions, pin numbers and library functions that do not exist for this chip. A wrong register write can lock a board.
  • Security shortcuts in connected devices. Certificate checking switched off to make the connection work; keys in source code.
  • Tests that cannot fail. A reference model derived from the design it is meant to check; assertions that pass vacuously.

The rule in every lab: generated work is accepted only with the evidence that fits the danger. For HDL that is a clean lint and synthesis report and met timing; for firmware, a table of every shared resource and its protection, and the datasheet page for every register; for a test, a run in which it fails on a deliberately broken design.

EMB is the only track that cannot run without new hardware. The other tracks use the existing computer labs; INF and SEC can use a virtual lab in place of a rack of switches and routers (framework, section 16). Within EMB the track-specific purchase is the FPGA boards and add-ons, the Wi-Fi boards and the LoRa modules. The debug-capable microcontroller boards, logic analysers and sensor kits are the same ones the core lab CSE-3548 needs; they should be bought once and timetabled for both. CSE-4890 needs no hardware at all. The equipment serves one section at a time, so a second section of 24 needs a second timetable slot, not a second set.

Board choices are left to the department. The criteria that matter: a free tool chain that runs on the lab PCs, a working debugger, local availability of replacements, and good documentation in English.
