Rationale and what changed

In the studio students called language models through an API. This course explains what is inside those models, how they are trained and adapted, and why they fail, so that graduates can fine-tune, debug and choose models and not only prompt them. It replaces the 2022 courses Neural Network and Fuzzy System and Pattern Recognition and Image Processing. Convolutional and recurrent networks share one week, because the transformer now does most of the work in text and much of it in vision; fuzzy systems, classical image processing, GAN training, reinforcement learning and the mathematics of diffusion are left out. Training large models from scratch is also left out: the department cannot do it and graduates will rarely be asked to. Students write backpropagation, a training loop and attention by hand once, then work with libraries and pre-trained models. Bangla text is used throughout, because it shows what low-resource conditions do to tokenisers, embeddings and model quality.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Write a PyTorch training loop without a high-level trainer (data loader, model, loss, optimiser, training and evaluation modes, validation) and train a small classifier with it.
  • Look at a pair of training and validation curves and name the likely fault (learning rate too high, underfitting, overfitting, leakage) and one fix.
  • Compute scaled dot-product attention for three tokens by hand, and say what each block in a transformer diagram does.
  • Load a pre-trained encoder and its tokeniser, fine-tune it on a small Bangla or English text-classification set, and report accuracy and macro-F1 against a majority-class and a TF-IDF baseline.
  • For a described use case, say whether to prompt, retrieve or fine-tune, and give two reasons.

Stretch goals

  • Build a small GPT-style decoder entirely from scratch, train it on a Bangla corpus and report loss and samples.
  • Implement a LoRA layer by hand, match the PEFT library's parameter count and result, and study the effect of rank.
  • Quantise a small open-weight language model, run it on a lab PC, and measure quality, memory and tokens per second against the full-precision model.

Weekly plan — theory

WeekTopicsCLO
1From the core course to deep networks: layers, activations, loss functions; the computational graph; backpropagation derived for a two-layer network; automatic differentiation1
2Optimisation in practice: mini-batch SGD, momentum, Adam and AdamW; learning-rate schedules and warm-up; initialisation; vanishing and exploding gradients; gradient clipping; effect of batch size1
3Making training work: overfitting, weight decay, dropout, early stopping, data augmentation; batch and layer normalisation; residual connections; reading loss curves; a debugging routine (overfit one batch first); planning experiments under a compute budget1, 2
4Convolutional and sequence models in brief: convolution, pooling, receptive field, the residual block, transfer learning for images; recurrent networks, what they did and why transformers replaced them1, 4
5Text as data and the idea of attention: sub-word tokenisation (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece); how Bangla script is tokenised (Unicode normalisation, conjuncts, tokens per word); token embeddings; queries, keys and values; scaled dot-product attention worked by hand3
6The transformer block: multi-head attention; causal and padding masks; positional information (sinusoidal, learned, rotary in outline); feed-forward layer, residual paths, layer normalisation3
7The full transformer and pre-training: encoder, decoder and encoder–decoder forms; counting parameters; cost in sequence length; the context window; masked-token and next-token objectives; what each model family is good for3, 4
8Fine-tuning: what transfers from pre-training; multilingual models and where Bangla sits in them; classification, token-labelling and sequence-to-sequence heads; learning-rate choice, forgetting, small-data practice; text metrics (macro-F1, exact match, ROUGE, BLEU, chrF) and their limits for Bangla; model cards and licences4
9Parameter-efficient fine-tuning and efficiency: adapters, prompt and prefix tuning, LoRA (the low-rank update, rank, scaling, target layers); quantisation to 8 and 4 bits and fine-tuning a quantised model; distillation in outline; memory arithmetic: what fits on a free notebook GPU4
10Embeddings and retrieval: sentence embeddings and contrastive training; similarity search and approximate nearest neighbours; bi-encoders and re-rankers; embedding quality for Bangla, romanised Bangla and code-mixed text; retrieval-augmented generation seen from the inside4, 6
11How large language models are built and generate text: pre-training at scale, instruction tuning and preference tuning in outline; decoding (greedy, temperature, top-k, top-p); in-context learning; how the context window is used; what public benchmarks do and do not show5
12How they fail: hallucination and why next-token training produces it; prompt sensitivity; stale knowledge; weak counting and arithmetic; bias; memorised training data; lower quality in low-resource languages; probing for failures; mitigations (grounding, constrained output, verification, abstaining)5
13Generative models beyond text, conceptually: autoencoders and latent spaces; diffusion (adding noise, learning to remove it, sampling, text conditioning and guidance, working in a latent space) without the probabilistic derivation, with a classroom demonstration of a pre-trained pipeline; contrastive image–text models and multimodal language models; synthetic media, provenance and copyright5
14Choosing an approach: prompting, retrieval, fine-tuning or a small specialised model, judged by task, data, quality, cost, latency, privacy and upkeep; worked cases (Bangla support-ticket routing, document question answering for a bank, product-description generation); what CSE-4883 adds; review6

Weekly plan — lab

CSE-4774 Deep Learning & Generative AI Lab · Lab · 1 credits · 2 contact hours a week

Compute: the department has no GPU cluster. Every exercise is sized for one free notebook GPU session (training runs of about 20 minutes or less) or the CPU of a lab PC. Models are small: networks of a few million parameters trained from scratch, pre-trained encoders of roughly 100–300 million parameters fine-tuned on a few thousand examples, and quantised small open-weight language models for inference. Wherever a larger model is adapted, a parameter-efficient method is used. Free quotas change and sessions disconnect, so students save checkpoints, the instructor keeps a trained checkpoint for every lab, and model weights are cached on a lab file server before class. Hosted APIs are used in week 12 on a free tier or under a spending cap for each pair.

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term (weeks 1–7) and final, 80 marks. At least half the marks are application and analysis: one forward pass and one gradient step of a tiny network by hand; attention weights for three tokens from given query, key and value vectors; the parameter count of a given transformer layer and the trainable parameters LoRA adds at a given rank; the memory needed to load a model at 16-bit and at 4-bit precision; the fault shown by a pair of loss curves; the defect in a printed PyTorch excerpt (gradients not cleared, wrong mode at evaluation, a mask that lets the model see the next token); a case asking for a choice between prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning under stated limits of data, budget and latency. Recall carries at most a fifth of the marks. Continuous assessment, 20: four Level 0 quizzes, best three counted (10); a handwritten problem set on backpropagation and attention (5) and a two-page reading note on one paper or model card (5).

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30; two supervised practical tests 30 (15 each, offline documentation allowed); lab project 25; individual viva 15. The project, in pairs, adapts a pre-trained model to a Bangla-language or locally relevant task (news or support-ticket classification, code-mixed sentiment, named entities, question answering over Bangla documents, handwritten-character recognition). It compares the adapted model with a simple baseline and a prompted model on a held-out test set, stays within free compute and the spending cap, and is documented with a model card. A student who cannot explain their own submitted work in the viva does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Textbooks: Prince, Understanding Deep Learning; Zhang, Lipton, Li and Smola, Dive into Deep Learning; Raschka, Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch); Jurafsky and Martin, Speech and Language Processing; Tunstall, von Werra and Wolf, Natural Language Processing with Transformers; Alammar and Grootendorst, Hands-On Large Language Models. Reference: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning. Papers: Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need"; Hu et al., "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models".
  • Software (free; current examples, reviewed at each offering): PyTorch and torchvision; Hugging Face Transformers, Datasets, Tokenizers, PEFT and Diffusers; sentence-transformers; FAISS; llama.cpp or Ollama for quantised models on CPU; free notebook services such as Google Colab and Kaggle Notebooks.
  • Models and data, as examples that will change: a multilingual encoder such as XLM-RoBERTa and a Bangla encoder such as BanglaBERT; public Bangla datasets such as NumtaDB (handwritten digits) and the Bengali part of XL-Sum (news summaries); a classification set chosen or labelled by the class. Check each licence before use.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Compute is the main practical risk. Run every lab yourself on the free tier the week before, keep a CPU-only fallback, and never plan a session around a large download over the lab network.
  • Attention is where mid-level students get lost. Work a three-token example with numbers on the board, then in a spreadsheet, before any code. The week 5 lab repeats it.
  • AI assistants write training loops and fine-tuning scripts fluently, so the amount of code is no evidence of learning. Marks go to experiment logs, diagnoses and comparisons, and the Level 0 tests check the training loop and the hand computations.
  • Model names date quickly. Teach families (encoder, decoder, encoder–decoder; open-weight and hosted) and update the examples at each offering. With Bangla data expect mixed scripts, romanised text and inconsistent Unicode; treat normalisation as part of the task, and do not let students scrape private social-media content.
