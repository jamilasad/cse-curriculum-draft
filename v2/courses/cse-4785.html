Rationale and what changed

Course I ends when an analyst escalates an alert. This course starts there: contain the incident, collect evidence properly, work out what happened, recover and report. Banks, MFS providers and telecom operators keep incident-response functions and are expected by their regulators and clients to document how incidents are handled, so the skill is hired directly. Nothing in the 2022 syllabus covered it. Students first verify hashes, walk a file system and build a timeline by hand with command-line tools and a spreadsheet, and only then use the automated ingest and timeline tools. Left out: mobile-device forensics, reverse engineering of malware beyond triage, commercial forensic suites, and courtroom procedure beyond the general principles of admissible evidence.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  1. Image a storage device or virtual disk without altering it, compute and verify its hash, and fill in a chain-of-custody record.
  2. From a supplied disk image, recover a deleted file and list user logons and recently opened files with their timestamps, using Autopsy or the Sleuth Kit.
  3. From a memory image, list processes and network connections with Volatility 3 and point out one suspicious process with a reason.
  4. Build a timeline of at least ten events in UTC from two sources, each event citing its evidence.
  5. Write a one-page incident summary that states what happened, what is confirmed, what is unknown and what to do next.

Stretch goals

  • Write a YARA rule for a lab sample family and test it for false positives against a clean file set.
  • Collect triage data from several range machines at once with Velociraptor or a script, and compare the result with manual collection.
  • Examine a case where the attacker cleared logs and changed timestamps, and show which evidence survived.

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term and final (80). At least half the marks are application and analysis; recall is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • A scenario at a stated time of day: choose between containment options and justify the choice by its cost and risk.
  • An excerpt of tool output (a process list from memory, Windows logon events, file timestamps, a Linux authentication log): say what happened and which lines show it.
  • A chain-of-custody record with planted gaps: find them and say why each matters.
  • Events from three sources in different time zones: convert them to UTC and build the timeline.
  • A paragraph from a poor incident report that mixes fact and guess: rewrite it.

Continuous assessment (20): four Level 0 quizzes, best three counted (10); two assignments (10), an individual role sheet and after-action note from the tabletop exercise, and a short written case analysis.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30; two supervised practical tests at Level 0, 30; lab project 25; individual viva 15. Project (teams of two or three): a full case built from a staged compromise in the track range. The evidence set holds a workstation disk image, a memory image, a packet capture, and firewall, SIEM and web-server logs. Teams verify the evidence, analyse it, build the timeline and write an incident report of at most ten pages with a one-page management summary. Teams receive variants of the case with different planted details. A student who cannot explain how a finding in the report was obtained does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Textbooks: Gerard Johansen, Digital Forensics and Incident Response; Jason Luttgens, Matthew Pepe and Kevin Mandia, Incident Response & Computer Forensics; Brian Carrier, File System Forensic Analysis; Michael Hale Ligh and others, The Art of Memory Forensics; Michael Sikorski and Andrew Honig, Practical Malware Analysis (early chapters).
  • Free references: NIST SP 800-61 (incident handling) and NIST SP 800-86 (forensic techniques in incident response).
  • Software (all free): Autopsy, the Sleuth Kit, dc3dd, Guymager, FTK Imager, PhotoRec, RegRipper, Volatility 3, Plaso, Wireshark, Zeek, NetworkMiner, YARA, the Sysinternals suite, CyberChef, Velociraptor; the SIFT Workstation is a ready-made alternative to the department's own image.
  • Hardware: one USB flash drive per pair for imaging. Evidence images are kept under 8 GB so that they fit ordinary lab PCs, and are served from a department file store.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The common failures are pasting tool output as if it were a finding, concluding more than the evidence supports, and time-zone errors. Require two columns in every findings sheet, "evidence" and "what it shows", and UTC everywhere.
  • Good evidence sets take days to build. Script the staged attacks in the range so that variants can be regenerated each year, and count this as preparation load before the first delivery. No live malware is brought into the lab; the department writes harmless programs that imitate persistence, beaconing and file encryption in a test folder.
  • For weaker students: question-led worksheets in weeks 2–6, a one-page reference for each artefact family, and a report template. Discussion in the tabletop may be in Bangla; written output is in English.
  • AI assistants are useful for explaining an event ID or improving the English of a report. They also invent the meaning of artefacts, so every claim is checked against the tool's documentation. Nothing from an evidence set is pasted into an external AI or scanning service; this mirrors the confidentiality rule of real casework.
