Rationale and what changed

This is the programme's only natural-science course. It replaces Physics I, Physics II, Physics II Lab and Chemistry (10.5 credits) with 4 credits. What is kept is the physics that explains how computers and networks work: charge, field and potential (capacitors, memory cells, touch screens), current, magnetism and induction (inductors, transformers, motors), waves and optics (fibre and wireless links), and semiconductors with the quantum ideas behind them (diodes, transistors, LEDs, flash memory). Mechanics, properties of matter, heat and thermodynamics, sound, nuclear physics and all of chemistry are out. Because MATH-1107 has no vector calculus, Gauss's and Ampère's laws are used only in symmetric cases and Maxwell's equations are stated in words. The lab has a second job that no other course does: measurement, uncertainty and the written report of an experiment. Problems are worked by hand first; simulations and curve-fitting tools come afterwards.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Calculate the force and field due to two or three point charges on a line, the capacitance and stored energy of a parallel-plate capacitor, and the resistance of a wire from its dimensions.
  • Give the direction and size of the force on a current in a magnetic field, and of the EMF induced in a coil by a changing field.
  • Convert between frequency and wavelength for any electromagnetic wave, find a critical angle, and work out received power in decibels after stated losses.
  • Explain with a labelled diagram why a p–n junction conducts in one direction and how a MOSFET acts as a switch.
  • In the lab, take a set of readings, plot them with labelled axes and units, fit a straight line, and state the result with an uncertainty.

Stretch goals

  • Write Maxwell's equations in integral form and show how they give the speed of an electromagnetic wave.
  • Use carrier concentrations and the diode equation to predict a measured I–V curve.
  • Explain quantum tunnelling through a thin barrier and relate it to flash memory and to leakage in very small transistors; read an introduction to quantum bits.

Assessment

Theory: mid-term and final (80). The mid-term covers weeks 1–7. At least half the marks are for application and analysis; recall of laws and definitions is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • Calculations with realistic values: the charge stored in a given capacitor, the field across a thin insulating layer, the resistance and heating of a copper track.
  • Explain with a diagram: why a fibre guides light, why a transformer does not work on direct current, why a diode conducts in one direction.
  • Read a graph: the time constant from a discharge curve, the turn-on voltage from an I–V curve, the best operating wavelength from a fibre attenuation curve.
  • A link calculation in decibels: launch power, loss per kilometre and connector losses give the received power.
  • Estimates: the wavelengths of 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz Wi-Fi, and what they imply for antenna size.

Continuous assessment (20). Four 15-minute quizzes, best three counted (10), Level 0. Two assignments (5 each), Level 1. Assignment 1 (weeks 1–7): numerical problems worked by hand, two of them checked in a PhET simulation. Assignment 2 (weeks 8–14): a one-page explanation in the student's own English, with one calculation, of how an allocated device works (touch screen, RFID card, optical-fibre link, LED or flash memory cell).

Lab (100).

  • Weekly lab work and lab record (30): one record per experiment with the data table, graph and result with uncertainty.
  • Two supervised practical tests at the bench, Level 0 (30): weeks 7 and 13; each student works alone.
  • Lab project (25): a small measured investigation done in pairs in weeks 10–14, chosen from a list such as Wi-Fi signal strength against distance and through walls, the field of a bar magnet against distance, the discharge of a large capacitor, or light passing through an increasing number of plastic sheets. Each student writes their own three-page report in English.
  • Individual viva (15): week 14. A student who cannot explain their own record and project does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • David Halliday, Robert Resnick and Jearl Walker, Fundamentals of Physics (main text).
  • Hugh D. Young and Roger A. Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics (alternative).
  • Ben G. Streetman and Sanjay Banerjee, Solid State Electronic Devices (reference for weeks 12–14).
  • PhET Interactive Simulations (free); the phyphox phone app (free) for magnetic-field and sound measurements; a spreadsheet or Python with Matplotlib for graphs.
  • Hardware, mostly already in a Physics laboratory: DC supplies, digital multimeters, resistors and capacitors, coils and bar magnets, a small transformer, laser pointers, acrylic blocks, diffraction gratings, plastic optical fibre, diodes and LEDs, photodiodes or small solar cells.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • EEE-1225 runs in the same semester and uses voltage, capacitors, inductors and the diode before this course explains them, the diode by five weeks. Agree symbols and sign conventions with the EEE teacher in week 1, and keep week 4 to the physics of conduction.
  • Students tend to substitute numbers into formulas with no physical picture. Open each topic with a demonstration or a PhET simulation and a question that asks for a prediction, and put a graph-reading or explain-why question in every quiz. For the weaker quarter, give worked examples that carry the units through every line.
  • Colleagues and students will ask why mechanics and heat are missing. The framework excludes them because no later course uses them. The time goes to weeks 10–14, which are new to most Physics service courses and are the part that CSE courses lean on most.
  • AI assistants solve textbook numericals and write lab reports. The lab mark therefore rests on the student's own readings, the bench tests and the viva. At Level 1 a student may ask an assistant to explain a concept or check units, not to produce the analysis or the report.

About this group of courses: Mathematics, Physics and Electrical Fundamentals

This file holds the outlines for the seven mathematics, physics and electrical courses of the programme. The seventh, MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, was added after review of the first draft. Together they replace about 40 credits of mathematics, physics, chemistry and electrical courses in the 2022 syllabus with 23 credits. A topic is kept only if a later course in the programme uses it, and each outline has a table naming the courses that do. The courses are taught as service courses by the Mathematics, Physics and EEE departments, so the outlines are written for a teacher from outside CSE, with examples, assignments and examination questions drawn from computing.
