Rationale and what changed

Digital Logic Design taught gates, flip-flops and small state machines, mostly on paper and in a first Verilog simulation. This course takes the step to how digital hardware is built in practice: described in a hardware description language, checked in simulation, synthesised, and made to meet timing on a real device. It is the entry course of the track, the prerequisite of CSE-4889 and the base for FPGA and chip-design jobs. It is new with the track; the framework's change table lists no 2022 course that it replaces. The synthesisable subset of SystemVerilog is used for design, and plain Verilog is read where students meet it in existing code. Left out: VHDL, transistor-level and analogue design, ASIC physical design, high-level synthesis, vendor block-diagram design flows, and high-speed interfaces such as DDR memory and gigabit transceivers.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Read a SystemVerilog module of 20–30 lines, draw the hardware it infers and say whether it contains an unintended latch.
  • Write a synthesisable finite-state machine of three to six states from a state diagram, with a synchronous reset, that passes lint with no latch or width warnings.
  • Write a self-checking testbench for a small module (counter, shift register, FIFO) that reports pass or fail without anyone reading the waveform.
  • Given clock-to-output, logic and set-up delays, compute the slack of a path at a stated clock period; find the worst path in a timing report and say where it starts and ends.
  • Take a supplied design from source to a working board: pin and clock constraints, build, programming, demonstration.

Stretch goals

  • A single-cycle RV32I core with memory-mapped LEDs and UART that runs a C program built with the RISC-V GNU toolchain; then a pipelined version.
  • An asynchronous FIFO with Gray-code pointers and a written argument for why it is safe; or a formal proof of the synchronous FIFO's properties with SymbiYosys.
  • Build the project with the open flow (Yosys and nextpnr) where the board allows it, and compare utilisation and timing with the vendor tool.

Assessment

Theory. Written mid-term (weeks 1–7) and final (whole course, weighted to weeks 8–14), 80 marks together. Kinds of question:

  • Given SystemVerilog code, draw the inferred hardware, or complete a timing diagram of its outputs.
  • Find and fix the defects in a module: unintended latch, blocking assignment in a clocked block, missing synchroniser, truncated width, divided clock.
  • From a specification, draw the state diagram and write the RTL for a controller with a small datapath.
  • Timing calculation: maximum frequency and slack from given delays; choose where to place a pipeline register and give the new latency.
  • Choose between two designs and justify: one-hot or binary, synchronous or asynchronous reset, shared or duplicated arithmetic.
  • Recall (at most one-fifth of the marks): definitions such as set-up time, look-up table, metastability.

Continuous assessment, 20: four short in-class quizzes at Level 0 (10), mostly "draw the hardware" and timing arithmetic; two assignments (10), one on reading RTL by hand and one a written timing and clock-crossing review of a supplied design.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30, marked at the bench each week. Two supervised practical tests 30 (weeks 6 and 12, 15 each). Lab project 25, in pairs: one of a UART-controlled peripheral (PWM or servo controller, sensor logger), a VGA game or text terminal, a frequency counter or signal generator, or a single-cycle RV32I subset running a small program. Every project has a self-checking testbench per module, constraints, met timing, a README in English with utilisation and timing figures, and an AI-use log. Individual viva 15 on the student's own lab work and project; a student who cannot explain their own code does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Textbooks: Sarah Harris and David Harris, Digital Design and Computer Architecture: RISC-V Edition; Pong P. Chu, FPGA Prototyping by SystemVerilog Examples; Stuart Sutherland, RTL Modeling with SystemVerilog for Simulation and Synthesis; Samir Palnitkar, Verilog HDL: A Guide to Digital Design and Synthesis (reference for older code).
  • Software, all free: Icarus Verilog (simulation), Verilator (lint and fast simulation), GTKWave, the free edition of the board vendor's tool (for example Vivado Standard or Quartus Prime Lite), Yosys and nextpnr for boards the open flow supports, the RISC-V GNU toolchain for the processor project, Git.
  • Industry norm: commercial simulators and, for chips, commercial synthesis and timing sign-off tools. With the open tools students miss full SystemVerilog support (the open simulators accept the synthesisable subset used here), mixed-language simulation and ASIC timing and power analysis. None of this limits the course.
  • Hardware: a low-cost FPGA board per pair, USB-UART adapter, logic analyser, add-on modules; one oscilloscope and one monitor per two benches.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The main failure is writing an HDL as software: loops expected to run in sequence, variables used as storage, modules "called". Before any code, students draw the block diagram with its registers, and the diagram earns marks. Ask of every block: where are the flip-flops?
  • "Passes in simulation, fails on the board" is nearly always a reset, a divided or unconstrained clock, or an unsynchronised input. Keep a checklist on the wall. Latch and multiple-driver warnings from synthesis are treated as errors.
  • For weaker students: module skeletons with port lists and a working testbench in weeks 2–5, so that the first success comes early; one state-machine template used all semester; a glossary of the English terms in tool reports; pairs at the bench but individual tests.
  • Assistants write plausible Verilog quickly, and much of it is in testbench style: delays, initial blocks, incomplete case statements. It simulates, then fails synthesis or becomes different hardware. They cannot see the timing report and they invent constraint syntax. Accept generated RTL only with clean lint, no latch warnings, met timing, and a student who can draw it in the viva. Good uses: explaining tool messages and timing paths, drafting stimulus, writing build scripts.
