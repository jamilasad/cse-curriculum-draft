Rationale and what changed

Machine learning, data analysis and performance measurement all rest on probability and statistics, so the 2-credit course of the 2022 syllabus becomes a 3-credit course. The emphasis moves from formula and table work to reasoning under uncertainty, simulation, and the analysis of real data in Python. Each idea is first computed by hand on a small table, then simulated, then applied to a dataset with library functions. Left out: moment generating functions, a long catalogue of distributions, continuous joint densities (MATH-1107 has no double integrals; MATH-2409 takes them up in semester 4), analysis of variance, nonparametric tests, and reading values from printed tables beyond one exercise. Examples come from computing: hash collisions, retries, packet loss, response times, server failures and A/B tests.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Solve a conditional-probability problem with a tree or a table, including a Bayes' theorem problem with a low base rate.
  • Compute the expectation and variance of a discrete random variable from its table, and find a normal probability by standardising.
  • Compute a 95% confidence interval for a mean or a proportion and say correctly, in one sentence, what it means.
  • Read the printed output of a two-sample test or a simple regression, state the conclusion in plain words, and name one assumption it depends on.
  • Read a short Python script that loads a dataset, summarises it and draws a histogram, and say what each line produces.

Stretch goals

  • Derive the maximum-likelihood estimators for the Poisson and exponential distributions, and show the bias of the uncorrected sample variance by simulation.
  • Simulate a single-server queue, compare the measured waiting time with the textbook formula, and relate its long-run behaviour to the steady state of a Markov chain from MATH-1209.
  • Analyse an A/B test in the Bayesian way with Beta priors and compare the answer with the two-proportion test.

Weekly plan — lab

None. This is a theory-only course with no paired lab. The Python data analysis is done in the two assignments described under Assessment.

Assessment

Theory: mid-term and final (80). The mid-term covers weeks 1–7. A formula sheet and a standard normal table should be provided, so that marks go to choosing a method and interpreting the result. At least half the marks are for application and analysis; recall is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • Worked probability problems set in computing: hash collisions, retry until success, packet loss, the chance that at least one of several servers is up.
  • A situation is described: choose a distribution, justify the choice, and compute a probability or an expectation.
  • Printed Python output is given (a summary table, a test result, a regression fit): interpret it and state the conclusion.
  • A conclusion with a flaw is given, such as reading a p-value as the probability that the hypothesis is true, or reading correlation as cause: find the flaw and correct it.
  • A short simulation script is given: say what quantity it estimates and how the estimate changes with more trials.

Continuous assessment (20). Four 15-minute quizzes, best three counted (10), Level 0. Two assignments in Python (pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, SciPy), Level 1:

  1. Weeks 3–7 (4 marks): describe a supplied real dataset, fit a distribution to one variable, and check the fit by simulation.
  2. Weeks 9–13 (6 marks): a small individual study. The student poses one question about a real dataset, estimates the answer with a confidence interval, runs one test or one regression, and writes a two-page report in English with labelled plots.

Each student works on a different random subsample, drawn with the student ID as the seed, so no two sets of numbers agree. The quiz after the second assignment asks a question about the student's own result, such as what happens to their interval if the sample is four times larger.

Resources

  • Michael Baron, Probability and Statistics for Computer Scientists (main text).
  • Ronald E. Walpole, Raymond H. Myers, Sharon L. Myers and Keying Ye, Probability and Statistics for Engineers and Scientists (alternative).
  • Allen B. Downey, Think Stats (free; Python throughout).
  • Python 3 with NumPy, pandas, Matplotlib, SciPy and Jupyter Notebook.
  • Public datasets, for example from the UCI Machine Learning Repository and World Bank Open Data.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The usual failure is matching a problem to a formula with no idea of what the answer means; the misread p-value is the standard case. Show each idea by simulation first (the central limit theorem, the coverage of a confidence interval) and give the formula second.
  • Keep joint distributions to discrete tables here. MATH-1107 has no double integrals; continuous joint densities are taken up in MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, once double integrals have been taught. In this course show continuous joint behaviour only with scatter plots and simulation, and tell students where the integrals will come.
  • Weak English shows most in written conclusions. Give sentence templates ("We are 95% confident that …", "The data do not give enough evidence that …") and mark the statistical meaning before the grammar.
  • AI assistants write pandas code and whole reports. Individual subsamples and the follow-up quiz keep the evidence honest. The course also gives students the means to check statistical claims that an assistant produces, and one quiz question should ask them to do so.

About this group of courses: Mathematics, Physics and Electrical Fundamentals

This file holds the outlines for the seven mathematics, physics and electrical courses of the programme. The seventh, MATH-2409 Calculus II in semester 4, was added after review of the first draft. Together they replace about 40 credits of mathematics, physics, chemistry and electrical courses in the 2022 syllabus with 23 credits. A topic is kept only if a later course in the programme uses it, and each outline has a table naming the courses that do. The courses are taught as service courses by the Mathematics, Physics and EEE departments, so the outlines are written for a teacher from outside CSE, with examples, assignments and examination questions drawn from computing.
