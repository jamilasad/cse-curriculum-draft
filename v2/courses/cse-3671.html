Rationale and what changed

The core teaches design at the level of classes (CSE-2325) and gives one small green-field team project (CSE-2434). Most graduates of this track will instead join a system that is years old, written by people who have left, and will be asked to change it without breaking it. AI assistants write plausible code quickly; they do not supply the decision about where code belongs, what it may depend on and what a change will cost later, and that decision is the subject of this course. It is a new course with no direct predecessor in the 2022 syllabus: the design content of Systems Analysis and Design went into Software Engineering (CSE-2433), and this course starts where that stops. Left out on purpose: a pattern-by-pattern tour of the design-pattern catalogue (the patterns needed were met in CSE-2325), heavy UML and document-driven methods, and microservices as a default. Services are treated as one option whose costs the student must be able to state.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • In an unfamiliar module of 20–50 files, trace one request from entry point to database and back, and draw a correct component diagram with dependency directions, within 60 minutes.
  • Point to three concrete coupling or cohesion problems in given code and say for each which change it makes hard.
  • From a one-page requirement, produce a domain model, a relational schema and a REST contract (resources, methods, status codes, error format) that agree with one another.
  • Write a one-page ADR with context, at least two options, the decision, and consequences including one that is negative.
  • Make a small change in the lab codebase that passes its tests and a review, and explain every changed line.

Stretch goals

  • Have a pull request merged by the upstream open-source project.
  • Extract a module behind an interface in stages (strangler fig) and show the dependency graph before and after.
  • Enforce two architecture rules automatically in the project's CI pipeline and report the violations that already exist.

Weekly plan — theory

WeekTopicsCLO
1What architecture is: structure, quality attributes and the decisions that are costly to reverse; functional requirements, quality attributes and constraints as drivers; every choice as a trade-off. Recording a decision: the ADR format. Case: the students' own semester-4 studio application4, 6
2Reading a codebase you did not write: build files, entry points, directory layout, tests as documentation, version history as evidence; tracing one request end to end; describing what you found with the C4 model (context, container, component)1
3Modularity I: information hiding; cohesion; kinds of coupling and how each looks in code; deep and shallow modules; interfaces as contracts; leaky abstractions2
4Modularity II: dependency direction and dependency inversion between modules; cycles; stable and volatile parts; package boundaries; evidence from tools: dependency graphs, fan-in and fan-out, files that change together in the Git history2
5Domain modelling I: a shared vocabulary with the client; entities and value objects; aggregates and the invariants they protect; domain events; event storming as a workshop; anaemic and rich models3
6Domain modelling II: bounded contexts and the relationships between them; from domain model to tables: aggregate and transaction boundaries, object-relational mapping choices, when the relational model should lead3
7API design: resources and HTTP semantics, idempotency, pagination and filtering, a uniform error format, versioning and backward compatibility; OpenAPI as the contract; REST, RPC and GraphQL compared; why every observable behaviour becomes someone's dependency3
8Architectural styles I: layered, hexagonal (ports and adapters) and the modular monolith; what each makes easy and hard to change and to test4
9Architectural styles II: service-based and microservice architectures; event-driven architecture (queues, publish–subscribe); serverless functions; the distributed monolith; team structure and Conway's law; what each style costs to operate4
10Data architecture: one database or one per service; consistency across boundaries (sagas and the outbox, as ideas); read models; caching: where, invalidation, staleness; schema change without downtime (expand and contract)3, 5
11Performance by design: latency, throughput and percentiles; back-of-envelope estimates of load and storage; finding the bottleneck; N+1 queries; stateless services and horizontal scaling; asynchronous work and back-pressure5
12Reliability and security by design: failure modes; timeouts, retries, idempotency, circuit breakers, bulkheads, graceful degradation; trust boundaries, least privilege and threat-modelling a design with STRIDE (uses CSE-3651); quality-attribute scenarios and a lightweight architecture evaluation5
13Design for change: characterisation tests, seams, strangler-fig migration, branch by abstraction, feature flags; technical debt as a recorded liability; refactor or rewrite; automated architecture rules2, 4
14Design documents and design review: writing a proposal others can criticise; running and receiving a review. Architecture when assistants write much of the code: what to specify, what to check, how structure erodes. Full worked case and revision6

The mid-term covers weeks 1–7. Weeks 10–12 are placed late so that CSE-3653 and CSE-3651, taken in the same semester, have introduced replication, failure and threat modelling first.

Assessment

Theory. Written mid-term and final, 80 marks; the final covers the whole course with more weight on weeks 8–14. Question types:

  • Code reading: two or three printed pages of an unseen small system. Draw its components and dependencies, name two coupling problems, say where a given change goes.
  • Design choice: a one-page case with requirements and constraints (for example an appointment system for a clinic chain with 40 branches, unreliable connectivity and a team of five). Choose between two or three given architectures, justify by quality attributes, and state what fact would change the decision.
  • Modelling: derive aggregates, a schema and an API contract from a case; or find the faults in a given API (a retry that is not idempotent, wrong status codes, a breaking change).
  • Estimation: requests per second, storage per year, whether one database server is enough.
  • Decision records: rewrite a poor ADR and list the consequences its author missed.
  • Recall, at most a fifth of the marks: definitions and characteristics of styles.

Continuous assessment (20): four Level 0 quizzes, best three counted (10); one individual assignment (10), an architecture description of the student's own CSE-2434 project with two retrospective ADRs and one change they would now make.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and record 30. Practical tests in weeks 7 and 13, 15 each. Project 25: in pairs, design and implement one medium feature or structural improvement in the lab codebase, with domain model, API contract, three ADRs, a review record, a working pull request with tests and an architecture rule, and a measurement note. Viva 15: the examiner picks a part of the codebase and a part of the student's own change and asks what would break if it moved. A student who cannot explain their own work does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture (main text).
  • John Ousterhout, A Philosophy of Software Design. Vlad Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design.
  • Michael Feathers, Working Effectively with Legacy Code.
  • Martin Kleppmann, Designing Data-Intensive Applications (selected chapters, weeks 10–11). Michael Nygard, Release It! (week 12).
  • Len Bass, Paul Clements and Rick Kazman, Software Architecture in Practice (reference).
  • Tools: Git; IntelliJ IDEA Community Edition or Visual Studio Code; PlantUML or Mermaid; jdeps and ArchUnit (Java) or dependency-cruiser (TypeScript); SonarQube Community Build; Swagger Editor for OpenAPI; k6; Docker.
  • Codebase: one per section, chosen and built by the instructor beforehand. Criteria: a web application with a database, in Java, TypeScript or Python; tens of thousands of lines or more; an automated test suite; active maintenance; builds on a lab machine in a few minutes. Candidates: one or two modules of Apache Fineract (Java, core banking, close to fintech work); Zulip (Python and TypeScript, with thorough developer documentation); or a department system whose source can be shared. Past studio projects are too small.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • Prepare the codebase before the semester: build it on a lab machine, keep an offline mirror of the repository and its dependencies, and select 15–20 small issues. Without this the first three labs are lost to set-up.
  • Reading code is slow for students with weak English and little experience. Give a guided tour in week 1, a reading worksheet for each of the first three labs and a glossary of the project's terms. Practical test 1 repeats the activity practised in weeks 2 and 3.
  • Common failures: diagrams of the architecture the student thinks the system should have instead of the one in the code (require a repository path for every box); and microservices chosen because they sound modern (make the student list what must then be operated, and by whom).
  • AI assistants summarise a codebase fluently and are often wrong in the detail; the week-4 comparison teaches students to check. In the viva ask why code is where it is: generated code can be defended line by line and still be in the wrong place.
