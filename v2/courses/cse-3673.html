Rationale and what changed

The core course gives every student one pass over machine learning. This course makes track students carry out the whole supervised-learning workflow properly on the kind of data most organisations actually hold: messy tables exported from operational systems. It keeps the useful parts of the 2022 courses Machine Learning and Data Mining and Pattern Recognition and Image Processing, and drops fuzzy systems, classical image processing and learning-theory proofs. Neural networks are left to CSE-4773, pipelines at scale to CSE-4783 and deployment to CSE-4883. Students write gradient descent, a cross-validation loop and a tree split by hand once, then use libraries for everything else.

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Take a raw CSV file with a described target and build a scikit-learn pipeline that imputes, encodes and scales, fits a logistic regression and a gradient-boosted model, and reports cross-validated results.
  • Choose between a random, stratified, grouped and time-ordered split for a described problem and say why the others are wrong.
  • Compute precision, recall and F1 from a confusion matrix by hand and pick a decision threshold from a table of error costs.
  • Find target or pre-processing leakage in a short notebook written by someone else.
  • Explain in plain words what a tree split, a regularisation penalty and one boosting round do.

Stretch goals

  • Implement gradient boosting for regression from scratch with depth-one trees and match a library's result on a small dataset.
  • Add calibrated prediction intervals (quantile regression or conformal prediction) to the project model and check their coverage.
  • Enter a public tabular-data competition and write up which changes moved the score and which did not.

Weekly plan — theory

WeekTopicsCLO
1The workflow as an engineering process: framing a business problem as prediction, unit of prediction, target definition, baseline, cost of each error type. First look at data: types, distributions, data dictionary. Train, validation and test sets; why the test set is locked. Unmarked diagnostic quiz1
2Linear models done carefully: least squares in matrix form, gradient descent, logistic regression as maximum likelihood, log-loss, softmax. Regularisation: ridge, lasso, elastic net, the regularisation path, why features must be scaled3
3Messy data from real organisations: missingness and its causes (MCAR, MAR, MNAR), imputation choices, outliers and impossible values, duplicates and entity matching, inconsistent codes and units, mixed Bangla and English entries, joins that multiply rows; keeping a log of cleaning decisions2
4Feature engineering: encoding categories (one-hot, ordinal, target encoding and its leakage risk), high-cardinality fields, dates and cyclic features, counts and ratios, log transforms, text columns with TF-IDF. Pipelines and column transformers. A catalogue of leakage: target, temporal, group, pre-processing2
5Validation and model selection: bias–variance decomposition, learning curves; hold-out, k-fold, stratified, grouped and time-ordered splits; variance of the estimate and bootstrap intervals; grid, random and Bayesian search; nested cross-validation; how repeated peeking spoils a test set4
6Metrics and decisions: confusion matrix, precision, recall, F-beta, ROC and precision–recall curves; calibration, reliability diagrams, Brier score; choosing a threshold from a cost table; regression metrics (MAE, RMSE, MAPE) and when each misleads4
7Class imbalance and rare events: why accuracy fails; class weights, under- and over-sampling, SMOTE and why resampling belongs inside the fold; threshold moving; cost-sensitive learning; cases: fraud, loan default, disease screening4
8Decision trees: impurity (Gini, entropy), split search worked by hand, depth and pruning, treatment of categorical and missing values, instability. Bagging and random forests: bootstrap samples, feature subsampling, out-of-bag error3
9Boosting: AdaBoost in outline; gradient boosting as fitting residuals step by step; shrinkage, depth, subsampling, early stopping; histogram-based implementations and their main parameters; stacking; when a regularised linear model is the better choice3, 4
10Interpretability and error analysis: coefficients and odds ratios; permutation importance; partial dependence and ICE plots; SHAP values (the Shapley idea, tree-based computation); correlated features; explanation is not causation; errors by slice5
11Time-series basics: trend, seasonality, autocorrelation, stationarity; naive and seasonal-naive baselines; exponential smoothing; ARIMA in outline; lag and calendar features with tree models; rolling-origin backtesting6, 4
12Clustering: k-means and its failure cases, hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN, Gaussian mixtures in outline; choosing the number of clusters; silhouette score; checking clusters against domain sense; customer segmentation case6
13Dimensionality reduction and anomaly detection: PCA from the SVD, explained variance, loadings; t-SNE and UMAP for visualisation only; isolation forest and robust z-scores; evaluating a detector when labels are few6
14Handing a model over: reproducible repository; the model report (data, validation design, results with intervals, limits, intended use); a first look at fairness across groups and at drift, both taken up in CSE-4883. Review of two complete cases1, 5

Weekly plan — lab

CSE-3674 Applied Machine Learning Lab · Lab · 1 credits · 2 contact hours a week

Compute: everything runs on the CPU of an ordinary lab PC or a free notebook service. Datasets are kept below about 500,000 rows so that a gradient-boosted model trains in minutes; no GPU is needed. Two datasets are used throughout: a deliberately messy organisational export prepared by the instructor (mixed Bangla and English labels, Bangla digits such as ০–৯ in numeric fields, district names spelled several ways such as Chattogram and Chittagong, dates in two formats, duplicate customers) and an imbalanced transactions set.

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term (weeks 1–7) and final, 80 marks. At least half the marks are application and analysis: find and fix the leakage or validation error in a printed notebook excerpt; compute metrics from a confusion matrix and choose a threshold from a cost table; work one tree split and one boosting round by hand on a ten-row table; read a regularisation path or a learning curve and say what to change; design the split, metric and baseline for a described case such as loan default at a microfinance institution. Stronger students are separated by short derivations (gradient of the log-loss, the ridge solution). Definitions and other recall carry at most a fifth of the marks. Continuous assessment, 20: four Level 0 quizzes, best three counted (10); one take-home assignment (10) made of a handwritten problem set and a two-page critique of the validation design in a supplied analysis.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30; two supervised practical tests 30 (15 each, network off, offline library documentation allowed); lab project 25; individual viva 15. The project is done in pairs: a prediction problem on tabular data from a real organisation or a public source, delivered as a reproducible repository and a four-page model report in English. Each pair fixes its test split in the proposal and may score it once. A student who cannot explain their own submitted work in the viva does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Textbooks: James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani and Taylor, An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in Python; Géron, Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn, Keras, and TensorFlow; Kuhn and Johnson, Feature Engineering and Selection; Molnar, Interpretable Machine Learning; Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice. For strong students: Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning.
  • Software (all free; current examples, reviewed at each offering): Python, NumPy, pandas, scikit-learn, Matplotlib, JupyterLab, Git; one gradient-boosting library (XGBoost, LightGBM or CatBoost); SHAP; statsmodels; imbalanced-learn; Optuna for the stretch work.
  • Data: UCI Machine Learning Repository, OpenML and Kaggle datasets; exports from partner organisations with personal fields removed.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The commonest failure is a result that is too good because of leakage or tuning on the test set. Make the locked test set a rule from week 1 and give marks for the validation design, not for the score.
  • AI assistants write a complete pipeline in seconds, often with leakage and with no reason given for any choice. Weekly marks therefore go to the decision log and the checks, and the two Level 0 tests measure whether the student can do the basic workflow alone.
  • For weaker students: give starter notebooks with the structure in place, a one-page pipeline checklist, and a week 1 refresher sheet on matrix notation and probability for those the diagnostic identifies. Memos and reports use a fixed template with sentence frames, because writing in English is a second difficulty on top of the content.
  • Keep the messy export messy. Cleaning it for students removes the part of the course that most resembles the job.
