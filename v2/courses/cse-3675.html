Rationale and what changed

The 2022 syllabus had one Computer Security course in semester 8 and nothing on monitoring or operations, so this course is new. It teaches the defender's daily work: reading traffic, placing firewalls and sensors, collecting logs and deciding which alerts are real. The security operations centre is the entry point to the field that is most open to mid-level students. The course runs beside CSE-3651, which teaches cryptography, access-control models and the vulnerability classes; those are used here and not re-taught, and weeks 1–7 rely only on Computer Networks and Operating Systems. Students read packets and logs by hand before they use a SIEM, and write firewall and detection rules by hand before they use rule sets written by others. Left out: training on any commercial firewall or SIEM product, exploit development, wireless attacks beyond a mention, and router and switch configuration in depth (that belongs to the INF track).

Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  1. Given a packet capture, list the hosts and services and identify one planted attack (a scan, ARP spoofing or password guessing) in Wireshark, pointing to the packets that prove it.
  2. Write a default-deny nftables rule set that allows a stated list of services, and prove with a scan that it works.
  3. Search central logs to answer five factual questions about an event: which account, from which address, when, how many attempts, and whether it succeeded.
  4. Triage a given alert as a true or false positive, citing the evidence, and write a ticket of ten lines or fewer that a colleague on the next shift could act on.
  5. State what the rules of engagement allow and forbid, and why scanning a system without permission is an offence.

Stretch goals

  • Write one detection both as a Sigma rule and as a native rule in the lab SIEM, run it against a week of baseline logs, and report its false-positive count before and after tuning.
  • Map the lab's detections to MITRE ATT&CK techniques, name the three largest gaps and close one.
  • Prepare for and sit CompTIA Security+ (optional, at the student's own cost).

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term and final (80). At least half the marks are application and analysis; recall is at most a fifth. Typical questions:

  • A printed excerpt of a capture: name the attack, point to the packets that show it, state one defence.
  • A firewall rule set with planted errors: say which of five given packets pass, find the shadowed rule, correct the set.
  • A short case (a bank branch, a 40-person software firm): draw the zones, place the firewall and sensor, write the main rules.
  • Twenty log lines from two sources: put the events in order, say what happened, classify the related alert.
  • Complete or correct a Suricata rule or a SIEM query for a described behaviour.

Continuous assessment (20): four Level 0 quizzes, best three counted (10); two assignments (10), a capture-analysis worksheet and a segmentation design for a case organisation.

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30; two supervised practical tests at Level 0, 30; lab project 25; individual viva 15. Project (mini-SOC, in pairs): build monitoring for a three-zone lab network with a firewall rule set, an intrusion sensor, central logs and at least four detections. In week 13 the instructor runs a scripted attack against it. The pair submits its rules, triage tickets and a two-page incident summary saying what was detected, what was missed and why. A student who cannot explain their own rules and tickets in the viva does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • Textbooks: William Stallings, Network Security Essentials; Chris Sanders, Practical Packet Analysis; Chris Sanders and Jason Smith, Applied Network Security Monitoring; Richard Bejtlich, The Practice of Network Security Monitoring. For those sitting the certificate: Mike Chapple and David Seidl, CompTIA Security+ Study Guide.
  • Software (all free): VirtualBox, Kali Linux, Metasploitable 2, a Windows evaluation virtual machine, Wireshark, tcpdump, Nmap, Ettercap or Bettercap, Hydra, hping3, nftables, OPNsense or pfSense, Suricata, Zeek, Wazuh, Sysmon, auditd, Sigma, Atomic Red Team, MISP. Reference: MITRE ATT&CK.
  • Hardware: ordinary lab PCs; 8 GB of memory runs the attacker and one target, 16 GB is better. One department server hosts Wazuh for each section.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The common failure is running a tool and submitting its output unread. Require every deliverable to point at specific packets or log lines and say in one sentence what they show.
  • Weaker students usually lack fluency in TCP/IP, not security ideas. Week 2 is a deliberate review; give a one-page protocol sheet, keep captures small, and let students work in pairs in weeks 3–6 while keeping individual lab records.
  • AI assistants explain a packet or a log line well and will draft a rule. They also invent field names and call harmless events malicious with confidence. Students log their AI use and test every drafted rule against lab data; tests and the viva are at Level 0.
  • Synchronise clocks on all range machines before week 9, or the log exercises fail for reasons that have nothing to do with security. A breach of the rules of engagement means immediate loss of lab access.
