Rationale and what changed

Digital logic is where the programme first shows that a computer is built from parts a student can wire and test. Computer Organization & Architecture (CSE-2425) needs its registers, multiplexers and state machines; Microcontrollers & Embedded Systems (CSE-3547) needs its logic levels and timing; the EMB track needs its first contact with a hardware description language. The Boolean algebra, combinational design and sequential design of the 2022 course are kept. Two things are added: a free logic simulator used from the first week, and an introduction to Verilog with simulation in the last weeks. Left out: the internal electronics of logic families beyond what is needed to wire ICs safely, Quine–McCluskey tabulation by hand, asynchronous sequential design, and Karnaugh maps beyond four variables (larger functions go to the tools). The lab stays at three hours because wiring and fault-finding on a breadboard cannot be hurried.

Minimum competency

  • Convert an 8-bit value between binary, hexadecimal, unsigned decimal and signed decimal, and add two 8-bit two's-complement numbers, saying whether overflow occurred.
  • From a truth table of up to four variables, produce a minimal sum-of-products expression with a Karnaugh map and draw the gate circuit.
  • Wire a given circuit of up to three ICs on a breadboard from the pin diagrams and verify it against its truth table.
  • From a state diagram of up to four states, derive the state table and the next-state equations, and build the circuit with D flip-flops in the simulator.
  • Complete a timing diagram for a circuit with one or two D flip-flops, and read a Verilog module of about 20 lines and say what circuit it describes.

Stretch goals

  • Design a 4-bit accumulator-based processor datapath in the simulator and run a short hand-assembled program on it.
  • Write a state machine in Verilog with a self-checking testbench, in both Moore and Mealy form, and compare the waveforms.
  • Work out the maximum clock frequency of a given sequential circuit from gate delays and setup time.

Assessment

Theory. Mid-term (weeks 1–7) and final (whole course, weighted to weeks 8–14) together carry 80 marks. At least half the marks are for application and analysis:

  • design a circuit from a word problem (truth table, Karnaugh map, circuit);
  • analyse a given sequential circuit to obtain its state diagram, or complete its timing diagram;
  • find and correct the fault in a given design: a wrong Karnaugh-map grouping, a miswired schematic, a state table that does not match its diagram;
  • choose between two implementations (gates or multiplexer, ripple or synchronous counter) and give the reason;
  • read a Verilog module of 15–20 lines and draw the circuit or predict the waveform.

Recall (gate symbols, characteristic tables, definitions) is at most a fifth. Continuous assessment (20): four quizzes of about 15 minutes at Level 0, best three counted (10); three problem sets done on paper and checked in the simulator, at Level 1 (10).

Lab (100). Weekly lab work and lab record 30. Two supervised practical tests at Level 0, 30. Lab project 25: in pairs, a small synchronous system with a state machine and at least one datapath block, such as a combination lock, a traffic-light controller with pedestrian request, a stopwatch on seven-segment displays or a vending-machine controller. It is built and tested in the simulator and then either on the breadboard or in Verilog with a testbench, with a design note in English (specification, state diagram, tables, test evidence) marked with the shared writing rubric. Individual viva 15. A student who cannot explain their own circuit in the viva does not pass the lab.

Resources

  • M. Morris Mano and Michael D. Ciletti, Digital Design: With an Introduction to the Verilog HDL (main text).
  • Thomas L. Floyd, Digital Fundamentals (easier reading, many worked examples).
  • Sarah L. Harris and David Harris, Digital Design and Computer Architecture: RISC-V Edition (also used in CSE-2425).
  • Software, all free and usable offline: Logisim-evolution; Icarus Verilog; GTKWave.
  • Hardware per bench: breadboard or digital trainer board, 5 V supply, LEDs, resistors, DIP switches, push buttons, seven-segment display, multimeter; 74-series ICs: 7400, 7404, 7408, 7432, 7486, 7474, 7476, 7483, 7447, 7490, 74138, 74151.

Notes for the instructor

In the planned public view this section is shown to the committee only (specification 3.4).

  • The usual failure is a student who can run the Karnaugh-map procedure but cannot get from a word problem to a truth table. Set at least one word problem every week. For sequential circuits, have students draw timing diagrams by hand before they simulate.
  • Pairs share a bench, but the two students wire on alternate weeks and both practical tests are individual. Give weaker students a one-page pin-out sheet and a fixed fault-finding order (power, ground, floating inputs, pin numbers, then logic), and run an open lab hour before each practical test.
  • An AI assistant produces a Karnaugh-map answer or a Verilog module in seconds, so marks sit in the supervised tests, the physical circuit and the viva. At Level 1 students may ask an assistant to explain a concept or an error message, not to produce the design. Show one assistant answer with a wrong grouping or a blocking-assignment error and have the class find the fault with the simulator.
  • The lab Verilog sheets for weeks 12–13 should be self-contained tutorials, since a lab group may meet before that week's lecture.

About this group of courses: Systems and Hardware Core

These six packages take the student from a single gate to a networked computer running many programs. Digital Logic Design (S3) builds gates, registers and state machines; Computer Organization & Architecture (S4) assembles those parts into a RISC-V processor with a memory hierarchy and shows how C becomes instructions. Semester 5 then looks at the machine from three sides: Microcontrollers & Embedded Systems programs a bare processor with no operating system, Operating Systems explains the software that shares one machine among many programs, and Computer Networks connects machines to each other. Automata, Languages & Compilers (S7) closes the sequence with what machines can and cannot compute, and how the languages used throughout the programme are translated and run.
