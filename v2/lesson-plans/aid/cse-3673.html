Minimum competency

Every passing student can, unaided:

  • Take a raw CSV file with a described target and build a scikit-learn pipeline that imputes, encodes and scales, fits a logistic regression and a gradient-boosted model, and reports cross-validated results.
  • Choose between a random, stratified, grouped and time-ordered split for a described problem and say why the others are wrong.
  • Compute precision, recall and F1 from a confusion matrix by hand and pick a decision threshold from a table of error costs.
  • Find target or pre-processing leakage in a short notebook written by someone else.
  • Explain in plain words what a tree split, a regularisation penalty and one boosting round do.

2. Class pattern (assumption to confirm)

  • Theory: 3 contact hours a week, given as two 90-minute classes. That makes 28 classes in 14 teaching weeks. Classes are numbered 1 to 28; class 2W−1 and class 2W fall in week W.
  • Lab: one 2-hour session a week, 14 sessions.
  • Examinations: the mid-term (weeks 1–7) and the final are held outside the 14 teaching weeks (README A3). The outline does not say otherwise.
  • Re-split: the department may re-split theory into three 60-minute classes. Use the rule in README A2. Each timed plan below is written in blocks so the cut falls between blocks.
  • If the mid-term must use a class slot: classes 15–26 move one slot later. Class 27 keeps its slot, and the second worked case of class 28 is done as homework from a written solution.
  • Quizzes: four Level 0 quizzes of 20 minutes, at the start of classes 8, 14, 20 and 26. The best three count.
  • Diagnostic: the unmarked Level 0 diagnostic is in class 1 (outline, week 1).

Week 1

Class 1 (Week 1): The workflow as an engineering process

  • Outline topics covered: the workflow as an engineering process; framing a business problem as prediction; unit of prediction; target definition; baseline; cost of each error type. Unmarked diagnostic quiz.
  • CLO / PO: CLO1 / PO2
  • Timed plan (90 min):
MinActivity

10–35Short lecture: the supervised-learning workflow as engineering (problem, data, split, baseline, model, validation, decision, hand-over). Worked framing of one case on the board: a mobile-payment provider wants to flag risky cash-out requests. Unit of prediction (one request), target (confirmed fraud within 30 days), the moment of prediction (before approval), what is known at that moment.

Week 2

Class 3 (Week 2): Least squares, gradient descent and logistic regression

  • Outline topics covered: linear models done carefully: least squares in matrix form, gradient descent, logistic regression as maximum likelihood.
  • CLO / PO: CLO3 / PO1
  • Timed plan (90 min):
MinActivity
0–5Diagnostic results in summary (no names). Where to get help: maths clinic times.
5–30Worked derivation on the board: the design matrix X, the loss ‖y − Xw‖², its gradient −2*X*ᵀ(y − Xw), the normal equations X*ᵀ*Xw = X*ᵀ*y. Shapes written beside every line.
30–45Gradient descent: the update w ← w − η∇L. The teacher runs three steps by hand on a two-point example, then shows a NumPy cell doing 200 steps, with the learning rate too small, right and too large.
45–70Worked derivation: logistic regression. The sigmoid, the likelihood of one example p^y(1 − p)^(1 − y), the log-likelihood, and why maximising it is the same as minimising log-loss.
70–80Pair task: compute the log-loss for four predictions (0.9 and 0.6 for positives; 0.2 and 0.7 for negatives) and say which example costs most.
80–90Formative check and summary.
  • TLA: worked derivations with shapes; a hand-run of gradient descent followed by a notebook demonstration; pair computation.
  • Materials and readings: ISLP, ch. 3 "Linear Regression" (least squares) and ch. 4 "Classification" (logistic regression); Géron, ch. 4 "Training Models" (the normal equation, gradient descent, logistic regression). Strong students: Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 3 and ch. 4.
  • Formative check: "The learning rate is doubled and the loss now rises every step. Why?" Correct answer: the step overshoots the minimum; reduce the learning rate.
  • Homework: By-hand practice set 1 (Level 1): two gradient-descent steps on a three-point dataset; the log-loss of five predictions; the gradient of the log-loss for one example. Prepares for lab 2 and quiz 1.

Class 4 (Week 2): Log-loss, softmax and regularisation

  • Outline topics covered: log-loss, softmax. Regularisation: ridge, lasso, elastic net, the regularisation path, why features must be scaled.
  • CLO / PO: CLO3 / PO1
  • Timed plan (90 min):
MinActivity
0–15The gradient of the log-loss for one example, (p − y)x, derived step by step. Softmax for three classes with numbers: scores (2, 1, 0) give probabilities about (0.67, 0.24, 0.09).
15–35Short lecture: why a model with many features overfits; the ridge penalty λ‖w‖² and its closed form (X*ᵀ*X + λ*I*)⁻¹*X*ᵀ*y*; the lasso penalty λ‖w‖₁ and why it sets some weights to zero; elastic net as a mix.
35–55Notebook demonstration: the regularisation path for ridge and lasso on a table with 20 features. Students predict, before each plot, which coefficients go to zero first.
55–70Demonstration: the same model with one feature in taka and one in lakh taka. The penalty treats them differently. Why features must be scaled before a penalised model.
70–80Think-pair-share: "A colleague says lasso picked the three most important causes of loan default. What is wrong with that sentence?"
80–90Formative check and summary.
  • TLA: worked derivation; prediction before demonstration ("predict, then run"); think-pair-share.
  • Materials and readings: ISLP, ch. 6 "Linear Model Selection and Regularization" (ridge regression and the lasso); Géron, ch. 4 "Training Models" (regularised linear models, softmax regression).
  • Formative check: "λ goes from 0.01 to 100 in ridge. What happens to the training error, the size of the weights, and the validation error?" Correct answer: training error rises; weights shrink; validation error usually falls and then rises.
  • Homework: By-hand practice set 2 (Level 1): the ridge solution for a 2 × 2 case; softmax for two score vectors; reading a printed regularisation path. Read the starter file for lab 2 before the lab.

Week 4

Class 7 (Week 4): Feature engineering

75–80Pairs list three features for the pharmacy case and say when each value is known.

  • Outline topics covered: validation and model selection: bias–variance decomposition, learning curves; hold-out, k-fold, stratified, grouped and time-ordered splits.
  • CLO / PO: CLO4 / PO4
  • Timed plan (90 min):
MinActivity
0–20Short lecture: the bias–variance decomposition of expected squared error, with a picture of a polynomial fitted to noisy points at three degrees.
20–35Learning curves: training and validation score against training-set size. The teacher draws three shapes (high bias, high variance, good fit) and students say which fix fits each.
35–60Splits: hold-out, k-fold, stratified k-fold, grouped k-fold, time-ordered splits. For each, the teacher shows a diagram of which rows go where.
60–80Case matching in pairs: six short cases (loan default with repeat borrowers, monthly sales, rare disease screening, images from several hospitals, customer churn, exam results by school). Pairs choose the split and say why each other split is wrong.
80–90Formative check and summary.
  • TLA: short lecture with sketches; learning-curve reading; case matching in pairs.
  • Materials and readings: ISLP, ch. 5 "Resampling Methods" (cross-validation) and ch. 2 (the bias–variance trade-off); Géron, ch. 4 (learning curves). Strong students: Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, ch. 7 "Model Assessment and Selection".
  • Formative check: "Loans from 2,000 borrowers, many with several loans. Why is stratified k-fold on loans wrong?" Correct answer: the same borrower can be in training and validation, so the score is too good; use grouped folds by borrower, and a time-ordered split if the model will be used on future loans.
  • Homework: Read the lab 5 sheet. By-hand practice (Level 1): draw the fold assignment for 12 rows from 4 groups under 3-fold grouped CV.

Class 10 (Week 5): Uncertainty, search and nested cross-validation

  • Outline topics covered: variance of the estimate and bootstrap intervals; grid, random and Bayesian search; nested cross-validation; how repeated peeking spoils a test set.
  • CLO / PO: CLO4 / PO4
  • Timed plan (90 min):
MinActivity
0–20Variance of the estimate: five fold scores 0.81, 0.78, 0.84, 0.80, 0.77. Mean and standard deviation on the board. Why "0.80" alone misleads.
20–40Bootstrap intervals: resample the test predictions with replacement 1,000 times; take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the metric. Notebook demonstration, and a hand version with 5 resamples of 6 predictions.
40–55Search: grid, random, Bayesian (a model of the score guides the next trial). Why random search often beats grid search when only a few settings matter.
55–70Nested cross-validation: an inner loop to tune, an outer loop to estimate. Diagram, and why tuning and scoring on the same folds is optimistic.
70–80Case discussion: a student tried 40 settings and reported the best test score. The class estimates what went wrong.
80–90Formative check and summary.
  • TLA: numerical example on the board; demonstration with a hand version alongside; diagram building; case discussion.
  • Materials and readings: ISLP, ch. 5 "Resampling Methods" (the bootstrap); Kuhn and Johnson, ch. 3 (model tuning and the risk of overfitting to resamples).
  • Formative check: "Model A: 0.82 (interval 0.78 to 0.86). Model B: 0.83 (interval 0.79 to 0.87). Is B better?" Correct answer: not shown; the intervals overlap heavily; prefer the simpler or cheaper model, or compare paired scores on the same folds.
  • Homework: Begin the take-home assignment when it is released in class 12. Prepare for practical test 1 in lab 6 with the practice test sheet (Level 1 practice).

Week 6

4. Lab lesson plan (CSE-3674)

Compute for every session (outline): the CPU of an ordinary lab PC or a free notebook service. Datasets stay below about 500,000 rows, so a gradient-boosted model trains in minutes. No GPU is needed.

Two datasets run through the semester (outline), both prepared by the instructor before week 1:

6.4 Sample questions with answers and marking notes

Recall (mid-term Q5(a), 2 marks). "State in one line each what MCAR, MAR and MNAR mean." Answer: MCAR: whether a value is missing does not depend on any data. MAR: it depends on observed data (for example, on branch). MNAR: it depends on the missing value itself (for example, the poorest leave income blank). Marking: 1 mark for MCAR and MAR together, 1 for MNAR.

Understanding (mid-term Q2(b), 2 marks). "λ in ridge regression rises from 0.1 to 100. What happens to the coefficients? Why must the features be scaled first?" Answer: The coefficients shrink towards zero (none become exactly zero). The penalty treats all coefficients alike, so a feature measured in small units (large coefficient) is penalised more than the same feature in large units; scaling puts them on equal terms. Marking: 1 + 1.

Application: derivation (mid-term Q2(a), 4 marks). "For one example, p = σ(w*ᵀ*x) and L = −[y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p)]. Show that ∂L/∂w = (p − y)x." Answer: ∂L/∂p = −y/p + (1 − y)/(1 − p) = (p − y) / (p(1 − p)). σ′(z) = p(1 − p). ∂z/∂w = x. Multiply: (p − y)x. Marking: ∂L/∂p 1; sigmoid derivative 1; chain rule set out 1; simplification 1.

Application: metrics and threshold (mid-term Q4, 6 marks). "TP 40, FP 60, FN 10, TN 890. (a) Precision, recall, F1. (b) A review costs 500 taka; a missed default 5,000 taka. At three thresholds: 0.2 (FP 150, FN 4), 0.3 (FP 80, FN 8), 0.5 (FP 30, FN 18). Which threshold?" Answer: (a) Precision 40/100 = 0.40; recall 40/50 = 0.80; F1 = 2 × 0.40 × 0.80 / 1.20 ≈ 0.53. (b) Cost at 0.2: 150 × 500 + 4 × 5,000 = 95,000; at 0.3: 40,000 + 40,000 = 80,000; at 0.5: 15,000 + 90,000 = 105,000. Choose 0.3. Marking: (a) 1 each; (b) 2 for the three costs, 1 for the choice with reason.

Analysis: leakage in a printed excerpt (mid-term Q3, 6 marks). The excerpt:

df = pd.read_csv("loans.csv")      # one row per loan; many borrowers have several loans
df["branch_te"] = df.groupby("branch")["defaulted"].transform("mean")
X = df.drop(columns=["defaulted"])  # columns include recovery_visits
y = df["defaulted"]
X_num = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X[num_cols])
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X_num, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=1)
model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(accuracy_score(y_te, model.predict(X_te)))

Answer and marking (1 mark each for up to four problems, 2 for the fix): the branch target encoding uses all rows, including test labels (target leakage through encoding); recovery_visits is only known after a missed payment (target leakage); the scaler is fitted on all rows (pre-processing leakage); the random split puts the same borrower on both sides and ignores time (group and temporal leakage); accuracy on imbalanced data with no baseline. Fix: split first by borrower and time; drop recovery_visits; put the scaler and a target encoder inside a Pipeline fitted on training folds only; report precision–recall measures and cost against a majority-class baseline.

Evaluation (mid-term Q5(b), 4 marks). "(i) Monthly sales of 200 shops, to forecast next month. (ii) X-ray images from five hospitals, to be used at a sixth hospital. Choose the split." Answer: (i) A time-ordered split (train on earlier months, test on later), grouped by shop if new shops will be added; random k-fold trains on the future. (ii) Grouped by hospital (leave one hospital out), because the model must work at a new hospital; a random split mixes images from the same hospital and machine. Marking: 1 for each split, 1 for each reason that rules out the others.

Application: tree split by hand (final Q1, 6 marks). "Ten loans. Feature x = missed payments in the last six months; target default (Y/N): x = 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4; target N, N, N, N, N, N, Y, Y, Y, Y. Find the best threshold by Gini." Answer: Parent: 6 N, 4 Y, Gini 1 − (0.36 + 0.16) = 0.48. Thresholds: x ≤ 0.5: left 3 N (0), right 3 N 4 Y (0.490), weighted 0.343. x ≤ 1.5: left 5 N (0), right 1 N 4 Y (0.32), weighted 0.16. x ≤ 2.5: left 6 N 1 Y (0.245), right 3 Y (0), weighted 0.171. x ≤ 3.5: left 6 N 3 Y (0.444), right 1 Y (0), weighted 0.40. Best: x ≤ 1.5, impurity decrease 0.32. Marking: parent 1; four thresholds 1 each; choice 1.

Application: one boosting round (final Q2, 6 marks). "x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6; y = 10, 12, 14, 30, 32, 34. Squared loss, start from the mean, learning rate 0.1, a depth-one tree split at x ≤ 3.5. Give the new predictions and the training MSE before and after." Answer: Mean 22. Residuals −12, −10, −8, 8, 10, 12. Leaf values −10 and +10. New predictions 21 (left) and 23 (right). New residuals −11, −9, −7, 7, 9, 11. MSE before 616/6 ≈ 102.7; after 502/6 ≈ 83.7. Marking: mean and residuals 2; leaf values 1; update 1; MSEs 2.

Application: time series (final Q5, part, 3 of 6 marks). "Daily sales, week 1 (Sat–Fri): 50, 52, 48, 70, 75, 40, 45. Week 2 actual: 54, 50, 52, 72, 80, 42, 47. Compute the MAE of the naive forecast (last value of week 1 for all seven days) and of the seasonal-naive forecast (same day last week)." Answer: Naive errors 9, 5, 7, 27, 35, 3, 2: MAE 88/7 ≈ 12.6. Seasonal-naive errors 4, 2, 4, 2, 5, 2, 2: MAE 21/7 = 3.0. Seasonal-naive is far better because of the weekly pattern. Marking: 1 + 1 + 1 for the comment. The other 3 marks: a backtest design (origins, horizon, baseline, no future data).

Analysis: interpretation (final Q4, 6 marks). A printed SHAP summary shows income_monthly and income_declared (correlation 0.95) both with moderate importance, and a partial-dependence plot of loan_amount that rises and then flattens. Marking notes: 2 marks for reading the plots correctly (higher loan amount raises predicted risk up to a level, then has little effect); 2 marks for the correlated-feature point (the credit is split between two near-copies; dropping one would move its importance to the other; the importance of "income" is the sum); 2 marks for "what it does not show": it does not show cause, does not show what happens to an applicant who changes their income, and may hide interactions that ICE curves would reveal.

Evaluation: design (final Q7, 6 marks). "A clinic screens for a disease that affects about 1 in 100 patients. A missed case is very costly; a false alarm costs a follow-up test. A colleague proposes accuracy and SMOTE before the split. Give your design and defend it." Marking notes: metric (recall at a minimum precision, or expected cost; PR-AUC for comparison) 2; resampling or class weights inside the folds, with stratified (and grouped by patient if repeat visits) folds 2; threshold chosen on validation data from the cost of each error, then checked once on the test set 1; a reasoned rejection of the colleague's proposal 1.
