3. Assumptions to confirm

These assumptions apply to all four course files. The department should confirm or change them.

  1. Teaching weeks. A semester has 14 teaching weeks (framework section 17, item 2).
  2. Theory classes. Three contact hours a week are given as two 90-minute classes, called A and B. That makes 28 classes.
  3. Lab sessions. One 2-hour session a week: 14 sessions. Timings in the plans add up to 120 minutes.
  4. Examinations. The mid-term and the final are held outside the 14 teaching weeks: the mid-term in an examination period after week 7, on weeks 1–7; the final after week 14, on the whole course with most marks from weeks 8–14. No teaching week is given up for them, unless an outline says otherwise.
  5. Split of the written marks. The 80 written marks are split 30 (mid-term) and 50 (final) in these plans. This is a working assumption only. The university regulation decides the split (framework section 17, item 6).
  6. Section sizes. A theory section has up to 40 students. A lab section has about 30 students, one lab instructor, and a teaching assistant in the test, project and viva weeks. A track runs when at least 20 students choose it (framework section 9).
  7. Staff. The theory teacher and the lab instructor may be the same person or two people. Each course file lists the duties of each.
  8. Severity scoring. Where a severity score is needed, the plans use CVSS. The department should fix one CVSS version for the track so that marking is consistent.
  9. Re-splitting theory into three 60-minute classes. The department may timetable three 60-minute classes a week instead of two 90-minute classes. The content of each week stays the same. Use this mapping:
Week's plan with two 90-minute classesWeek's plan with three 60-minute classes
Class A, minutes 0–60First class
Class A, minutes 60–90, then class B, minutes 0–30Second class
Class B, minutes 30–90Third class

Keep each formative check at the end of the 60-minute class in which its topic ends. If a 15-minute quiz would be split by the change, move it to the start of the next 60-minute class. Homework is set at the end of the last class of the week unless the plan says otherwise.

4.3 Quizzes

  • Four quizzes, Level 0, on paper, in the first or last 15 minutes of a named theory class. The course file names the classes.
  • Each quiz has 10 raw marks. The course mark is the average of the best three, which gives a mark out of 10.
  • A quiz may allow the course's one-page reference sheet when it tests use rather than recall. The course file says which.
  • A missed quiz is the one dropped. There is no make-up quiz.
  • For CSE-4885, all four quizzes are on campus in weeks 7–14 (framework section 10).

4.4 Assignments

  • Two assignments per course, each marked out of 20 raw marks with a rubric given in the course file, then scaled to 5.
  • AI-use Level 2, with the T6 log and disclosure statement.
  • Submission is through the course repository or the learning-management system the department uses, by the stated deadline.
  • Late work (proposal): up to three days late loses 20% of the marks gained; later than that is not marked unless the delay is excused with evidence under university rules.

A student who cannot explain their own submitted work in the viva does not pass the lab, whatever the project mark (framework section 7). Section 12 asks the committee how this is recorded.

4.6 Deadlines, late work and missed tests

  • Weekly lab work is due at the end of the session. Work completed by the start of the next session is marked one R1 level lower. Later work gets no marks unless the delay is excused.
  • Practical tests. A student who misses a test for a reason the university accepts sits a variant of the test within two weeks. Whether this is allowed, and what counts as a reason, must be checked against the university's examination rules.
  • Viva. A student absent from the viva with an accepted reason is given a viva before the lab result is submitted.
  • Project. Late project work follows the assignment rule in 4.4.

5. AI-use rules for the track

5.1 Levels in the four courses

The levels below are copied from the outlines' header rows. The course files do not change them.

CourseExaminations and quizzesAssignmentsLab workLab tests and viva
CSE-3675 / 36760220
CSE-4775 / 4776022; in week 13 the AI system is itself the target (Level 3)0
CSE-4785 / 4786022, with no evidence uploaded to any external service0
CSE-4885 / 48860220

In-class formative checks are Level 0. Ungraded homework follows the assignment level (2). Where homework prepares for a Level 0 quiz or test, the plans advise students to do it unaided first.

What the levels mean (framework section 6):

  • Level 0: no AI assistance.
  • Level 2: AI as a pair programmer, with disclosure. Use is logged, output is tested, and the student can defend every line.
  • Level 3: AI is the material. Students build, evaluate or, in CSE-4776 week 13, attack an AI system.

5.2 What Level 2 means in this track

  • An AI tool may explain a packet, a log line, an artefact or an error, draft a rule, a query, a patch or a paragraph, and improve English.
  • The student tests every drafted rule, query or patch against lab data and keeps the test result.
  • The student checks every claim about a field name, event ID, artefact or standard against the tool's documentation or the course reading. AI tools invent field names and artefact meanings and call harmless events malicious with confidence (outline notes for CSE-3675 and CSE-4785).
  • The student records the use in the T6 log and attaches the disclosure statement.
  • Data rule (all four courses). Nothing from an evidence set, a placement host, a client document or a non-public lab data set is pasted into an external AI service, scanning service or website. CSE-4785 and CSE-4885 make this an explicit rule; the track applies it everywhere because real casework works this way. A local model run on the lab machine is not an external service.

5.3 How Level 0 is enforced

  • Quizzes and examinations: on paper, in the room, no devices.
  • Supervised practical tests: on lab machines on the isolated range, with no internet route. Phones and personal devices are switched off and kept in bags at the front. At least two variants of every task are used, by alternate rows. Offline tool documentation (manual pages and built-in help) is allowed. The course file says whether one A4 reference sheet made by the student is allowed. The invigilator walks the room and records machine numbers.
  • Viva: individual, face to face, on the student's own submitted work.
  • Suspected undisclosed AI use in take-home work is handled first by asking the student to explain the work. If the student cannot, the case is handled under the university's academic-integrity rules (to be checked).

R3 Evidence claim standard

Every finding in a lab sheet, findings sheet, ticket or report is written as an evidence claim:

EvidenceWhat it showsConfidence
The exact location: file name and hash or evidence ID, packet number, log line, offset, registry key, record number, or request and response; the UTC time; the tool and its versionOne sentence that says what this evidence shows, and nothing moreconfirmed (the evidence shows it directly), likely (strong but indirect evidence), or assumed (a hypothesis still to test)

Example (from a lab-generated capture): Evidence: capture-wk3.pcap, packets 112–1,140, 09:14:02–09:14:04 UTC, Wireshark. What it shows: range host T2 sent SYN packets to more than 1,000 ports of range target T1 within two seconds and sent no ACK after the replies, which is a SYN scan. Confidence: confirmed.

Times are always in UTC. A report may add local time (UTC+6 for Bangladesh) in brackets.

R4 Supervised practical test marking

  • Each test has 15 marks, divided between tasks in the course file.
  • A correct answer with evidence earns full marks for that task. A correct answer without evidence earns half. A wrong answer earns no marks, but marks for correct method are given where the course file allows partial credit.
  • A required safety or integrity step that is skipped (restoring the snapshot, verifying a hash, checking the target is in scope) loses the marks set for that step. A student who attacks or scans anything outside the stated test target is stopped and handled under T2.
  • Two examiners agree the marking scheme before the test. A second marker checks a sample (section 8.4).

The viva is individual, at Level 0, and about 8–10 minutes long. It is about the student's own submitted work: lab records, the project, and the student's part of a team product. The examiner chooses the items. Two examiners sit together where possible.

R7 Team contribution

For team projects (pairs or teams of three):

  1. Each member writes a contribution statement of half a page: what they did, with links to commits, tickets, rules, report sections or evidence.
  2. Each member rates every other member from 1 to 5 on four points: did their share, quality of work, met deadlines, helped others. Ratings are private to the instructor.
  3. The instructor checks the statements and ratings against the repository or ticket history.
  4. Proposal: when the evidence clearly shows that a member contributed much less than a fair share, the instructor may reduce that member's project mark by up to 20% of the team mark. No member gets more than the team mark. The student is told the reason and may respond before the mark is final. The decision is recorded.

7. Templates

T1 Rules-of-engagement agreement

Based on the "Lab environment and the legal and ethical boundary" section of the track outline and the "Legal and ethical boundary" row of each course. One agreement is signed per course, with that course's schedule attached. No student gets lab access without a signed agreement on file.

Note for the department: the university's legal officer should check the wording below, decide whether the agreement should name the law that applies, and confirm the disciplinary reference.

RULES-OF-ENGAGEMENT AGREEMENT: SEC TRACK LAB

Course code and title: ____________________ Section: ______ Semester: ______ Student name: ____________________ Student ID: ____________

Part 1. Why this agreement exists

Accessing, scanning or interfering with a computer system without its owner's permission is a criminal offence in Bangladesh, as it is in most countries, and good intentions are not a defence. Written authorisation with a defined scope is what separates a security test from an offence. This agreement is that authorisation for this course. It also sets its limits.

Part 2. What I am authorised to do

2.1 I may use the tools and techniques taught in this course only against the permitted targets listed in the schedule for my course (Part 7), and only for the work of this course. 2.2 The department owns the permitted targets and gives this permission. The permission ends at the end of the semester, or earlier if my lab access is withdrawn. 2.3 If my schedule allows it, I may run the department's images on my own laptop under the same rules: host-only network, no bridged or NAT network adapter on any attacker or target machine, and no route to the campus network or the internet.

Part 3. What I must not do

3.1 I will not scan, probe, attack, intercept or monitor the campus network, any university system, any other person's device or account, or any internet host, even "just to check". 3.2 I will not change the network settings of a lab virtual machine so that it can reach anything outside the range. 3.3 I will not copy samples, evidence sets, captures, logs or target images out of the lab or the department file store, except where my schedule allows it. 3.4 I will not use the techniques learned in this course anywhere else, for anyone, without separate written authorisation from the owner of the system. 3.5 I will not capture or read real people's traffic, messages or data. Monitoring exercises use lab-generated data only. 3.6 I will not paste evidence, logs, client documents or placement-host data into any external AI service, scanning service or website. 3.7 I will not share my lab accounts, credentials or test material with anyone.

Part 4. Data handling

4.1 I keep my lab work in my lab record and in the course repository named by the instructor. 4.2 I treat every evidence set, case document and data set as confidential, even when it is fictional. 4.3 I delete working copies when my schedule says so and sign the deletion record if one is required.

Part 5. Reporting a mistake

5.1 If any action of mine may have gone outside the permitted targets (for example, a scan that reached a wrong address, or a machine that was on the wrong network), I will stop at once and tell the instructor. 5.2 I understand that a mistake reported at once is handled as a near miss, and that hiding a mistake is treated as a breach (T2).

Part 6. Consequences of a breach

6.1 A breach of this agreement leads to loss of lab access and referral under the university's disciplinary rules. 6.2 The effect on my course result is set out in the course file and decided under the university's rules.

Part 7. Schedule for my course

The instructor attaches schedule A, B, C or D below and fills in the range details.

Declaration

I have read this agreement and the schedule, the instructor has explained them, and I have passed the short check on them. I agree to follow them.

Student signature: ____________ Date: ________ Instructor name and signature: ____________ Date: ________

(No: it is outside the permitted targets, and verbal permission is not written authorisation for this course.) A student must score at least 8 of 10. T2 Rule-breach procedure

Definitions.

  • A breach is an act against Part 3 of T1, or any deliberate act outside the permitted targets or scope.
  • A near miss is an error reported at once by the student, with no intent, where no action reached anything outside the range (for example a virtual machine started with a NAT adapter and noticed before use).
  • Suspected undisclosed AI use is not handled here; see 5.3.

Steps.

  1. Stop. The instructor stops the activity and disconnects the machine's network adapters. Nothing is deleted and the virtual machine is not reverted.
  2. Record. In the same session the instructor writes down the time (UTC and local), the lab PC and virtual machine, the students involved and what was seen. Relevant range logs and captures are saved and hashed.
  3. Check reach. The instructor and the range custodian check whether any traffic or action reached a system outside the range: the adapter settings, the capture, the sensor and SIEM logs.
  4. Inform. If anything outside the range may have been touched, the course teacher informs the head of department the same day, and the university IT office so that it can check its own systems.
  5. Access. For a breach, lab access is suspended at once (the CSE-3675 outline: "A breach of the rules of engagement means immediate loss of lab access"). For a near miss, the set-up is fixed, the event goes in the near-miss log, and the student continues.
  6. Statement. The student writes a statement within two working days.
  7. Decision. The head of department, or the body the university names, decides and refers the case under the university's disciplinary rules. The effect on the lab result needs a committee decision (section 12).
  8. Learn. The range custodian reviews the cause (for example an image shipped with a NAT adapter) and fixes it for everyone. Near misses are reviewed at the end of each semester.

Records. The incident form, saved logs with hashes, the student's statement and the decision are kept in a confidential part of the course file.

8. CLO attainment and records for outcome-based education

8.1 Tagging

  • Every assessed item is tagged with exactly one CLO: each examination question or sub-question, each quiz question, each rubric criterion of an assignment or project, each practical-test task, each viva criterion, and each weekly lab session.
  • If an item serves two CLOs, it is split into two parts with separate marks.
  • Each CLO's PO mapping comes from the outline. PO attainment is derived from CLO attainment.

8.2 Calculation

For each student and each CLO:

CLO score (%) = marks obtained on items tagged with that CLO ÷ maximum marks of those items × 100

  • For attainment, every quiz the student took counts, not only the best three that count for the grade.
  • An item missed with an accepted excuse is left out of both the top and the bottom of the fraction. An item missed without an excuse counts as zero.
  • Proposal: a student attains a CLO when the CLO score is at least 50%.
  • Proposal: the course attains a CLO when at least 60% of the students who sat the final assessment attain it.
  • Attainment is calculated separately for the theory code and the lab code. The outline gives one CLO set for both; a CLO that one component does not assess is reported as "not assessed in this component".
  • PO contribution: for each PO, the course reports the attainment of the CLOs mapped to it. Aggregation across courses is the department's outcome-based education (OBE) committee's work.

The 50% and 60% figures are proposals. The OBE committee should set them for the whole programme.

8.3 Records

One spreadsheet per section and component, with:

  • one row per student and one column per assessed item;
  • under each item heading: its CLO, its Bloom band and its maximum marks;
  • a summary sheet with each student's CLO scores, the attained or not-attained result, and the course-level percentage per CLO.

Each course file (the folder the department keeps for each offering) holds:

  1. the outline and this lesson plan, with any changes made during the semester;
  2. question papers, quizzes and practical tests, each with its Bloom and CLO table, model answers and marking scheme;
  3. three marked samples of every assessment (one high, one middle, one low), with the student's consent or names removed as the university requires;
  4. a sample of lab records and AI-use logs;
  5. the register of signed rules-of-engagement agreements and the short-check results;
  6. viva forms;
  7. the CLO attainment spreadsheet and a one-page attainment report;
  8. the near-miss log and any T2 incident records (confidential);
  9. the end-of-course review: what changed for the next offering and why.

8.4 Moderation and closing the loop

  • Proposal: a second marker checks a sample of each written examination and of the project reports: at least five scripts or 10%, whichever is more. If the two marks differ by more than 10% of the maximum on a script, the two markers agree a mark and check the rest of the batch for the same problem.
  • Practical tests are marked against the scheme agreed before the test (R4).
  • At the end of each offering, the teacher writes a one-page attainment report: for each CLO, the percentage of students who attained it, the likely reason for any CLO below target, and one concrete change for the next offering (for example, an extra worked example, a change to a lab sheet, a new practice test).

9. Track-wide support for weaker students

The intake is about 15% strong, 60% mid-level and 25% below average (framework section 1). The track outline says SOC, GRC and audit work "rewards method, patience with detail and clear writing more than fast programming", so these roles are within reach of a mid-level student who does the labs properly. The four course files build on the measures below.

  1. Method before speed. Every lab sheet gives a fixed method: the question, where to look, what to record (R3), how to check. Mid-level students succeed by following a method carefully. Marks reward evidence and explanation, not the number of flags or alerts.
  2. Floor tasks and stretch tasks. Each lab sheet marks the floor tasks every student must finish (tied to the minimum competency) and optional stretch tasks. The R1 level depends on the floor tasks.
  3. Worked examples and templates. One worked example of every document type before students write their own: a findings sheet, a ticket, a finding, a timeline, a risk register, a policy, an audit finding. Templates T3–T7 remove the blank-page problem.
  4. One-page reference sheets. A protocol sheet (CSE-3675), an artefact sheet per family (CSE-4785), a glossary in plain English (CSE-4885). Quizzes that test use rather than recall may allow them.
  5. Pairs in weekly labs, individual records. Pairs share a machine and talk through the work. Each student keeps an individual record and sits the tests and the viva alone.
  6. Practice before every test. A practice variant of each supervised test is released one week before it and worked through in a clinic.
  7. Early warning. After quiz 1 and after practical test 1, students below 40% are invited to a clinic. The teacher records who was invited and who came.
  8. Clinics. One hour a week of open lab or office time, run by the lab instructor or a teaching assistant, more often in the two weeks before each test.
  9. Language. Discussion in class and in the lab may be in Bangla. Written output is in English, in short, plain sentences. CSE-4885 allows a first draft in Bangla before the English version. R2 marks clarity, not grammar.
  10. Peer mentors. Strong students who have finished a course may act as lab mentors the next year, where the department can recognise the work.
  11. Certificates as extra evidence. A mid-level student with a modest CGPA can show an employer a certificate. CSE-3675 is aligned with CompTIA Security+ (optional); framework section 13 also lists ISC2 CC for CSE-3651 and CSE-3675. Sitting either is the student's own choice and cost.
  12. Stretch goals for the strongest 15%. Each outline lists stretch goals. They earn no extra marks but are good capstone and portfolio material.

10. The track range: ownership and preparation

10.1 What the range is

All four labs use one shared environment (track outline): department-supplied virtual machines in VirtualBox on a host-only network with no route to the campus network or the internet, on each lab PC; heavier shared services (the SIEM server and the evidence file store) on one department server or a few repurposed PCs (framework section 16); targets restored from snapshots after each session.

A host-only network exists only inside one PC, so it cannot reach a shared server on another machine. For the shared Wazuh server and the evidence store, the lab PCs need a separate, isolated lab network segment (a separate switch or VLAN) that connects them to the server and has no route to the campus network or the internet. The range guide must describe this segment. Section 12 lists it as a decision.

10.2 Range custodian

The department names one range custodian: the CSE-3676 lab instructor or a technical officer. The custodian:

  • keeps the master images with version numbers and SHA-256 hashes, and a change log;
  • checks isolation at the start of each semester and after any image change: every attacker and target image has only host-only adapters, and from inside the range neither the campus gateway nor an internet address can be reached;
  • keeps the Wazuh server for each section and the evidence file store running, backed up and access-controlled;
  • keeps time synchronised on all range machines from one time source inside the range (the CSE-3675 outline says to do this before week 9);
  • keeps the register of signed agreements, the near-miss log and the lab-PC inventory;
  • restores or checks snapshots after each session, with the lab instructor of that session.

12. Issues for the committee that affect the whole track

Course-specific issues are in section 9 of each course file.

  1. Week-14 lab time is too short. Individual vivas of about 8 minutes for 30 students need about four examiner-hours on their own. With the demonstrations, readouts or panel presentations, the course files estimate four to six examiner-hours. One 2-hour session with one instructor is not enough in any of the four courses. A decision is needed: a second examiner (the theory teacher), an extra slot in week 14, or vivas in the examination period.
  2. The range needs an isolated lab network. The outline puts each PC's virtual machines on a host-only network, and the Wazuh server and evidence store on a separate department machine. A host-only network cannot reach another machine. CSE-3676 (from lab 9 and the project), CSE-4786 (the evidence store) and CSE-4886 (Wazuh alerts) need an isolated lab segment, such as a separate switch or VLAN, with no route to the campus network or the internet. The department should build it before the first offering of CSE-3676 and have the university IT office confirm the isolation.
  3. The viva gate and the access gate. Framework section 7 says a student who cannot explain their own work does not pass the lab, whatever the project mark. The plans also make the rules-of-engagement check a gate for lab access. The university's grading regulation must allow a result that overrides the numeric total. The committee should decide what is recorded (fail, incomplete or a second viva), whether a second viva is offered, and how many attempts at the access check are allowed (the plans propose three).
  4. Effect of a rules-of-engagement breach on the result. A breach means immediate loss of lab access. A student without access cannot complete the remaining assessed lab work. The committee should decide the effect on the lab result, approve the near-miss rule in T2, and ask the legal officer to review the wording of T1.
  5. Split and timing of the written examinations. The plans assume 30 + 50. For CSE-4885 the mid-term must fall after the six-week Industry Practice block (framework section 10). The examination office must confirm both.
  6. Lab PCs and room access. Several labs run three to five virtual machines at once; the outline says 8 GB runs an attacker and one target and 16 GB is better. CSE-4785 evidence may not leave the lab, so students need supervised lab-room time outside timetabled hours for project work in weeks 10–13.
  7. Preparation load. The unseen CSE-4776 targets, the CSE-4786 evidence sets and case variants, the CSE-4886 cases and infrastructure-as-code projects, and the practical-test variants in all four labs take days or weeks to build and must be refreshed each year. No one is yet named to do this or given workload credit for it.
  8. Overlap with the semester-6 core course. CSE-3652 repeats parts of the track labs for SEC students: its week-11 lab (scanning, sniffing, ARP spoofing, firewall rules) overlaps CSE-3676 labs 3–6, and its weeks 8–9 use the same public web targets and flaw classes as CSE-4776 labs 2–4. SEC students also sign two rules-of-engagement agreements in semester-6 week 1. The department may want SEC students to do extension tasks in CSE-3652 and to use one combined agreement.
  9. Shared standards. The department should fix one CVSS version for the track (CSE-3675 and CSE-4775 both use CVSS). The CSE-4785 outline teaches the incident-response lifecycle "after NIST SP 800-61"; the four-phase model is from Revision 2, and the CSE-4785 file asks the committee to confirm which revision is the reading.
  10. Attainment thresholds. The 50% (student) and 60% (course) thresholds in section 8 are proposals for the OBE committee.
