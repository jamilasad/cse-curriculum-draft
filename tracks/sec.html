Entry-level openings are mostly in operations, compliance and audit. Penetration-testing and application-security posts are fewer and expect stronger programming.

Who it suits

Mid-level to strong students. SOC, GRC and audit work rewards method, patience with detail and clear writing more than fast programming, so these roles are within reach of a mid-level student who does the labs properly. Application security and penetration testing need a student who already reads and writes code comfortably in more than one language. The track does not suit a student who dislikes reading logs and documents or writing reports: every course ends in a written report, and SOC work is often shift work. Employers in this field often screen on certificates. Within the track only Course I is aligned with one (CompTIA Security+), and sitting it is optional.

What students already know

  • Programming in Python, C and Java (semesters 1–3), and the Linux shell and Git, checked unaided in the Core Competency Assessment.
  • Database Systems and SQL (semester 4).
  • A team web application built in the Software Engineering Studio (semester 4), which each team attacked and fixed at an introductory level.
  • Operating Systems and Computer Networks with labs (semester 5).
  • In semester 6, alongside Track Course I: the core Information Security course with its lab (CSE-3651, CSE-3652), Distributed Systems & Cloud Computing with the Cloud & DevOps Lab (CSE-3653, CSE-3654), and the AI Application & Agent Engineering Studio (CSE-3660).

The track assumes CSE-3651 teaches cryptography, authentication and access-control models, the common vulnerability classes at overview level and basic threat modelling. The track courses use that material and do not teach it again.

Lab environment and the legal and ethical boundary

All four labs use one shared environment, called the track range in the outlines below. Each lab PC runs department-supplied virtual machines in VirtualBox on a host-only network with no route to the campus network or the internet. Heavier shared services (the SIEM server, the evidence file store) run on one department server or a few repurposed PCs. Targets are restored from snapshots after each session. Students may run the same images on their own laptops under the same rules.

Accessing, scanning or interfering with a computer system without its owner's permission is a criminal offence in Bangladesh, as it is in most countries, and good intentions are not a defence. Written authorisation with a defined scope is what separates a security test from an offence. Each course therefore:

  • states this boundary in its first week;
  • requires a signed rules-of-engagement agreement before lab access, listing the permitted targets (the range's addresses and supplied images), the prohibited actions (any scan or attack on the campus network, other people's devices or any internet host; removing samples or evidence sets; using lab techniques elsewhere), the data-handling rules, and the consequence of a breach (loss of lab access and referral under the university's disciplinary rules);
  • runs offensive exercises only against systems the department owns.

For this reason the header table of each package below has one row that the general template does not have, "Legal and ethical boundary".

Suggested capstone direction

Capstone teams work with a real client under written authorisation and a scope approved by the supervisor. Directions that fit the track:

  • A monitoring service for a small organisation (a university department, a local firm, an NGO): open-source SIEM, log sources, detection rules tuned to that organisation, and a triage playbook.
  • A security assessment and remediation of a real application whose owner has agreed in writing, delivered as a report, fixes and a pipeline that keeps the fixes in place.
  • A compliance-readiness toolkit for a small software exporter: gap assessment against a chosen control set, policies, and scripts that collect audit evidence automatically.
  • An awareness or fraud-detection tool for a local problem, such as phishing and social-engineering messages in Bangla aimed at MFS users.

Substitution

CSE-3675 is Track Course I, the foundation of the track, and cannot be replaced (framework section 9). One of the semester-7 or semester-8 courses may be swapped, with the adviser's approval, for a course of another track offered in the same semester.

The natural swap is Cloud Infrastructure & Virtualization (CSE-4787 + CSE-4788, INF track, semester 7) for a student heading for cloud security. It replaces CSE-4775 or CSE-4785; replacing CSE-4785 is usually the smaller loss for that student. Linux System Administration & Automation (CSE-3677, INF) runs in semester 6 against CSE-3675 and so is not available as a swap; SEC students who want deeper Linux administration can take it as an extra course or cover the RHCSA or LFCS syllabus on their own.

In the other direction, CSE-4775 is a listed substitution for SWE students and is written so that it does not depend on CSE-3675.
