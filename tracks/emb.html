An honest note on the market

The domestic hardware industry is small: a handful of chip design-services firms, some consumer-electronics manufacturers, and research centres of foreign companies. They hire in small numbers and not always every year. The track should therefore stay small, about 20–30 students per intake (one lab section of 24, or two small ones), and it should not be promoted as a sure route to a hardware job.

Three things follow, and advisers should say them plainly when students choose a track.

  • Verification is the most open door. It is done entirely in simulation, so it can be done from Bangladesh for foreign chip companies; it is a large part of what design-services firms do, and a chip project needs at least as many people verifying as designing. CSE-4889 is in the track for that reason. The department should confirm current hiring with the local firms before each intake.
  • Firmware and IoT jobs exist at manufacturers, at start-ups and in remote work for foreign product companies, but remote firmware work usually goes to people with experience, because it needs hardware on the desk and trust.
  • Many EMB graduates will take a general software job first. The core gives them what that needs (two studios, databases, networks, cloud, security). The habits of this track transfer well: reading datasheets, measuring instead of guessing, and finding faults that cannot be seen on a screen.

Who it suits

  • Students who liked the Digital Logic Design and Microcontrollers labs and stayed with a fault until they found it.
  • Students who are comfortable in C. Nothing in the track avoids programming; it is programming with hardware added.
  • Careful, methodical mid-level students, who do well in verification and firmware testing, where thoroughness counts for more than speed. Design roles (RTL, architecture) are harder to enter and go mostly to the strongest.
  • Strong mathematics is not needed. Reading datasheets and tool reports in English is needed, and each course gives support for it.

It does not suit a student who chooses it to avoid software work, or who dislikes long debugging sessions.

How the four courses build on each other

Two lines run through the track and meet at the boundary between hardware and software.

  • Hardware line. Digital Logic Design (S3) → CSE-3679 (S6), where hardware is described in an HDL and run on an FPGA → CSE-4889 (S8), where such designs are verified the way industry does it. The UART, FIFO and SPI blocks that students write in CSE-3679 come back in CSE-4889 as the designs under test.
  • Firmware line. Microcontrollers & Embedded Systems (S5) → CSE-4779 (S7), which adds a real-time kernel, drivers, power and reliability → CSE-4789 (S7), which connects the device to a network, a back end and a small machine-learning model.
  • Same-semester pair. CSE-4779 and CSE-4789 run together in semester 7. CSE-4789 uses only core knowledge until week 5 and real-time tasks after that, by which time CSE-4779 has taught them. AID students who take CSE-4789 as a substitution receive the task structure as a supplied skeleton.
  • Where the lines meet. Memory-mapped registers: in CSE-3679 the student builds a peripheral that has them, in CSE-4779 writes a driver for one, and in CSE-4889 verifies one.

By the framework's rule, CSE-2440 Core Competency Assessment must be passed before registering for the semester-7 track courses, CSE-4779 and CSE-4789.

Suggested capstone direction

Two kinds of project fit the track.

  1. A connected product for a real user: sensor nodes, a gateway, a back end and a dashboard, with inference on the device, over-the-air update and a measured power budget. Local problems that fit: machine monitoring on a garment-factory line, pond water quality for aquaculture, cold-room and vaccine-fridge logging, energy metering for a building, water-level and flood sensing, monitoring of solar home systems. A team of three or four should cover all layers between them: firmware, connectivity and back end, data and model, hardware and testing.
  2. An FPGA design with serious verification: a small RISC-V system with a custom peripheral or accelerator, or verification components for an open-source core, with a coverage-driven testbench and a regression. CSE-4889 comes only in semester 8, so Capstone I uses the testbench skills of CSE-3679 and Capstone II adds coverage and assertions; the supervisor should plan for that.

Do not accept "microcontroller, sensor and display" with no user, no measurement and no handling of failure. Each proposal states a requirement with numbers (battery life, latency, accuracy, clock frequency, coverage) and the final report measures against it.

AI use. All four labs run at Level 2: AI as pair programmer, with use logged, output tested and every line defended. Quizzes, written examinations, supervised practical tests and vivas are at Level 0. In each lab the first weeks are done unaided, so that the student has a mental model before an assistant writes anything.

Where assistants help in this field: explaining tool messages, timing reports and fault registers; writing testbench stimulus, build scripts and boilerplate (UVM classes, MQTT clients, dashboards); summarising a datasheet section that the student then checks against the datasheet.

Where their output is especially dangerous:

  • HDL that simulates but does not synthesise, or synthesises into different hardware. Delays and initial blocks in design code, incomplete assignments that create latches, blocking assignments in clocked blocks, a signal driven from two places. The simulation passes; the board does not work.
  • Timing and clock-domain crossings. An assistant cannot see the timing report. It suggests constraints with invented syntax, or a timing exception that hides a real violation. A missing synchroniser fails rarely and at random on hardware, and never in simulation.
  • Concurrency in firmware. Variables shared between an interrupt and a task with no protection, blocking calls inside interrupt handlers, guessed stack sizes and priorities. The code runs on the bench and fails under load or after some hours.
  • Invented hardware facts. Register addresses, bit positions, pin numbers and library functions that do not exist for this chip. A wrong register write can lock a board.
  • Security shortcuts in connected devices. Certificate checking switched off to make the connection work; keys in source code.
  • Tests that cannot fail. A reference model derived from the design it is meant to check; assertions that pass vacuously.

The rule in every lab: generated work is accepted only with the evidence that fits the danger. For HDL that is a clean lint and synthesis report and met timing; for firmware, a table of every shared resource and its protection, and the datasheet page for every register; for a test, a run in which it fails on a deliberately broken design.

EMB is the only track that cannot run without new hardware. The other tracks use the existing computer labs; INF and SEC can use a virtual lab in place of a rack of switches and routers (framework, section 16). Within EMB the track-specific purchase is the FPGA boards and add-ons, the Wi-Fi boards and the LoRa modules. The debug-capable microcontroller boards, logic analysers and sensor kits are the same ones the core lab CSE-3548 needs; they should be bought once and timetabled for both. CSE-4890 needs no hardware at all. The equipment serves one section at a time, so a second section of 24 needs a second timetable slot, not a second set.

Board choices are left to the department. The criteria that matter: a free tool chain that runs on the lab PCs, a working debugger, local availability of replacements, and good documentation in English.

Substitutions

  • Into the track. CSE-4789 IoT Systems & Edge AI is the natural substitution for AID students (framework, section 9). It needs only core prerequisites, and its edge-ML weeks teach deployment under tight memory, which the AID courses do not.
  • Out of the track. The most sensible single substitution is CSE-4781/4782 Software Testing & Quality Engineering (SWE, semester 7) in place of CSE-4789, for a student aiming at verification or wanting a stronger software fallback: test design, coverage thinking and continuous integration carry directly into verification work. A student aiming only at connected products may instead replace CSE-4889 (S8) with CSE-4881/4882 Cloud-Native Engineering & Site Reliability, if the SWE track teacher accepts the student's background.
  • Not advised. Replacing CSE-3679 (it is the prerequisite of CSE-4889) or CSE-4779 (it is the centre of the firmware line).

Substitution checker

Overlap. Track Course I (semester 6) is each track's foundation and cannot be replaced. A student may replace one of Track Courses II–IV with a course from another track offered in the same semester, with the adviser's approval. A course that is a prerequisite of the student's own semester-8 track course cannot be swapped; in practice an AID student may swap only CSE-4783 and an INF student only CSE-4777. Natural substitutions, all in semester 7: Data Engineering & Analytics or Web & Application Security for SWE students; Cloud Infrastructure & Virtualization for SWE or SEC students; IoT Systems & Edge AI for AID students.
